Drivers and vehicles

Speed and other recorded factors

Outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, injury crashes where police recorded inappropriate speed had 3.41 times as many deaths per crash as other crashes in 2016–2023. By road type it ran from 1.22 times on dual carriageways to 5.90 times on urban streets; their weighted summary is 2.00 times (95% interval 1.69–2.36). These are associations in police records, not estimates of what speed causes. Recorded speed was not in steady decline: interurban roads were flat to 2020 and fell in 2021–2022, and a change in recording cannot be excluded.

More deaths per crash on every road type

In 2023, police recorded inappropriate speed in 6.9% of injury crashes outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, and those crashes accounted for 21.8% of the deaths. They are concentrated on interurban roads other than motorways and dual carriageways, where any crash is more often fatal and where most of their deaths occur, so comparing them with other crashes on the same kind of road and in the same year lowers the ratio.

The ratio was largest on urban streets, where an ordinary injury crash rarely kills (0.73 deaths per 100 crashes, against 4.32 with speed recorded), and smallest on dual carriageways. The road-type ratios differ by more than chance, so the all-roads summary of 2.00 describes no single kind of road: it is weighted towards other interurban roads, and its interval is widened to allow for how much the ratios differ. How the ratios are estimated is set out in the technical notes.

A recorded factor is a police judgement about a crash

Each share below is the proportion of injury crashes in which officers recorded the factor: a judgement made after the crash, usually once its outcome is known, not a finding that the factor caused it. A crash can carry several factors, so the shares overlap. They describe the police record of crashes, not how often drivers drink, speed or are distracted on the road.

No file records how fast anyone was travelling, so the ratio above shows that crashes with speed recorded were deadlier than other crashes on the same kind of road, not that speed caused those crashes or their deaths.

Recording could bias the ratio either way. Police may look harder for speed when someone has died, which would raise it, while speed that played a part but went unrecorded leaves those crashes among the others, which would lower it. The data cannot measure either bias.

Recorded alcohol rose; recorded speed fell late

Recorded inappropriate speed on interurban roads stayed between 17.5% and 19.5% of crashes until 2020, then fell 22% to 2022. On urban streets it fell from 4.1% to 3.1%. On all roads it fell from 10.0% to 6.9%, more than on either kind of road, because crashes shifted to urban streets, where speed is rarely recorded. A fall in recorded speed can reflect less speeding, a change in how officers record it, or both; these data cannot separate them.

Recorded alcohol on interurban roads rose from 5.5% of crashes in 2014 to 8.2% in 2023. The series has no break, but its share rose 11% in 2016, the year other series broke, and a further 30% from 2016 to 2023. On urban streets recorded alcohol rose within the run that starts in 2016, from 2.5% to 3.2%. More recorded alcohol can reflect more drinking drivers, more breath tests after crashes, or both.

Distraction or inattention on interurban roads has no break and stayed between 24.8% and 28.8% of crashes. Illegal manoeuvres, which the report defines as failing to give way, too short a following distance, illegal overtaking, an improper turn, or negligent or reckless driving, rose on interurban roads from 25.4% to 37.8%, a level they had already reached by 2017.

Where recording is not comparable

A share can move because something changed on the road, because the way crashes are recorded changed, or both. A fixed rule therefore marks an abrupt rise or fall between two years, or too few crashes to test, as a break in comparability, and each series is compared only within the runs of years between its breaks (technical notes). The rule finds discontinuities; it cannot say whether a break, or a trend within a run, comes from behaviour, from recording or from both.

Recorded distraction on urban streets rose 68% in 2016 and fell 58% in 2019 while the interurban series moved smoothly, so it is compared only within 2016–2018 and 2019–2023. Recorded alcohol on urban streets rose 57% in 2016 too; the reason for the shared timing is unknown.

The driver tables cannot show a trend in speeding

DGT's yearbook tables record for each driver in an injury crash whether the police noted a speed infraction, but from 2016 about half of drivers have no speed status (19% in 2015, 52% in 2016), so no trend in speeding can be read from them. The speed report's crash series, used above, has no break of this kind.
