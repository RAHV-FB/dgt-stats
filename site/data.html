Definitions

Injury crash
A crash on a public road in which at least one person is killed or injured. DGT's national records and series count only injury crashes, and a slight injury there needs medical care. Barcelona's crash table also holds crashes the Guàrdia Urbana attended in which nobody was hurt (890 in its year), and it counts people who refused medical care as slightly injured.
Death
A death within 30 days of the crash, DGT's consolidated definition, used for the national series and rates. The Catalan file counts deaths within 24 hours; Barcelona's records keep deaths within 24 hours apart from later deaths, and the analysis of people counts both. In DGT's national records for 2016–2024, 14.2% of the deaths within 30 days occurred after the first 24 hours, so the two definitions are never combined.
Serious injury
Admission to hospital for more than 24 hours; any other injury is slight. The Catalan file and Barcelona's crash table classify at 24 hours, so a person who died later counts there as seriously injured. The Catalan file holds crashes with a death or serious injury (serious and fatal crashes); those in which nobody died within 24 hours are non-fatal.
Fatal share
Among the serious and fatal crashes in Catalonia, the share in which someone died within 24 hours. For Barcelona the site gives a serious-or-fatal share instead: of the people whose outcome was recorded, or of the crashes the police attended, the share with a serious or fatal injury.
Zone
DGT's grouping of roads into interurban roads, and urban streets and crossings. The Catalan file also distinguishes through-town roads, which count as urban in comparisons with DGT's records.
Driver involved
The driver of a vehicle in an injury crash, injured or not.
Licence holder
A holder of any driving permit in DGT's census; the B permit is the car licence.
Crash frequency
How often a group appears in injury crashes relative to a measure of its exposure, such as kilometres driven, fuel sold or licence holders.
Severity
How serious the outcome is once a crash, or a person in one, is already in the records: deaths per injury crash, deaths per driver involved, the fatal share of serious crashes. A death rate per unit of exposure is crash frequency multiplied by severity.
Recorded factor
A circumstance the police record about a crash, such as alcohol, inappropriate speed or distraction (Barcelona's records call them causes). It is a police judgement, not a finding by this study that the circumstance caused the crash.
Association
A statistical relationship in observed records. Every comparison on the site between an outcome and a circumstance is an association; none is an estimate of a causal effect, which would need a design that these records do not provide.
Predictive model
A model judged only on records not used to fit it, and kept only if it ranks those records better than a simple table of the same data.
Sampling interval
A 95% interval that allows only for chance: which people a survey happened to sample, and chance in the counts of crashes. It takes every other choice of the analysis as given, so the real uncertainty can be much wider.
Sensitivity range
The span of an estimate across the alternative assumptions tested. It carries no probability, and it is not a limit: assumptions not tested, or tested ones combined differently, could fall outside it.
Conditional estimate
An estimate that holds only if a stated assumption holds, and is always printed with it; its sampling interval holds the assumption fixed.

Rates, denominators and intervals

Each rate pairs a count with a denominator meant to contain it:

Per resident
The burden of road deaths on a population, most of whom were not travelling at the time. A rate per resident falls when people travel less.
Per licence holder
Counts only drivers: on the drivers page, car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not.
Per registered vehicle
Counts only the occupants of motorcycles, cars, vans, trucks and buses.
Per tonne of road fuel
Tonnes of petrol and diesel sold: the only annual measure of traffic that covers every road.
Per vehicle-kilometre
The Ministerio de Transportes' measurements on State, regional and provincial interurban roads, and DGT's estimates by vehicle type from inspection odometer readings. Kilometres by driver age are estimated from the EMEF working-day survey's age profile, applied to Spain's population and scaled to DGT's car kilometres.
Per driver involved
Deaths among the drivers involved in injury crashes: how often involvement ends in death. It needs no measure of travel.

Pedestrians and cyclists hold no licence for the trip in which they are hurt and travel in no registered vehicle, so they are counted against residents and road fuel only. Road fuel stands in for the kilometres driven on all roads, and the kilometres by driver age carry one region's survey to Spain; neither can be tested for Spain as a whole. Some rates do not fully meet the rule, and each page says so: rates per resident count visitors' deaths, rates per licence holder (by sex) count unlicensed and foreign drivers, rates per registered vehicle and by vehicle type count foreign vehicles, and interurban deaths are compared with national road fuel only as a check.

How often crashes happen and how deadly they are

A death rate measured against traffic combines two quantities that can move separately: deaths per kilometre (or per tonne of fuel) equal injury crashes per kilometre multiplied by deaths per injury crash. The split depends on how completely slight-injury crashes are recorded: if fewer are recorded, crashes per kilometre fall and deaths per crash rise by the same factor, and their product does not move. Deaths are counted completely, so the death rate is the firmer measure and its split the more fragile reading. The split is applied on Long-run trends, Drivers and Vehicles.

Intervals and ordinary year-to-year variation

Rates carry 95% intervals that treat counts as Poisson with a known denominator; ratios of two rates carry log-normal intervals. Spain's annual counts scatter around their trend more than Poisson chance allows, so changes against 2019 are read against intervals widened to span an ordinary year's variation (Long-run trends).

Reading police crash records

Crash records contain only the crashes the police recorded, and the Catalan file only those with a death or serious injury. A share computed from such records, such as the fatal share on interurban roads, measures how severe crashes were once they had happened and been recorded. It does not measure how often crashes happen or how dangerous a road is per kilometre travelled, because the records contain no measure of travel. The severity models share this limit: they predict a severe outcome only among crashes that were recorded.

The recorded factors on the site are DGT's, such as speed, alcohol or distraction, the Catalan file's “influence” fields, and Barcelona's contributing factors and driver causes. A crash can carry several, and how completely they are recorded varies by year and with the severity of the crash.

Missing values keep their own categories. “Not specified”, “not applicable”, a field's own “unknown” code and an empty cell are four different states, and none is read as zero or as “no”, except in the fog and strong-wind fields. DGT fills those in only when there was fog or strong wind, apart from a few province-years that code them another way (coding breaks). In Barcelona's crash table a blank count also means zero: no cell holds an explicit zero, and with blanks read as zero the victims add up in every crash.

The audit of DGT's records counts a field as unrecorded when it is “not specified”, “unknown” or empty in a crash it applies to: the junction type and the right-of-way flags, for instance, do not apply to a crash away from a junction. Of the 18 fields it examines (the 13 right-of-way flags counted as one), three are unrecorded in more than 30% of the crashes they apply to, one of them only just (30.2%). How unevenly the provinces record them is set out under Data sources and scope.

The figure leaves “not applicable” out of each field's share, so a field that applies only to some crashes is judged on the crashes it applies to. The pavement field is “not applicable” in 87% of crashes, and the island field is empty, which DGT's dictionary defines as not applicable, in 94%. In the figure an empty fog or strong-wind field counts as recorded. The junction type, the right-of-way flags and the crossing road apply only at a junction, which this table cannot identify, so they are not drawn.

Recording can also depend on the outcome. Fatal crashes may be investigated more fully, so fields left unspecified in non-fatal crashes are more often filled in for fatal ones. In the Catalan file, nine placeholder levels in eight fields (“not specified” or an unexplained blank) are recorded at least 1.5 times as often in non-fatal crashes as in fatal ones; these fields are left out of the severity model. The road owner is the reverse case, blank far more often on fatal records, and the model leaves out the crashes it affects (model note).

Coding breaks in DGT's records

Four changes in DGT's coding affect series by road type and junction, and all four come from the records for the four Catalan provinces.

  • Until 2020 those records code almost every crash on a conventional road as a conventional road with a dual carriageway (3,727 such crashes in 2020, 7 on single carriageways); from 2021 they use the single-carriageway code instead. Elsewhere the dual-carriageway code holds between 1,257 and 1,769 crashes a year.
  • In 2024 many crashes on urban streets begin to be coded in the “other” road group (codes 7, 8 and 10–14, service roads, slip roads, cycle paths and other minor roads): 83% of that year's crashes in the group are on urban streets, against 43% in 2016–2023. Most of them carry code 14, “other” itself, and 15,531 of the 17,519 crashes with that code in 2024 are Catalan.
  • From 2023 the same records code the junction flag the wrong way round. The share of their crashes coded at a junction goes from between 38% and 42% a year in 2016–2022 (35% in 2021, under the narrower definition below) to 62% in 2023 and 63% in 2024. Elsewhere it stays between 37% and 40%. From 2023, their crashes coded at a junction carry no junction type (“not specified”) in every case, and 94% of those coded away from a junction carry one, a field the rest of Spain leaves empty away from a junction. The Servei Català de Trànsit's file holds the same crashes with a death or serious injury: in 2023 DGT codes 69.5% of the Catalan ones at a junction, and the Catalan file places 69.6% of them between junctions.
  • In 2021 the records use a narrower definition of a junction: DGT's count of these Catalan crashes at a junction matches the Catalan file's crashes within a junction, where in 2016–2020 and 2022 it matches those within or within 50 metres of one. That year 712 Catalan crashes coded away from a junction carry a junction type or a right-of-way flag, against 10 in all the other years before 2023; counted at a junction, they bring that year's share to 38%.

Road-type series are therefore read year by year and alongside zone, the two kinds of conventional road form one group, and no road-type trend is drawn. Comparisons of Catalonia with the rest of Spain group every conventional road together for the same reason.

Junction shares as published are not compared across 2023. The association of junctions with fatal outcomes reads the flag the other way round in those province-years, which puts the Catalan share at a junction at 38% in 2023 and 37% in 2024, against 38% elsewhere in Spain in the same years (Crash circumstances in Spain).

The fog and strong-wind fields are filled in only when there was fog or strong wind, so a blank in them is read as none, except where a province's records give the condition to more than 10% of their crashes. Elsewhere fog or strong wind is recorded in at most 7.6% of a province-year's crashes.

From 2017 the records for the province of Barcelona code fog in 33% to 45% of crashes a year, against 0.0% in 2016. The Servei Català de Trànsit's file records fog in the same number of the province's crashes with a death or serious injury in each year of 2017–2023. Strong wind is recorded in every crash of Barcelona, Girona, Lleida and Tarragona in 2021 and in 19% to 22% of Sevilla's crashes in 2017–2018. In those province-years the field is coded another way: neither a value nor a blank says whether there was fog or strong wind. Neither field enters the national association analysis or the severity model.

Checks on the data

Before any analysis, reconciliation checks tie DGT's crash records, yearbook tables and driver census to DGT's published totals. Every check passes. On the crash records they confirm that every code is in DGT's dictionary and compare crashes, victims and deaths by year, province, month and vehicle type with the yearbook and DGT's statistical tables, and the crashes and deaths of the speed report's provinces with that report. The other checks compare the driver census and the yearbook's driver tables with DGT's published totals. The kilometre table by owner's age reconciles with the same release's published fleet to within 0.5%, the margin left by owners DGT could not classify. The full list of checks is published as a table.

Reproducing the results

Every result is computed by the code in the repository from the raw published files, which are kept with their checksums and, where recorded, their download addresses. The code also holds a few values typed from cited documents: definitions such as the public-holiday calendars that separate working days, figures read from archived files, and the road-to-straight-line ratio and benchmarks of the EMEF's 2021 distance report. Run in this order, from the repository root:

  1. python scripts/ingest.py all reads data/raw into data/staging and runs the reconciliation checks.
  2. python scripts/build_tables.py builds the processed table of DGT crash records.
  3. python scripts/model.py fits the associations in DGT's crash records.
  4. python scripts/analyse.py tables writes the national result tables, including the explorer's.
  5. python scripts/microdata.py build builds the Catalan and Barcelona tables.
  6. python scripts/microdata.py quality checks the Catalan and Barcelona files.
  7. python scripts/microdata.py features builds the model's features.
  8. python scripts/microdata.py analyse writes the Catalan and Barcelona result tables.
  9. python scripts/microdata.py sources compares the Catalan file with DGT's.
  10. python scripts/severity_model.py fits and evaluates the Catalan severity model.
  11. python scripts/emef.py all estimates car-driving distance by age from the EMEF survey.
  12. python scripts/exposure_risk.py all computes involvement per kilometre by driver age and its ranges.
  13. python scripts/analyse.py figures draws the figures and captions.
  14. python scripts/build_site.py writes this site and docs/FINDINGS.md.

Each page reads its numbers from the result tables, and the build stops if a table no longer supports a sentence that describes it. Automated tests rerun the checks on every change. The README gives the set-up and the running times; the implementation notes name the module behind each method and the source register gives each source's known coding breaks and terms of use.

The research questions, the choice of sources, the statistical design, the interpretation and the decision to publish each result are the author's, and so is responsibility for them. AI coding assistants were used during implementation, debugging, data-processing work and review.

Long-run trends: fitting, projections and checks

These notes support Long-run trends; the methods document gives the full account.

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with up to three turning points, which describe the series and say nothing about causes. The variance is the mean times a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never below 1 (7.3 for the count, 2.8 per tonne of fuel); deaths per tonne of road fuel use the logarithm of fuel as an offset. Segments are at least four years long, and the number of turning points is chosen by QBIC, taking the simplest model within 2 points of the lowest. Both measures fall slowly to 2002–2003 (0.7% a year as a count), then 10.7% to 10.9% a year for 9–10 years; deaths per tonne of fuel then kept falling, 2.2% a year from 2011 to 2019. Segment intervals use Student's t and take the turning points as known (segments).

The projection continues the last segment, refitted on its own years, so it carries the scatter of those years: 1.5 for the count, the factor that sets an ordinary year's variation on the last year against 2019, and 1.6 per tonne of fuel. A year's range is the 95% prediction interval, with Student's t for five and seven residual degrees of freedom. Refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly, the search finds the same turning points, and 2023 and 2024 still lie above the range. Deaths per tonne of fuel in 2024 were 3.5% above 2019, within an ordinary year's variation, but above a trend that kept falling, about 2% a year and 10% in all by 2024.

The interurban series divides interurban deaths by the vehicle-kilometres the Ministerio de Transportes measures by traffic counts on State, regional and provincial roads. The kilometre series is comparable only from 2008, and the latest year, 2023, is partly estimated. Roads run by municipalities and other bodies carry 10.1% of interurban deaths in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023, so the level of the rate is too high by about that share, but its comparison moves by less than 1%. Measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% over 2019–2023. DGT's inspection-based kilometres, from 2022, put kilometres per tonne 3.0% lower in 2024 than in 2022.

If fewer lesser casualties are recorded, crashes per tonne fall and deaths per crash rise by the same factor, leaving deaths per tonne unchanged. The injury-crash series has two steps: injury crashes rose 13% from 1997 to 1998, and urban ones 26% between 2013 and 2016 while interurban ones fell 2%. On the measured networks in 2023, conventional roads had 2.28 times the injury crashes per kilometre of motorways and 1.51 times the deaths per injury crash.

DGT estimated the 30-day deaths of 1993 to 2010 from deaths within 24 hours, with correction factors, and has counted them since 2011 by matching crash records with the register of deaths.

Seasons: the month model and the traffic series

These notes support Seasons.

Each month is compared with the average month of the same year, by a quasi-Poisson regression of monthly deaths on year and month, with the days in the month, or road fuel sold, as the offset. It pools nine years from 2014 to 2024, leaving out 2020 and 2021. The index in the figure of deaths and traffic by month averages each year's ratio to its mean month, so it differs a little from the model.

Road fuel sold is the one series that divides deaths. Petrol sold, which leaves out diesel vehicles, and traffic on the state toll motorways, a small part of the network, are shown beside deaths only.

The 2006 points licence: model, placebos and specifications

These notes support The 2006 points licence.

The model is a Poisson regression of monthly deaths within 30 days from January 2000 to November 2007, with a term for each calendar month, a trend, and a step and a change of slope at July 2006; the slope changes by +7.5% a year (95% interval +1.4% to +14.0%). Intervals use Newey–West standard errors (12 lags). The pre-trend has one bend, chosen by QAIC on the months before the change alone; the straight line is 7 points worse, which favours the bend without being decisive.

The July placebos fit a straight-line trend on 60 months before and 17 after every July from 1998 to 2018 whose window avoids July 2006 and the pandemic. July 2006 ranks first of 15, a one-sided p-value of about 0.07, the smallest this test can give. The placebo steps vary 2.5 times as much as the model's standard errors imply, so the model's other intervals are probably too narrow as well.

Forecasting the 17 months after each July from the 60 months before it, the months after July 2006 fall 7.1% below their forecast, the third largest shortfall of 15; those after July 2004 and 2001 fell further. Deaths in the twelve months from July 2006 were 11.4% below the twelve months before, the fourth largest fall of 27 years; over 2003–2013 deaths fell about 11% a year anyway.

The window ends before the Penal Code reform of December 2007. Extended to December 2009 with a second step at the reform, the step is −0.8% (−9.4% to +8.7%). DGT estimated 30-day deaths from 24-hour deaths until 2010, which the fitted window rests on. Of 7 specifications the straight-line pre-trend gives the largest fall, and three give a step whose interval includes no change: a bend fixed at January 2004, a step without a change of slope and the window extended to December 2009 with a second break in December 2007.

Driver age and sex: kilometres and crashes per kilometre

These notes give the method behind the rates per kilometre on the drivers page; the exposure study gives it in full.

What is measured

The numerator is DGT's count of private-car drivers involved in injury crashes in 2024 and of those killed within 30 days, by age band; taxi and ride-hailing drivers are left out. No national source counts kilometres by the age of the driver, so the denominator is estimated.

From the surveys to Spain

The EMEF, the working-day mobility survey of the province of Barcelona, records the car trips its respondents drove on one working day, with each trip's duration and, in the later editions, its straight-line distance in bands, from which the kilometres are estimated (road distance is 1.45 times the straight line). Its 2022–2024 editions give each age group's car-driver kilometres per resident, by sex. Madrid's 2018 household travel survey (Consorcio Regional de Transportes de Madrid) gives the same measure for the Madrid region by exact age. Each survey's kilometres per resident are applied to the population of Spain, which gives each age group's share of the kilometres. Neither region stands for Spain, and the two disagree most at 65 and over, so the estimate uses the average of the two shares; the Barcelona-area survey's 65-and-over kilometres are first put on Spain's mix of ages at 65–74 and 75 and over, using Madrid's split between them.

The ranges

The range beside each ratio is the lowest and highest value under these alternatives, each computed around the published estimate: another regional profile (each survey alone, the Barcelona area carrying driving per licence holder instead of per resident, or one part of the province alone); another age mix for the weekends and holidays and for the remainder (the EMEF's weekend stays away, MOVILIA's weekend trips and long car journeys); another split of the 65-and-over kilometres at 75; and all the drivers of unrecorded age placed in one age group. The first three are combined in every way; the last is tried alone. A range is the spread of the alternatives tried, not a confidence interval and not a bound: other choices would widen it. At 18–29 the lowest ratio (1.5) comes from: Rest of the province alone; weekends and holidays: EMEF weekend stays; remainder: MOVILIA long journeys; the highest (3.6) from: Barcelona city alone.

The 95% sampling interval reflects only sampling error in the surveys and chance variation in the crash counts. It is probably too narrow, because the resampling treats respondents as independent although the surveys sample them in clusters. The EMEF respondents aged 65 and over were in work more often in 2022–2024 (6.0% to 8.3%) than the census counts for Catalonia (4.3%); setting the share to the census would lower the 65-and-over kilometres per resident by about 3%.

Deaths per kilometre

Deaths per kilometre combine involvement per kilometre with deaths once involved, so they carry the estimate of kilometres. Drivers aged 65 and over were killed 3.4 times as often per kilometre as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 2.7–4.4; range 2.0–4.3), against 1.4 times for involvement. Under every alternative the first ratio is above the second, so most of the excess in deaths per kilometre comes from how often a crash kills an older driver, not from how often older drivers are in crashes.

A check in Barcelona on working days

Inside Barcelona on working days, involvement per kilometre can be compared without carrying the survey to Spain or assuming anything about weekends: the Guàrdia Urbana's 2025 records give the age of most of the car drivers involved in injury crashes in the city, uninjured drivers included, and the EMEF gives the kilometres the survey area's residents drove inside the city on a working day, counted over the 248 working days of 2025. The part of a trip into or out of the city that lies inside it cannot be measured, so the table gives three versions of the kilometres, and only the ratios between ages are read.

Men and women

The survey's kilometres are split by sex and carried to Spain in the same way as by age; on that estimate men drove 66.6% of car kilometres in 2024. Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders and the drivers of vans, trucks and buses, the gaps per licence holder are wider. The licence holders are holders of any class of licence; the census gives car-licence holders by sex and age for 2023–2024 only, and counting only them raises the ratio of men's to women's deaths per licence holder in those years from 3.49 to 3.57.

Drivers whose age is not recorded

They are 2.3% of the drivers involved and are left out of every rate, which lowers each absolute rate by that share. The ratios assume their ages follow the recorded mix; the ranges include the case where all of them belong to one age group.

Drivers aged 75 and over

The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, so the survey measures their driving as one group. The split between 65–74 and 75 and over comes from Madrid's 2018 survey, which has exact ages: the estimate for 75 and over is a conditional estimate, true if drivers aged 75 and over drive as much less than those aged 65–74 in Spain as in Madrid. Two other splits are tried in the range: Madrid's kilometres per licence holder applied to Spain's licence holders, and equal kilometres per licence holder at 65–74 and 75 and over. Licence holding falls from 65–74 to 75 and over about as much in the province of Madrid as in Spain, so the first changes little; the second gives the lowest ratios. The sampling interval of the estimate (2.0–3.0) holds the split fixed, while the range (0.9–3.1) includes 1: the data do not establish that drivers aged 75 and over are involved more often per kilometre than drivers aged 45–64, or by how much. What they do show, with no kilometres, is that a crash is far more often fatal for them (see the section on deaths once a crash has happened on the drivers page).

Vehicle types: fleet, kilometres and crash counts

The rates on the vehicles page divide DGT's counts of vehicles involved in injury and fatal crashes in 2022 by the vehicles in circulation and by the kilometres they were driven. DGT models the kilometres from odometer readings taken at roadworthiness inspections, annualised and attached to the 2022 fleet; they are valid for aggregates, not individual vehicles, and cover all roads. Vans and trucks up to 3,500 kg form one group because the crash records and the vehicle register divide them differently; quadricycles count with mopeds and motorcycles in the kilometres but among other vehicles in the crash tables.

DGT's later release gives mean kilometres by vehicle type for 2023 and 2024 on the same basis (see Long-run trends); this page matches the crash tables and the kilometres for 2022 only. The crash counts include foreign-registered vehicles while the kilometres are those of Spanish-registered vehicles, a mismatch that cannot be measured and bears most on heavy trucks and buses.

Speed and other recorded factors: the speed report, the adjusted ratio and the break rule

These notes support Speed and other recorded factors; the methods document gives the full account. The data are DGT's Informe temático Factor Velocidad, which leaves out Catalonia and the Basque Country and gives crashes and deaths with speed recorded by road type, and the injury crashes with each of four factors recorded. Totals of all crashes by road type come from DGT's crash records for the same provinces, which reproduce the report's totals exactly in every year from 2014 to 2023; the report's interurban categories are matched to the records' road types by name, a match checked only through the interurban total.

The adjusted ratio is the speed coefficient of a quasi-Poisson model of deaths with crashes as exposure and road type and year as terms. It brings the crude ratio of 3.41 down to 2.00. It assumes one ratio for every road type, which the data reject (likelihood-ratio statistic 446 on 3 degrees of freedom), so it is a weighted summary of unequal ratios and its interval is widened by the square root of the Pearson dispersion (13.5). The road-type intervals are Poisson intervals widened the same way by each road type's own dispersion across years.

The break rule marks a rise of more than 25% or a fall of more than 20% in one year, or fewer than 200 crashes in either year, as a break; 6 of the 108 year-to-year changes are breaks or too small to test. The threshold is a convention, and an unbroken run is not proof that recording stayed the same: the rule rules out only single-year jumps. The drug series is left out because every change in it breaks the rule.

Crash circumstances in Spain: the regressions and their checks

These notes support Crash circumstances in Spain; the methods document, in its section on associations in DGT crash records, gives the full account.

The records and the regressions

DGT's file has one row per injury crash, with counts of the people killed and injured but no record of individual drivers, vehicles or people. Two logistic regressions are fitted, one for at least one death within 30 days and one for a death or a hospitalisation, on the circumstances the police record: zone, road type, crash type, junction, lighting, weather, road surface, alignment, time of day, weekend (from 20:00 on Friday to the end of Sunday), number of vehicles and year. A missing value is kept as a level of its own, so no crash is dropped, and levels with fewer than 500 crashes are merged into their reference category. The intervals are 95% confidence intervals, with standard errors clustered by province.

Illustrative crash profiles

Turned into probabilities for illustrative crashes in 2024, with every circumstance not named at its reference level, the odds ratios give a fitted probability of a death from 0.4% (urban street, side or front-side collision, daylight, two vehicles) to 12.0% (conventional road, head-on collision, daylight, two vehicles). The profiles show the size of the odds ratios, not a forecast for any one crash. The road type “other” mixes two kinds of crash: in 2024 DGT's records for the four Catalan provinces code urban streets as another kind of road (coding breaks), so its odds ratio, 1.74, rises to 2.40 without Catalonia's crashes.

The inverted junction flag

In 2023 and 2024 DGT's records for the four Catalan provinces code the junction flag the wrong way round (coding breaks), and the rest of each record shows it: only three of the 29,949 crashes they flag at a junction carry a junction type or a right-of-way flag, while 99.8% of the 17,756 they flag away from one carry either, as nearly every crash at a junction does in 2016–2022. The analysis reads the flag the other way round for these 47,705 crashes (5.5% of all). The Catalan share at a junction is then 38% in 2023 and 37% in 2024, close to the rest of Spain's (38%).

Outside Catalonia the odds ratio from 2023 is 0.74 (0.66–0.83). In the full model the flag as published gives 0.75 (0.69–0.81) against 0.69 (0.65–0.73) read the other way round, and from 2023 alone 0.98 (0.76–1.28) against 0.72 (0.65–0.79): the inverted records pull the published flag's association towards 1.

Checks on later years

Fitted on 2016–2022 without a year term and applied to the crashes of 2023–2024, the regression for a death ranks crashes well: given one crash with the outcome and one without, it gives the crash with the outcome the higher probability in 0.80 of pairs (ROC-AUC; 0.5 is chance, 1 a perfect ranking), and the regression for a death or a hospitalisation in 0.69. The probabilities are also calibrated: in the tenth of crashes with the highest fitted risk, 7.7% were predicted to be fatal and 7.2% were, and the mean fitted probability is 1.7% against 1.6% observed. Their Brier score (mean squared error) is nonetheless only 4.3% lower than giving every crash the training years' share (5.4% for a death or a hospitalisation), because deaths are rare. The regressions are not used for prediction, as a matter of purpose: they describe associations.

Refitted with every level that records a missing value folded into its reference, the regression for a death scores 0.78, and those levels on their own 0.54.

Missing values and regional recording

Some levels stand for a missing value, a field's “unknown” or “not specified” code. They stay in the regression but are left out of the charts and of the results in the text. Road alignment “unknown”, with an odds ratio of 0.12, is among the strongest terms and sits almost entirely in one region: it is recorded for 34.7% of crashes in Catalonia against 0.5% elsewhere, and 95.7% of its 78,990 crashes are Catalan, although Catalonia has 24.9% of all crashes. It marks recording practice, not an effect. The other missing-value levels are almost all outside Catalonia (at most 0.07% Catalan).

A recording practice that goes with the outcome could distort the other coefficients through such a level, so the regression for a death was refitted without Catalonia's 217,966 crashes. The full model's odds ratio lies inside the interval of the refit for 21 of the 32 terms. The largest moves are in zone and road type, which have to be read together: the zone “urban crossing” (4.28 to 2.57), the zone “interurban road” (3.24 to 2.00) and the road type “other road” (1.74 to 2.40). The wet-surface odds ratio moves from 0.62 to 0.72 and the junction odds ratio from 0.69 to 0.70, both still below 1.

How the file's counts compare with DGT's national records

The Catalan file shares no record identifier with DGT's national crash records, so the two are compared by year and province (the file's traffic demarcations). Over 2016–2023, the file's fatal crashes equal DGT's crashes with a death within 24 hours in all 32 province-years, and its total count matches DGT's 24-hour count of crashes with a death or serious injury to within one crash in every province-year. Its fatal count is 67–95% of DGT's count of crashes with a death within 30 days. The file does not state its definition; the counts show that it is the 24-hour one.

Fields left blank more often in non-fatal crashes

The recording check, run on the crashes of 2010–2022, finds eight fields that are left blank or marked “not specified” much more often in non-fatal crashes than in fatal ones. The field for special lanes, for example, is blank in 7.7% of non-fatal crashes and 1.0% of fatal ones. The file gives no reason, but the blank itself carries information about the outcome, and none of these fields is used on the Catalonia page. Otherwise the file is consistent: it has no exact duplicate rows (14 rows are identical to another in everything but the time, and are kept), and every severity label agrees with the deaths and injuries recorded on the same row.

Barcelona: the people left out, the rear-end codes and every recorded cause

Serious or fatal injury by road user, age and sex

A person counts as seriously injured after more than 24 hours in hospital, and the shares put serious and fatal injuries together. They leave out the 1,351 people whose outcome was not recorded and one death the police recorded as natural. Crashes without a victim, and the two rear-end codes

The police recorded no victim in 890 of the 7,741 crashes they attended. Among the crashes with a victim, 3.7% had a serious or fatal injury, against 3.3% of all crashes.

The rear-end group joins two codes: the Guàrdia Urbana's own code for a rear-end collision (2 of 1,672 crashes with a serious or fatal injury) and the Catalan file's term for one (26 of 43). How the Catalan severity model was built

The model is a penalised logistic regression fitted to the 22,638 crashes of 2010–2023 in the Servei Català de Trànsit's file. It has a starting level for each zone (urban street, road through a town, interurban road) in each province, and one term for each other input, which may differ between urban streets and interurban roads. Its settings were chosen by the rule used for every test year: fitted on 2010–2021 and scored on 2022–2023, the model whose terms may differ between urban streets and interurban roads predicted better than the model whose terms are the same on every kind of road, with a penalty setting C of 0.1 (a smaller C penalises the terms more strongly), chosen from values between 0.001 and 32 (the grid is extended while its best value lies at an end) and better than its neighbours on both sides; roads through towns get the average fatal share of such roads in the province because it predicted those years better than the model; and each province has a starting level of its own because that predicted those years better than one level per zone for all of Catalonia. In the test years the same rule chose the model whose terms may differ by kind of road in six of the eight years, with C from 0.01 to 1,000, and a starting level for each province in seven; in 2017 the best penalty was the weakest the grid allows, where the validation loss had stopped changing, so that year's model is in effect unpenalised. Its intervals come from refitting it on crashes resampled with replacement.

Left out are 1,840 crashes on conventional roads whose owner is recorded as “other” or left blank: a blank owner is far commoner on fatal records and “other” on non-fatal ones, so the field records how a crash was documented, not the road. The estimates, the averages and the tests therefore describe crashes on roads with a named owning network or of another type. The crashes left out were fatal in 14.7% of cases. With them, 12.6% of all crashes would count as fatal instead of 12.5%, and 19.2% of interurban crashes instead of 20.0%.

A more flexible method, gradient-boosted trees, ranked crashes a little better (0.75) but gives no interval for an estimate and cannot be read term by term, so the logistic model is the one published. The method is described in the model note.
