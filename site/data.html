Definitions

Injury crash
A crash on a public road in which at least one person is killed or injured. DGT's national records and series count only injury crashes, and a slight injury there needs medical care. Barcelona's crash table also holds crashes the Guàrdia Urbana attended in which nobody was hurt (890 in its year), and it counts people who refused medical care as slightly injured.
Death
A death within 30 days of the crash, DGT's consolidated definition, used for the national series and rates. The Catalan file counts deaths within 24 hours; Barcelona's records keep deaths within 24 hours apart from later deaths, and the analysis of people counts both. In DGT's national records for 2016–2024, 14.2% of the deaths within 30 days occurred after the first 24 hours, so the two definitions are never combined.
Serious injury
Admission to hospital for more than 24 hours; any other injury is slight. The Catalan file and Barcelona's crash table classify at 24 hours, so a person who died later counts there as seriously injured. The Catalan file holds crashes with a death or serious injury (serious and fatal crashes); those in which nobody died within 24 hours are non-fatal.
Fatal share
Among the serious and fatal crashes in Catalonia, the share in which someone died within 24 hours. For Barcelona the site gives a serious-or-fatal share instead: of the people whose outcome was recorded, or of the crashes the police attended, the share with a serious or fatal injury.
Zone
DGT's grouping of roads into interurban roads, and urban streets and crossings. The Catalan file also distinguishes through-town roads, which count as urban in comparisons with DGT's records.
Driver involved
The driver of a vehicle in an injury crash, injured or not.
Licence holder
A holder of any driving permit in DGT's census; the B permit is the car licence.
Crash frequency
How often a group appears in injury crashes relative to a measure of its exposure, such as kilometres driven, fuel sold or licence holders.
Severity
How serious the outcome is once a crash, or a person in one, is already in the records: deaths per injury crash, deaths per driver involved, the fatal share of serious crashes. A death rate per unit of exposure is crash frequency multiplied by severity.
Recorded factor
A circumstance the police record about a crash, such as alcohol, inappropriate speed or distraction (Barcelona's records call them causes). It is a police judgement, not a finding by this study that the circumstance caused the crash.
Association
A statistical relationship in observed records. Every comparison on the site between an outcome and a circumstance is an association; none is an estimate of a causal effect, which would need a design that these records do not provide.
Predictive model
A model judged only on records not used to fit it, and kept only if it ranks those records better than a simple table of the same data.
Sampling interval
A 95% interval that allows only for chance: which people a survey happened to sample, and chance in the counts of crashes. It takes every other choice of the analysis as given, so the real uncertainty can be much wider.
Sensitivity range
The span of an estimate across the alternative assumptions tested. It carries no probability, and it is not a limit: assumptions not tested, or tested ones combined differently, could fall outside it.
Conditional estimate
An estimate that holds only if a stated assumption holds, and is always printed with it; its sampling interval holds the assumption fixed.

Rates, denominators and intervals

Each rate pairs a count with a denominator meant to contain it:

Per resident
The burden of road deaths on a population, most of whom were not travelling at the time. A rate per resident falls when people travel less.
Per licence holder
Counts only drivers: on the trend pages the drivers of motorcycles, cars, vans, trucks and buses killed or admitted to hospital, and on the drivers page car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not.
Per registered vehicle
Counts only the occupants of motorcycles, cars, vans, trucks and buses.
Per tonne of road fuel
Tonnes of petrol and diesel sold: the only annual measure of traffic that covers every road.
Per vehicle-kilometre
The Ministerio de Transportes' measurements on State, regional and provincial interurban roads, and DGT's estimates by vehicle type from inspection odometer readings. Kilometres by driver age are estimated from the EMEF working-day survey's age profile, applied to Spain's population and scaled to DGT's car kilometres.
Per driver involved
Deaths among the drivers involved in injury crashes: how often involvement ends in death. It needs no measure of travel.

Pedestrians and cyclists hold no licence for the trip in which they are hurt and travel in no registered vehicle, so they are counted against residents and road fuel only. Road fuel stands in for the kilometres driven on all roads, and the kilometres by driver age carry one region's survey to Spain; neither can be tested for Spain as a whole (assumptions tested). Some rates do not fully meet the rule, and each page says so: rates per resident count visitors' deaths, rates per licence holder (by sex, and on Since 2019) count unlicensed and foreign drivers, rates per registered vehicle and by vehicle type count foreign vehicles, and interurban deaths are compared with national road fuel only as a check.

How often crashes happen and how deadly they are

A death rate measured against traffic combines two quantities that can move separately: deaths per kilometre (or per tonne of fuel) equal injury crashes per kilometre multiplied by deaths per injury crash. The split depends on how completely slight-injury crashes are recorded: if fewer are recorded, crashes per kilometre fall and deaths per crash rise by the same factor, and their product does not move. Deaths are counted completely, so the death rate is the firmer measure and its split the more fragile reading. The split is applied on Long-run trends, Drivers and Vehicles.

Intervals and ordinary year-to-year variation

Rates carry 95% intervals that treat counts as Poisson with a known denominator; ratios of two rates carry log-normal intervals. Spain's annual counts scatter around their trend more than Poisson chance allows, so changes against 2019 are read against intervals widened to span an ordinary year's variation (Since 2019; assumptions tested).

Reading police crash records

Crash records contain only the crashes the police recorded, and the Catalan file only those with a death or serious injury. A share computed from such records, such as the fatal share on interurban roads, measures how severe crashes were once they had happened and been recorded. It does not measure how often crashes happen or how dangerous a road is per kilometre travelled, because the records contain no measure of travel. The severity models share this limit: they predict a severe outcome only among crashes that were recorded.

The recorded factors on the site are DGT's, such as speed, alcohol or distraction, the Catalan file's “influence” fields, and Barcelona's contributing factors and driver causes. A crash can carry several, and how completely they are recorded varies by year and with the severity of the crash.

Missing values keep their own categories. “Not specified”, “not applicable”, a field's own “unknown” code and an empty cell are four different states, and none is read as zero or as “no”, except in the fog and strong-wind fields. DGT fills those in only when there was fog or strong wind, apart from a few province-years that code them another way (coding breaks). In Barcelona's crash table a blank count also means zero: no cell holds an explicit zero, and with blanks read as zero the victims add up in every crash.

The audit of DGT's records counts a field as unrecorded when it is “not specified”, “unknown” or empty in a crash it applies to: the junction type and the right-of-way flags, for instance, do not apply to a crash away from a junction. Of the 18 fields it examines (the 13 right-of-way flags counted as one), three are unrecorded in more than 30% of the crashes they apply to, one of them only just (30.2%). How unevenly the provinces record them is set out under Data sources and scope.

The figure reads the fields the audit examines by the same rule and leaves “not applicable” out of each field's share, so a field that applies only to some crashes is judged on the crashes it applies to. The pavement field is “not applicable” in 87% of crashes, the junction type in 61% (those away from a junction), and the island field is empty, which DGT's dictionary defines as not applicable, in 94%. In the figure an empty fog or strong-wind field counts as recorded.

Recording can also depend on the outcome. Fatal crashes may be investigated more fully, so fields left unspecified in non-fatal crashes are more often filled in for fatal ones. In the Catalan file, nine placeholder levels in eight fields (“not specified” or an unexplained blank) are recorded at least 1.5 times as often in non-fatal crashes as in fatal ones; these fields are left out of every published model. The road owner is the reverse case, blank far more often on fatal records, and the calculator leaves out the crashes it affects (model review).

Coding breaks in DGT's records

Four changes in DGT's coding affect series by road type and junction, and all four come from the records for the four Catalan provinces.

  • Until 2020 those records code almost every crash on a conventional road as a conventional road with a dual carriageway (3,727 such crashes in 2020, 7 on single carriageways); from 2021 they use the single-carriageway code instead. Elsewhere the dual-carriageway code holds between 1,257 and 1,769 crashes a year.
  • In 2024 many crashes on urban streets begin to be coded in the “other” road group (codes 7, 8 and 10–14, service roads, slip roads, cycle paths and other minor roads): 83% of that year's crashes in the group are on urban streets, against 43% in 2016–2023. Most of them carry code 14, “other” itself, and 15,531 of the 17,519 crashes with that code in 2024 are Catalan.
  • From 2023 the same records code the junction flag the wrong way round. The share of their crashes coded at a junction goes from between 38% and 42% a year in 2016–2022 (35% in 2021, under the narrower definition below) to 62% in 2023 and 63% in 2024. Elsewhere it stays between 37% and 40%. From 2023, their crashes coded at a junction carry no junction type (“not specified”) in every case, and 94% of those coded away from a junction carry one, a field the rest of Spain leaves empty away from a junction. The Servei Català de Trànsit's file holds the same crashes with a death or serious injury: in 2023 DGT codes 69.5% of the Catalan ones at a junction, and the Catalan file places 69.6% of them between junctions.
  • In 2021 the records use a narrower definition of a junction: DGT's count of these Catalan crashes at a junction matches the Catalan file's crashes within a junction, where in 2016–2020 and 2022 it matches those within or within 50 metres of one. That year 712 Catalan crashes coded away from a junction carry a junction type or a right-of-way flag, against 10 in all the other years before 2023; counted at a junction, they bring that year's share to 38%.

Road-type series are therefore read year by year and alongside zone, the two kinds of conventional road form one group, and no road-type trend is drawn. Comparisons of Catalonia with the rest of Spain group every conventional road together for the same reason (Model method and tests).

Junction shares as published are not compared across 2023. The association of junctions with fatal outcomes reads the flag the other way round in those province-years, which puts the Catalan share at a junction at 38% in 2023 and 37% in 2024, against 38% elsewhere in Spain in the same years (Crash circumstances in Spain).

The fog and strong-wind fields are filled in only when there was fog or strong wind, so a blank in them is read as none, except where a province's records give the condition to more than 10% of their crashes. Elsewhere fog or strong wind is recorded in at most 7.6% of a province-year's crashes.

From 2017 the records for the province of Barcelona code fog in 33% to 45% of crashes a year, against 0.0% in 2016. The Servei Català de Trànsit's file records fog in the same number of the province's crashes with a death or serious injury in each year of 2017–2023. Strong wind is recorded in every crash of Barcelona, Girona, Lleida and Tarragona in 2021 and in 19% to 22% of Sevilla's crashes in 2017–2018. In those province-years the field is coded another way: neither a value nor a blank says whether there was fog or strong wind. Neither field enters the national association analysis or the calculator.

How the models were built and judged

The published model is the calculator's: a penalised logistic regression of whether a crash with a death or serious injury in Catalonia was fatal, with a death within 24 hours, given circumstances a reader can describe (Crash severity model). Crashes on conventional roads whose owner is recorded as “other” or left blank are left out, because that field records how a crash was documented (model review), so the model and its tests describe crashes on roads with a named owning network or of another type.

The model is tested by nested rolling origin. For each year of 2016–2023, three settings are chosen by fitting on the earlier years except the last two and scoring those two:

  • The strength of the penalty, from 10 values and more if the best lies at either end.
  • Whether each circumstance may count differently on urban streets and interurban roads.
  • Whether roads through towns get the model's estimate or the average of such roads in the province.

The model is then fitted on all the earlier years and predicts the year, beside a table of fatal shares by road and crash type fitted on the same years, so no choice sees the year it predicts. The published model was chosen by the same rule, scoring 2022–2023 after fitting on 2010–2021, and then fitted on every year. Its intervals come from 500 refits on resampled crashes. Its method is set out in the calculator report.

ROC-AUC measures how well a model ranks: it is the probability that the model places a randomly chosen case with the outcome above a randomly chosen case without it. A value of 0.5 is no better than chance and 1 is a perfect ranking. The site gives it to two decimals: the Catalan severity model's ROC-AUC of 0.74 over 2016–2023 means that it ranks a fatal crash above a non-fatal one in 74% of such pairs. When the outcome is rare, as deaths are, ROC-AUC can be high while most of the cases ranked highest are still cases without it.

A model that ranks well may still add nothing to what a simple table shows. Each model is therefore compared, on the same test records, with a table fixed before the test: the share of severe outcomes in each group of the training records. A model is kept only if its ROC-AUC exceeds the table's by at least 0.02 and the 95% interval of the difference excludes zero. Over 2016–2023 the Catalan severity model scores 0.74 against 0.71 for its table, a gain of 0.03 (95% interval 0.02–0.04).

A model can rank well and still give probabilities that are too high or too low. Calibration compares the predicted probabilities with the observed shares in groups of test records; its slope is 1 when the predictions spread exactly as widely as the outcomes, and above 1 when they are not spread widely enough. Probabilities are shown as estimates only if the slope lies between 0.8 and 1.25 and the mean predicted probability is within 25% of the observed share, a rule fixed in advance.

Over all the test years the Catalan severity model passes this rule: the slope of its recalibration fit is 1.03 and its mean prediction is 12.4% against 12.3% observed, but it does not pass in every province and kind of road (Crash severity model). The fit's intercept is 0.05; for estimates that match the outcomes the slope is 1 and the intercept 0. Intervals for the scores come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the test records, 500 in the external tests.

The project's earlier models used a single split by time. The original Catalan model was trained on the crashes of 2010–2020, its settings were chosen on 2021–2022, it was refitted on 2010–2022 and tested once on 2023. It was replaced by the Catalan severity model, because its strongest predictor was the road's owner.

The Barcelona models were trained on months 1–9 of 2025, with settings chosen by 5-fold cross-validation that keeps the people of one crash in the same fold, and tested on months 10–12; no crash appears on both sides of a split. The Barcelona crash-severity model ranked unseen crashes no better than its table of shares by accident type, so the study reports the table instead. The Barcelona person-severity model ranks people without giving probabilities and is kept for research. Every model the project fitted was re-evaluated in the model review.

The external tests set each model beside a model of the same kind trained in the test population. The Catalan severity model and the original Catalan model are tested with each of the four provinces left out in turn and with Barcelona city against the rest of Catalonia, and the Barcelona models on one district at a time.

No other source records the calculator's inputs, so it has no test outside the Catalan file. For DGT's records elsewhere in Spain, a harmonised version of the original model refits the original model's specification on the 11 of 12 candidate variables whose mapped distributions match on the Catalan crashes of 2016–2023, which both files contain (road type does not). Barcelona's records share no crash with the Catalan file, so the eight variables used for Barcelona were chosen only because their codings map exactly or defensibly (Model method and tests).

Before fitting, each variable was classified by how far the outcome could shape it. The models use only circumstances recorded about the crash or the person. Variables the outcome may shape, such as police causes and the Catalan influence fields, enter only separate comparison versions of the original models, and variables that encode the outcome enter none (audit of the variables). The check for fields recorded differently when a crash was fatal looked only for placeholders rarer among fatal crashes, pooled over zones, and did not catch the road-owner field; the re-evaluation found it.

Since 2019: ordinary variation and denominators

These notes support Since 2019; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on counts against risk.

How ordinary variation was measured

Each interval on the page is widened by the square root of its own count's dispersion factor: the variance of the count around the 2013–2019 trend as a multiple of the pure-chance (Poisson) variance. The page gives the three factors. Each rests on seven years and is itself uncertain: the 95% interval of the factor for deaths runs from 0.6 to 8.7, so whether deaths vary beyond chance is not established (assumptions tested).

The intervals therefore use Student's t distribution with the trend fit's five residual degrees of freedom instead of the normal distribution, which widens them by 31%. The factor for deaths is the one Long-run trends uses to project the count from 2020.

The intervals for injury crashes

Injury crashes fell 2.0% as a count, 5.5% per resident and 0.3% per tonne of road fuel. Judged against pure chance alone, the falls as a count and per resident would be real changes; against the 2013–2019 scatter none of them stands out.

That scatter does not describe an ordinary year for crashes. Between 2013 and 2016 recorded urban injury crashes rose 26% while interurban ones fell 2%, a step that a straight trend cannot follow, so the crash intervals are about 11 times as wide as pure-chance intervals and too wide to say whether the falls are larger than an ordinary year.

DGT's yearbook does not split injury crashes by vehicle type, so they have no rate per licence holder or registered vehicle.

Which casualties each denominator divides

Licence holders divide only the deaths and admissions of drivers, and the fleet only those of occupants, of motorcycles, cars, vans, trucks and buses: pedestrians and cyclists hold no licence for their journey and travel in no registered vehicle.

Bicycles and personal mobility vehicles are left out of both because they need neither a licence nor a registration. Mopeds are left out because it is not established whether the fleet counts them, and the two numerators are kept to the same vehicles. The yearbook's category of other vehicles is left out because it held personal mobility vehicles until they were given their own category.

Kilometres per tonne of fuel and DGT's kilometre estimates

The hypothetical drifts on the page are illustrative assumptions, not estimates. Had kilometres per tonne grown 1% a year since 2019, deaths per kilometre would show a fall of 1.5%; at 2% a year, a fall of 6.2%, where deaths per tonne of fuel show a rise of 3.5%. The same drift bears on how far deaths per tonne of fuel lie above the pre-pandemic trend (Long-run trends).

DGT's kilometre release of 2024 gives the mean distance driven per vehicle for 2022, 2023 and 2024 as one series, estimated from roadworthiness inspections, and its 2022 values are those of DGT's 2022 release. By those estimates a car was driven 13,073 km on average in 2022 and 12,295 km in 2024, and all vehicles together drove 1.6% fewer kilometres in 2024 than in 2022, while road fuel sold rose 1.5%.

Over those years deaths per kilometre by DGT's estimate rose 3.9% and deaths per tonne of fuel 0.8%. The series starts in 2022, so it cannot be set against 2019 or the long run, and fuel remains the denominator of Since 2019. The rates per kilometre on the vehicles page use its 2022 estimates, and those on the drivers page its 2024 estimates.

Long-run trends: fitting, projections and checks

These notes support Long-run trends; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on the long run and the pandemic.

How the trends were fitted

Each trend changes by a constant percentage a year between up to three turning points placed where the data put them; the turning points describe the series and say nothing about causes. The count, occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of fuel turn within 2 years of each other.

All three show a slower decline to 2002–2003 (0.7% a year as a count, 4.4% to 4.8% a year as rates), then a fall of between 10.7% and 13.4% a year over the next 9–10 years. In their last segment the count and deaths per vehicle show no clear trend (both intervals include no change), while deaths per tonne of fuel kept falling, 2.2% a year from 2011 to 2019 (1.2% a year over 2013–2019). The intervals in the table take the turning points as known.

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with quasi-likelihood errors: the variance is the mean multiplied by a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never set below 1. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel are fitted as death counts with the logarithm of the fleet or of fuel as an offset; the long-run page's first figure multiplies their trends back by each year's fleet or fuel.

For each number of turning points from none to three, every placement that leaves segments at least four years long is fitted and the best kept. The number of turning points is then chosen by QBIC, a Bayesian information criterion in which the Poisson log-likelihood is divided by the dispersion of the largest model. The simplest model within 2 points of the lowest QBIC is taken.

The dispersion factors of the chosen models are 7.3 for the count, 4.5 per registered vehicle and 2.8 per tonne of fuel. Segment intervals are 95% confidence intervals for the annual change, from Student's t with the fit's residual degrees of freedom.

The projection continues the last segment: the trend of the years from the last turning point to 2019, refitted on those years alone. Its scatter is that of those years: 1.5 for the count (the factor that Since 2019 uses for an ordinary year's variation in deaths), 2.8 per registered vehicle and 1.6 per tonne of fuel; the whole fit's factors also carry the poorer fit of the early years.

The 95% prediction interval of a projected year, called its range, combines the uncertainty of the refitted trend with the scatter of a single year around it, with Student's t quantiles for the five, five and seven residual degrees of freedom of the three refits.

The pre-pandemic projections under other choices

The count of deaths was 25% below its trend in 2020 and 18% below in 2021, outside the range, and has been within it since 2022; in 2024 it was 8% below the trend. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle were 25% below their trend in 2020, outside its range, and 15% below in 2021, within it. Their range is wider because occupant deaths are fewer and scatter more around their 2013–2019 trend.

Whether a year this far below trend lies outside the range depends mostly on the scatter it is read against: with the continuous trend and the scatter of the whole 1993–2019 fit, which the poorer fit of the early years inflates, the count in 2021 lies within its range and the per-vehicle measure outside it.

Deaths per tonne of fuel stayed within their range in 2020 and 2021, because fuel sales fell too, and were 16% and 14% above the trend in 2023 and 2024. Against 2019 itself, deaths per tonne of fuel in 2024 were 3.5% higher, within the variation of an ordinary year. The two readings differ because the trend kept falling after 2019, about 2% a year and 10% in all by 2024: the trend asks whether the decline of 2011–2019 went on, the comparison with 2019 whether the rate rose.

The search puts the last turning point of deaths per tonne of fuel in 2011. They fell 10% from 2011 to 2013 and then 1.2% a year over 2013–2019, so a trend started in 2013 leaves smaller excesses (table below). Refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly, the search finds the same turning points, and 2023 and 2024 still lie above the range.

The excess also assumes that the kilometres a tonne of fuel represents kept changing after 2019 at their pace of 2011–2019. No series can check this on all roads (road fuel as a measure of traffic). Had kilometres per tonne grown faster by 1.3 percentage points a year from 2020, the 2023 excess would fall within the range; for 2024, 0.6 points would be enough.

Interurban deaths per measured kilometre

The Ministerio de Transportes measures, by traffic counts, the vehicle-kilometres travelled each year on the interurban roads of the State, the regions and the provincial councils. Dividing interurban deaths by them gives the only available series whose numerator and denominator cover nearly the same roads. Fitted to interurban deaths per kilometre over 2008–2019 (the kilometre series is comparable only from 2008), the same trend model places the last turning point in 2013, and the trend refitted to 2013–2019 falls 1.8% a year.

The latest year, 2023, is partly estimated: the yearbook keeps the regional and provincial networks' lengths of the year before and takes the State network's traffic as the reference for all networks. Projected forward with each year's kilometres, 2023 comes out 5% above the trend, inside its range of ±12%. In 2020, when measured kilometres fell 22%, deaths per kilometre were on trend (a ratio of 1.00).

The kilometres leave out roads run by municipalities and other bodies, which DGT's crash records identify: between 8.7% and 11.2% of interurban deaths each year from 2016 to 2024 occurred on them, 10.1% in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023. The rate's level is therefore too high by about that share, but the comparison between 2019 and 2023 moves by less than 1%. Before 2016 the share cannot be measured.

Divided instead by national road fuel, with a trend fitted the same way, the same deaths were 13% above their trend in 2023, against a range of ±12%. Fuel is sold for every road, towns included, so this ratio serves only as a check on road fuel as a measure of traffic.

The check shows the two measures parting: measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national road fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% a year over 2019–2023 (from 8,741 to 9,420), 1.4 percentage points a year faster. Had kilometres per tonne grown that much faster on all roads, the 2023 excess in deaths per tonne of fuel would fall within its range.

The other source of kilometres points the other way: DGT's inspection-based kilometres, which start in 2022, put kilometres per tonne of fuel 3.0% lower in 2024 than in 2022 (road fuel as a measure of traffic). On all roads, deaths per kilometre are not measured.

Recording behind the long-run series

By injury crashes most of the fall in deaths per tonne of fuel is in how deadly a crash is; by admissions to hospital all of it is in how often people are seriously hurt. Each split depends on how completely the lesser casualties are recorded: if fewer of them are recorded, the frequency falls and the deaths per casualty rise by the same factor, leaving their product unchanged. The injury-crash series has two steps inside the split's years: injury crashes rose 13% from 1997 to 1998 (21% on interurban roads), and urban injury crashes rose 26% between 2013 and 2016 while interurban ones fell 2%.

On the measured networks in 2023, conventional roads had 2.28 times the injury crashes per kilometre of motorways and 1.51 times the deaths per injury crash; that split too depends on how completely injury crashes are recorded on each kind of road. The interval of the ratio of deaths per kilometre allows for Poisson error in the two death counts and takes the kilometres as exact.

DGT estimated the 30-day deaths of 1993 to 2010 from deaths within 24 hours, with correction factors drawn from following a sample of people admitted to hospital. Since 2011 it has counted them by matching crash records with the register of deaths (DGT's accident yearbook, annex on the revised method). The trends refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly throughout, place the turning points of the count and of deaths per tonne of fuel in the same years (assumptions tested).

Seasons: the month model and the traffic series

These notes support Seasons; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on seasonality and mobility.

How months are compared

Each month is compared with the average month of the same year, so the long-run trend does not enter the seasonal pattern; the model compares each month with the geometric mean of the year's months. The intervals allow for monthly counts that vary more than chance alone would produce. Both versions, as a count and per tonne of road fuel, pool nine years from 2014 to 2024, leaving out 2020 and 2021.

The index in the figure of deaths and traffic by month is a simple average of each year's ratio to its mean month, so it differs a little from the model: the average August stands at 113 on the index and at 1.14 times the average month in the model.

The three measures of traffic

Road fuel sold (petrol plus diesel) is the one series used to divide deaths, standing in for kilometres as on the annual pages. Two narrower series are shown beside deaths only: petrol sold, which leaves out every diesel vehicle, and traffic on the state toll motorways, measured directly but on a small part of the network, as vehicles per kilometre of motorway. On the index road fuel sold falls from 109 in July to 105 in August, while petrol sold rises from 115 to 116.

The summer excess reads differently against each. Against road fuel sold, deaths rise more than fuel sales in July and August; against toll-motorway traffic they rise less in both months; against petrol sales they rise more in July and less in August.

The 2020 lockdown against 2017–2019

Both narrower series were already above their 2017–2019 level before the lockdown: in January and February 2020 petrol sold was 13% and 16% higher and toll-motorway traffic 9% and 10% higher. The toll network had also shrunk, from 2,513 km on average in the April of 2017–2019 to 1,894 km, as concessions expired. Measured from their level early in the year, the April falls of petrol and toll traffic would be larger still, so deaths still fell less than both.

The 2006 points licence: model, placebos and specifications

These notes support The 2006 points licence; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on the 2006 case study.

The model and the choice of pre-trend

The model is a Poisson regression of monthly deaths within 30 days from January 2000 to November 2007, with a term for each calendar month, a trend, and a step and a change of slope at July 2006. It projects the months before the change forward and measures how far the months after fall below that projection. After the step of −7%, the slope changes by +7.5% a year (95% interval +1.4% to +14.0%), so fitted deaths meet the projection again in about July 2007. That is why the average over the 17 months is smaller than the step.

Monthly deaths vary more than a Poisson model assumes and are correlated from month to month; the intervals use standard errors that allow for both (Newey–West, 12 lags), and a negative binomial model gives a similar step (−7%). Even so, the July placebos show these model-based intervals to be too narrow.

The earlier months favour a trend that bends once, in August 2003, where the decline steepened: it fits them better than the straight line by 7 points of QAIC, a measure of fit that penalises extra terms and allows for monthly deaths varying more than a Poisson model assumes. A difference of that size favours the bend but is not decisive. The choice uses only the months before the change; it is still a selection step, which is why the alternative pre-trends and specifications are reported.

July placebos, forecasts and twelve-month comparisons

Spanish road deaths usually peak in July or August: one of the two was the deadliest month in 27 of the 32 years from 1993 to 2024. The same step model, with a straight-line trend on a common window of 60 months before and 17 after, was fitted at every July from 1998 to 2018 whose window does not include July 2006 or the pandemic; that leaves out the Julys of 2005 and 2007–2011.

If the licence had changed nothing, July 2006 would still rank first one time in 15, a one-sided p-value of about 0.07. That is the smallest this test can give with 15 Julys, so it cannot reach conventional significance; the narrow margin over the next July is the better guide.

At 6 of the 14 other Julys, where nothing was introduced, the 95% interval lies entirely on one side of zero; an interval with the coverage it claims would do so about 0.7 times in 14. The placebo steps vary 2.5 times as much as the model's standard errors imply. An interval set by that spread puts the July 2006 step at −21% to +3%, which includes no change. The other intervals of the case study come from the same kind of model, so they are probably too narrow as well.

Two checks fit no step. Forecasting the 17 months after each July from the 60 months before it, the months after July 2006 fall 7.1% below their forecast, the third largest shortfall of the 15 Julys; the months after July 2004 and 2001 fell further below their own forecasts. Comparing the twelve months from each July with the twelve months before it, which cancels the seasons, deaths fell 11.4% across July 2006, the fourth largest fall of the 27 years that can be measured.

The larger falls all came later, in 2008, 2009 and 2012. This comparison does not remove the trend: over 2003–2013 deaths fell about 11% a year, so a fall of this size across one July is what the trend alone would give.

Deaths per kilometre

Deaths per kilometre combine involvement per kilometre with deaths once involved, so they carry the estimate of kilometres. On the estimate, drivers aged 65 and over were killed 3.44 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 2.7–4.4; sensitivity range 2.09–4.46), while their involvement per kilometre was 1.41 times (sensitivity range 0.85–1.82).

Under every assumption tested the first ratio is above the second, so most of the excess in deaths per kilometre comes from how often a crash kills an older driver, not from how often older drivers are in crashes.

Drivers whose age is not recorded

Drivers whose age the tables do not record (2.3% of those involved) are left out of every rate, which lowers each absolute rate by that share. The ratios assume their ages follow the recorded mix: if all were aged 18–29, the young drivers' ratio would be 2.96; assigned to any one age group, they would put the ratio for 65 and over between 1.32 and 1.68.

The former owner-age figure

Until October 2026 the drivers page divided the same drivers, taxi and ride-hailing drivers included, by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, the only kilometres by age that DGT publishes. On those kilometres it said that drivers aged 75 and over were involved about as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54 (1.02 times); the figure it gave for young drivers, 6.75, compared drivers aged 18–24 with 35–54.

On the age groups used now, those owner kilometres put drivers aged 18–29 at 4.62 times the 45–64 rate and drivers aged 65 and over at 1.00, against 2.68 and 1.41 on the estimated kilometres.

A car's owner is often not its driver: young people drive cars registered to their parents, and owners aged 75 and over have 1.14 cars registered to them for every car-licence holder of that age, more cars than holders, some of them driven by others. So the owner kilometres credited too little driving to the young and too much to the old.

Drivers aged 75 and over: the conditional estimate and its range

No source for Spain measures how far drivers aged 75 and over drive apart from those aged 65–74, so their rates per kilometre rest on an assumption about how the 65-and-over kilometres divide between the two ages: one gives the conditional estimate, the others the sensitivity range. The counts, the rates per licence holder and the deaths once involved need no kilometres.

Counted. In 2024, 4,455 car drivers aged 75 and over and 6,970 aged 65–74 were involved in injury crashes in Spain, and 71 and 59 of them died within 30 days: 15.9 and 8.5 per 1,000 involved, against 4.6 at 45–64 (charted on the drivers page). These figures say how often a crash kills the driver. They do not say how often drivers of these ages are in crashes for the distance they drive, or who caused the crashes.

Per 1,000 holders of a car licence, 2.84 drivers aged 75 and over were involved, against 2.13 at 65–74 and 2.98 at 45–64. Many licence holders aged 75 and over drive little or not at all, so these are not rates per driver. Every estimate per kilometre below is higher than the ratio of these rates, 0.95, because under every assumption tested licence holders aged 75 and over drive fewer kilometres than those aged 45–64.

Conditional estimate. The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, although the survey sampled 65–74 and 75 and over separately until 2016. Madrid's 2018 household travel survey records exact ages: there, residents aged 75 and over drove 0.34 times the car distance per resident of those aged 65–74 if men and 0.28 times if women.

If people aged 75 and over in Spain in 2024 drive as much less as in Madrid, with the kilometres by age from the average of the two surveys' profiles, drivers aged 75 and over were involved in injury crashes 2.43 times as often per kilometre as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 2.0–3.0), and drivers aged 65–74 1.11 times (1.0–1.2). Under the other assumptions tested the figure for 75 and over runs from 0.97 to 3.20 (the sensitivity range, below).

The sampling intervals allow for sampling error in both surveys and chance in the crash counts, and hold the Madrid assumption fixed. They may be too narrow or too wide: the Barcelona-area survey's resampling treats respondents as independent although the survey samples them in clusters, which makes an interval too narrow, and the Madrid survey's ignores the stratification of its sample, which makes one too wide.

The Barcelona-area survey's kilometres at 65 and over are a mean over the province of Barcelona's residents of those ages, a slightly larger share of whom are aged 75 and over than in Spain. Before the two profiles are averaged they are split at 75 with Madrid's ratio and put back together with Spain's mix of ages, as the Madrid profile already is. On the Barcelona profile alone, as in the analysis of the range below, drivers aged 75 and over were involved 2.06 times as often (1.6–2.6).

Why this assumption, and what could move it
  • It uses only kilometres measured by exact age; the second split, Madrid's kilometres per licence holder, also needs DGT's counts of licence holders, taken six years after the survey.
  • Car-licence holding falls about equally from 65–74 to 75 and over in the province of Madrid, the province of Barcelona and Spain (holders per resident at 75 and over against 65–74, DGT, 2024: men 0.72, 0.72, 0.71; women 0.28, 0.28, 0.26). That is consistent with the assumption but does not test it.
  • The Madrid survey has residents aged 65 and over driving much less, relative to those aged 45–64, than the Barcelona-area survey does. Each survey's profile carried to Spain's population gives 0.28 against 0.41 times as far per resident on working days; among each survey's own residents the figures are 0.29 and 0.40. Averaging the two profiles takes the middle of that disagreement.
  • Some evidence points lower: licence holding at 75 and over has risen since 2018, more than at 65–74, and surveys in other European countries show a gentler fall in driving after 75 (see the exposure study).
  • Some points higher: the Barcelona-area survey's weighting does not separate 75 and over within 65 and over, so its 65-and-over sample may hold too few people aged 75 and over; on the Barcelona profile, at the most this would raise the figure from 2.06 to about 2.28, a case included in the sensitivity range.
  • Also higher: applied to Spain's licence holders, the Madrid pattern implies that women aged 75 and over with a car licence drive 1.08 times as far as women aged 65–74 with one, while Spanish surveys of women's driving, though too uncertain to rule that out, point to less; less driving by these women would raise the figure.
  • Which way the figure errs is not known.

Across the assumptions. Four splits of the 65-and-over kilometres were tested:

  1. Madrid's split, used for the estimate.
  2. Madrid's kilometres per licence holder, applied to Spain's licence holders.
  3. An upper limit for men from the number of days they drive, with women driving as far per licence holder at 75 and over as at 65–74.
  4. Equal kilometres per licence holder at 65–74 and 75 and over.

Each split was combined with every alternative for the 65-and-over kilometres, and together they give a sensitivity range of 0.97 to 3.20 at 75 and over and 0.67 to 1.70 at 65–74. Of these alternatives, the region whose age profile stands in for Spain's (the province of Barcelona's, its four parts, Madrid's, or the province's carried per licence holder) was crossed with the age mixes for weekends and holidays and for the kilometres no part explains.

Trip distances, survey years, work driving, the older respondents' employment and age mix, and a weekend age mix on its own were varied one at a time, each with everything else as on the Barcelona profile with Madrid's split. A sensitivity range is not an interval: it carries no probability, and it is not a limit, because the choices varied one at a time are not combined with each other or with the crossed ones, which would move both ends.

The split with equal kilometres per licence holder stays in the range: women's driving does not rule it out, and the evidence against it for men comes from regional or dated surveys rather than a national measure of kilometres.

The three allocations of the unexplained kilometres reported above stay out of it, for the reasons given there. At 75 and over, across the profiles and splits, they would give 1.04–1.94 with the same kilometres per licence holder at every age, 1.62–4.63 with no driving at 65 and over, and 1.10–2.56 with DGT's kilometres by the owner's age.

With 120 drivers aged 75 and over, the Madrid split's 95% sampling intervals (0.8–1.8, 1.2–2.3 and 1.0–2.0) are wide: one includes the 45–64 rate, one lies above it and one ends so close to it that another set of resamples could put its lower end on either side. Two kinds of data would narrow this range. The EMEF's own records hold each respondent's exact age, so its kilometres at 65–74 and at 75 and over, tabulated by the Institut Metròpoli or the ATM, would replace the transfer of Madrid's pattern; a request for those tables has been prepared but not sent (the data request). A survey of driving on every day of the week across Spain, by exact age, would replace the assumptions about weekends and about the kilometres the working-day survey does not cover.

Drivers who drive few kilometres, at any age, tend to have more crashes per kilometre, partly because more of their driving is on streets with junctions and less on motorways, so a higher rate per kilometre at 75 and over would not by itself show that age makes driving less safe. These rates are averages over everyone of an age who drives and say nothing about any one driver.

They count involvement in injury crashes, whoever caused them; responsibility is not measured. The higher deaths once involved are counted and do not depend on any of these assumptions. Speed: the speed report and the adjusted ratio

These notes support Speed; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on speed as a severity factor. How police-recorded factors are read is set out under reading police crash records.

How the speed report and the crash records are combined

DGT's speed report, the Informe temático Factor Velocidad, leaves out Catalonia and the Basque Country, which keep their own crash records. It gives crashes and deaths with speed recorded by road type, but totals of all crashes only for all roads, interurban roads and urban streets. DGT's crash records for the same provinces reproduce those three totals exactly in every year from 2016 to 2023, and supply the totals for each interurban road type.

The report's interurban categories are matched to the road types of the crash records by name (autopistas to motorways, autovías to dual carriageways, the rest to other interurban roads); that match is checked only through the interurban total, not road type by road type.

The adjusted ratio and the road-type intervals

The adjusted ratio comes from a quasi-Poisson model of deaths per crash with road type and year. It brings the crude ratio of 3.41 down to 2.00; on a logarithmic scale, road type and year account for 43% of the crude ratio.

The model assumes one ratio for every road type. Letting the ratio differ by road type improves the fit by a likelihood-ratio statistic of 446 on 3 degrees of freedom and lowers the Pearson dispersion from 13.5 to 2.0: most of the extra variation the summary's interval allows for is the difference between road types. The road-type intervals are Poisson intervals widened by the square root of each road type's own dispersion across years (1.9 to 2.4).

Recorded factors: the break rule and the series it splits

These notes support Recorded factors; the methods document gives the full account, in its section on other recorded factors. The shares come from DGT's speed report, which counts for each year from 2014 to 2023 the injury crashes in Spain outside Catalonia and the Basque Country in which the police recorded each of five factors.

The break rule

Every year-to-year change in every series was tested against a fixed rule: a rise of more than 25% or a fall of more than 20% in a single year, or fewer than 200 crashes in either year, marks a break in comparability, and a series is compared only within the runs of years between its breaks. Of the 135 year-to-year changes, 33 are breaks or too small to test.

The threshold is a convention, and every change is published with its test so that another threshold can be applied. An unbroken run is not proof that recording stayed the same: the rule rules out only single-year jumps.

Where the series break

Outside the drug series, the only breaks by kind of road are those of urban alcohol (2016) and urban distraction (2016 and 2019). Every other series by kind of road runs unbroken from 2014 to 2023.

Drugs were recorded in at most 838 crashes a year. The count rose 12-fold from 2014 to 2018, then fell to 99 in 2020; every year-to-year change breaks the rule or has too few crashes to test.

The mix of roads and the all-roads fall in recorded speed

Recorded inappropriate speed fell 23% on interurban roads and 24% on urban streets from 2014 to 2023, but 31% on all roads, because the mix of crashes moved towards urban streets, where speed is rarely recorded: 40.6% of injury crashes were on interurban roads in 2014 and 34.2% in 2023. At the 2014 mix, the 2023 share on all roads would be 7.6% rather than 6.9%, a fall of 23% from 10.0%, close to the falls within each kind of road.

Crash circumstances in Spain: the regressions and their checks

These notes support Crash circumstances in Spain; its model card and the methods document, in its section on associations in DGT crash records, give the full account.

The records and the regressions

DGT's file has one row per injury crash, with counts of the people killed and injured, but no record of individual drivers, vehicles or people. Its fields are recorded too unevenly between provinces to train a predictive model (data sources and scope), but they can describe associations. Two logistic regressions are fitted, one for at least one death within 30 days and one for a death or a hospitalisation.

Their circumstances are those the police record: zone, road type, crash type, junction, lighting, weather, road surface, alignment, time of day, weekend (from 20:00 on Friday to the end of Sunday), number of vehicles and year. A missing value is kept as a level of its own, so no crash is dropped, and levels with fewer than 500 crashes are merged into their reference category. The intervals are 95% confidence intervals, with standard errors clustered by province.

Zone, road type and illustrative crash profiles

Zone and road type describe one location between them, so the chart of every odds ratio shows each against the other's reference, and the page's table adds the two together. The road type “other” mixes two kinds of crash: in 2024 DGT's records for the four Catalan provinces code urban streets as another kind of road (coding breaks), so its odds ratio, 1.74, rises to 2.40 without Catalonia's crashes.

Turned into probabilities for illustrative crashes in 2024, with every circumstance not named at its reference level, the odds ratios give a fitted probability of a death from 0.4% (urban street, side or front-side collision, daylight, two vehicles) to 12.0% (conventional road, head-on collision, daylight, two vehicles). As probabilities the fitted values improve little on the base rate (stability over time), so the profiles show the size of the odds ratios, not a forecast for any one crash.

The inverted junction flag

In 2023 and 2024 DGT's records for the four Catalan provinces code the junction flag the wrong way round (coding breaks), and the rest of each record shows it: only three of the 29,949 crashes they flag at a junction carry a junction type or a right-of-way flag, the fields that describe a junction, while 99.8% of the 17,756 they flag away from one carry either, as nearly every crash at a junction does in 2016–2022. The analysis reads the flag the other way round for these 47,705 crashes (5.5% of all).

The share of Catalan crashes at a junction is then 38% in 2023 and 37% in 2024, against 38% elsewhere in 2023–2024 and 38% to 42% a year in Catalonia in 2016–2022 (35% in 2021, when the records count a crash within 50 metres of a junction away from it).

Fitted on each period apart, crashes at a junction had 0.69 (0.65–0.72) times the odds of a death in 2016–2022 and 0.72 (0.65–0.79) in 2023–2024; outside Catalonia the later figure is 0.74 (0.66–0.83). Read instead from the junction type alone, the full model's junction odds ratio is 0.69 (0.65–0.73); with those crashes' junction left unrecorded, 0.70 (0.66–0.74); with the flag as published, 0.75 (0.69–0.81), pulled towards 1 by the inverted records.

How stable the associations are over time

Fitted on 2016–2022 without a year term and applied to the crashes of 2023–2024, the two regressions keep their ordering of crashes. Given one crash with the outcome and one without, the share of such pairs in which a regression gives the crash with the outcome the higher probability is its ROC-AUC, from 0.5 for chance to 1 for a perfect ranking: 0.80 for the regression for a death and 0.69 for a death or a hospitalisation.

Part of that ordering comes from how the form was filled in rather than from the crash: refitted with every level that records a missing value folded into its reference, the regression for a death scores 0.78, and those levels on their own 0.54. Across the wider set of fields the DGT microdata audit examines, which fields were left unrecorded scores 0.68 on its own, one reason the file is not used to train a predictive model. The Catalan severity model scores 0.74 on years it had not seen, but among crashes already selected for a death or serious injury; here the deaths are picked out among all injury crashes, of which 1.6% were fatal.

As probabilities, the fitted values improve little on giving every crash the training years' share of each outcome: their Brier score (mean squared error) is 4.3% lower for a death and 5.4% lower for a death or a hospitalisation.

Refitted one year at a time, the regression for a death gives 36 of its 108 yearly estimates for its 10 largest terms (each a crash-type, zone or road-type term) and its junction and wet-surface terms outside the full model's interval. Each year's estimate has its own sampling error, so some departures are expected: the full model's value lies outside the year's own interval for 15. Road type accounts for 21 of the 36; its odds ratios swing from year to year against the zone odds ratios, the two splitting one location contrast between them.

Tested against their yearly errors (Cochran's Q), none of these terms varies between years by more than chance; the least stable is the road type “other road” (p = 0.07). In the regression for a death or a hospitalisation, tested the same way, road type “other road” (p = 0.009) does vary: the level that DGT's records for the Catalan provinces fill with urban streets in 2024. The junction odds ratio lies between 0.63 and 0.79 in every year. With the Catalan junction flag as published, the junction term did vary (p = 0.005), at about 1 in 2023 and 2024 (coding breaks).

Missing values and regional recording

Some levels stand for a missing value, a field's “unknown” or “not specified” code. They stay in the regression but are left out of the charts and of the results in the text. One of them is among the strongest terms in the whole regression, and it sits almost entirely in one region. Road alignment “unknown”, with an odds ratio of 0.12, is recorded for 34.7% of crashes in Catalonia against 0.5% elsewhere: 95.7% of its 78,990 crashes are Catalan, although Catalonia has 24.9% of all crashes.

The odds ratio marks crashes recorded mostly by Catalonia's police forces: it describes recording practice. The other missing-value levels are almost all outside Catalonia (at most 0.07% Catalan).

A recording practice that goes with the outcome could distort the other coefficients through such a level, so the regression for a death was refitted without Catalonia's 217,966 crashes. All 32 other odds ratios stay inside the full model's interval. The largest moves are in zone and road type, which have to be read together: for the zone “urban crossing”, the odds ratio goes from 4.28 to 2.57.

The wet-surface odds ratio moves from 0.62 to 0.72 and the junction odds ratio from 0.69 to 0.70, both still below 1, while alignment “unknown” itself moves from 0.12 to 0.44 once most of its crashes are gone.

How the file's counts compare with DGT's national records

The Catalan file shares no record identifier with DGT's national crash records, so the two are compared by year and province (the file's traffic demarcations). Over 2016–2023, the file's fatal crashes equal DGT's crashes with a death within 24 hours in all 32 province-years, and its total count matches DGT's 24-hour count of crashes with a death or serious injury to within one crash in every province-year. Its fatal count is 67–95% of DGT's count of crashes with a death within 30 days. The file does not state its definition; the counts show that it is the 24-hour one.

Fields left blank more often in non-fatal crashes

The recording check, run on the crashes of 2010–2022, finds eight fields that are left blank or marked “not specified” much more often in non-fatal crashes than in fatal ones. The field for special lanes, for example, is blank in 7.7% of non-fatal crashes and 1.0% of fatal ones. The file gives no reason, but the blank itself carries information about the outcome, and none of these fields is used on the Catalonia page. Otherwise the file is consistent: it has no duplicated rows, and every severity label agrees with the deaths and injuries recorded on the same row.

Checks on the data

Before any analysis, 482 checks tie DGT's crash records, yearbook tables and driver census to DGT's published totals. On the crash records they check that each crash has one identifier and every code is in DGT's dictionary, and they compare:

  • Crashes and victims per year with the yearbook.
  • Crashes and deaths by province and by month with DGT's 2024 tables.
  • Deaths and vehicles by type with DGT's statistical tables.
  • The crashes and deaths of the speed report's provinces with that report.

The other checks compare the driver census and the yearbook's driver tables with DGT's published totals. All the checks pass, and the crash records match the yearbook exactly, year by year. The kilometre table by owner's age reconciles with the same release's published fleet to within 0.5%, the margin left by owners DGT could not classify. The full list of checks is published as a table.

Reproducing the results

Every result in the study is computed by the code in the repository from the raw published files. The code also holds a few values typed from documents, each cited where it is used:

  • Definitions, such as the public-holiday calendars that separate working days.
  • Figures read from archived files.
  • Two sets of measurements whose documents are not archived: the road-to-straight-line ratio and benchmarks of the EMEF's 2021 distance report, and the figures of the Fundació RACC's 2013 survey of older drivers.

The raw files are kept with their checksums and, where recorded, their download addresses, and one command sequence in the README rebuilds the checks, tables, models, figures and pages in order. Each page reads its numbers from the result tables, and the build stops if a table no longer supports a sentence that describes it. Automated tests rerun the checks on every change.

The repository also holds the implementation notes, with the module behind each method, the source register, with what each source is and its terms of use, the manifest of the raw files, with each file's checksum and, where recorded, its download address, and the data inventory.

The project was developed through a reproducible, source-driven workflow. The research questions, the choice of sources, the statistical design, the interpretation and the decision to publish each result are the author's, and so is responsibility for them. AI coding assistants were used during implementation, debugging, data-processing work and review.
