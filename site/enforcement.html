On the central estimates, removing drink- and drug-driving would save the most lives, about 582 a year, then every driver keeping to the limit, about 449, then ending distraction, about 266 on the police record. The first two overlap within their ranges, and distraction's figure is the least certain: it could be as high as 640. For both drink-driving and speeding, more enforcement goes with fewer crashes, but the evidence is more consistent for drink-driving: breath-test checkpoints, the most studied measure here (40 studies), cut alcohol-related crashes by 17% (12% to 21%) across the areas where they run, and work better the more often they run. Automatic speed cameras show larger effects, 41% to 47% fewer fatal or serious crashes in international reviews, but only on the stretches they cover. Phone bans have not measurably changed total deaths. So higher enforcement against drink- and drug-driving has the strongest case for cutting total deaths, with speed cameras close behind; for every lever 73% to 84% of the gain is on interurban roads.

The ceiling: each factor removed

Each lever is taken to its limit with its own model: the speed-law simulator with every driver above the limit slowing to it, and the alcohol and drugs and distraction models with no driver affected. The ranges come from each model's risk evidence; distraction's police-record figure is its low end, and a camera study puts its ceiling at 640. The three cannot be added: a crash with a drunk, speeding driver is avoided once, not twice. Removing all three together, if they act independently, avoids 1,017 deaths a year, 57% of the total; alcohol and speed often go together, so the true combined figure is if anything lower.

The three problems have different shapes. Drink-driving is rare on the road, 2.1% of drivers stopped at random, but each drunk driver is very dangerous, so a small group causes a large share of deaths. The Guardia Civil carried out 7.4 million breath tests in 2024 on the roads it polices, which leave out Cataluña, País Vasco and towns with their own police (DGT); 1% of its routine tests were positive. Drug tests are far rarer, 122,938, one for every 60 breath tests, although drugs without alcohol were found in 10% of killed drivers against 27% over the alcohol limit (INTCF). Speeding is widespread: 57% of cars on conventional roads are over 90 km/h and the fastest 15% go at 109 km/h or more, and under the Power Model the fastest carry most of the gain. Distraction is common, 9% of drivers are seen holding a device, and it is the hardest of the three to detect from outside the car.

How far enforcement reaches each

To save 100 lives a year, enforcement would have to remove about 17% (16% to 20%) of drink- and drug-driving, about 20% (14% to 31%) of speeding (that share of the drivers above the limit slowing to it), or about 38% (26% to 42%) of distracted driving on the police record; the ranges come from the risk evidence. The studies below are the best available on each lever. They measure what an enforcement measure did to crashes where it was tried, not how much of the behaviour it removed, and all but one are from outside Spain.

Speed. Automatic cameras show the largest effects, where they stand: fatal crashes fall by 47% (23% to 63%) near fixed cameras, mostly measured within half a kilometre to a kilometre of them, and crashes killing or seriously injuring someone by 41% (22% to 55%) on section-controlled stretches (TØI, ch. 8.2). Two Spanish studies from Barcelona (Pérez et al. 2007; Novoa et al. 2010) are among those pooled; the later found crashes on the city's ring roads fell by 30% (20% to 38%), with no change on its arterial streets. Spain already has fixed and section cameras: 63% of DGT's 5.4 million fines in 2024 were for speed, issued by the Guardia Civil, fixed and section cameras and helicopters (DGT). How much more a new camera would save depends on how many deaths happen on the stretches it covers, which DGT's public data do not show. More hours of speed checks go with fewer crashes, more so for fatal crashes, in TØI's dose-response curves, but the studies vary widely: pooled, more of the enforcement already in place changed the number killed or seriously injured by −9% (−34% to +26%).

Alcohol and drugs. Breath-test checkpoints are the most studied measure on this page: across 40 studies, alcohol-related crashes fell by 17% (12% to 21%) in the areas where they ran, and all crashes by 9% (7% to 12%) in the studies with a control group. More is better: the effect grows with how often checkpoints run, testing every driver stopped works better than testing only on suspicion, and of 12 studies of how much enforcement there was, most of them American, all but one found more enforcement went with fewer crashes (TØI, ch. 8.7). How much more Spain would gain from its 7.4 million tests a year has not been measured; Norway's planners assume that three times as many random tests would cut fatal crashes by 3%, an assumption rather than a measurement. Patrols stopping only the drivers the police suspect showed no clear effect, −3% (−9% to +4%) in fatal crashes. For drugs no study has measured what random testing does to crashes; a curve fitted to two Norwegian roadside surveys predicts that a 60% higher chance of being caught would cut drug-impaired driving by 45%.

Distraction. Bans on handheld phones changed crashes by −2% (−4% to 0%), not a significant change, and bans on texting went with +5% (+2% to +8%). Comprehensive handheld bans went with 7% (3% to 10%) fewer driver deaths, also where the police could enforce them on their own, but total deaths changed by −2% (−6% to +1%), and after texting bans the police could enforce on their own by −3% (−5% to 0%): both intervals include no change. Police enforcement of a ban has been shown to reduce handheld phone use, but its effect on crashes varies between studies and is uncertain (TØI, ch. 8.14). Spain already bans using a phone at the wheel unless hands-free (Reglamento General de Circulación, art. 18.2).

For every lever 73% to 84% of the gain is on interurban roads, which carry 72% of deaths. For speed the prize there is on conventional roads: everyone keeping to 90 km/h on them saves 280 of the 335 interurban lives full compliance saves. Cameras reach it only on the stretches they cover: section control along whole stretches, a fixed camera about a kilometre. The speed figure leaves out the 84 deaths a year on other interurban roads, where no speeds were measured. In towns alcohol and drugs lead on the central estimates, 156 lives against 114 for speed and 42 for distraction. But the urban speed figure takes every urban death to be on a street at 50 km/h, the smaller effect, because DGT does not say on which streets they happen: if 32% or more were on streets at 30 km/h, speed would lead in towns too. At the low end of the evidence the urban speed effect is 0.

Conclusion

Higher enforcement against drink- and drug-driving has the strongest case for cutting total deaths. It is the largest prize on the central estimates, about 582 deaths a year, and saving 100 takes removing about 17% of it; it is concentrated in a few drivers, 2.1% of those stopped at random; and the evidence that more enforcement brings fewer crashes is the most consistent, from checkpoints that work across the areas where they run and work better the more often they run. Drug testing is the thinnest part of it, one test for every 60 breath tests although drugs without alcohol were found in 10% of killed drivers, but no study has measured what more of it would do to crashes. Speed is close behind: 449 a year (298 to 616), 20% of speeding to save 100, and automatic cameras have the largest effects of any measure here, 41% to 47% fewer fatal or serious crashes near them, but only on the stretches they cover, so the gain depends on placing them where people die: interurban and above all conventional roads. Distraction is the least certain on both counts: its toll could be anywhere from about 237 to 640 deaths a year, and phone bans have not measurably changed total deaths. For all three, most of the gain is on interurban roads; in towns, on the central estimates, it is drink- and drug-driving.

Limits. Each lever uses its own model, so the comparison inherits each one's limits: the speed model applies international dose-response evidence to measured Spanish speeds on autopistas, autovías and conventional roads; the alcohol, drug and distraction models apply measured risks to the police record of fatal crashes. Urban speed is a range because DGT does not split urban deaths by the limit of the street. The combined figure assumes the factors act independently. Removing a share of a factor is not the same as an enforcement measure: the studies above report how crashes changed where a measure was tried, mostly outside Spain, not how much of the factor it removed, so the two are set side by side rather than combined.
