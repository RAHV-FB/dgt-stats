Removing drink- and drug-driving would save the most lives, about 582 a year, then every driver keeping to the limit, about 449, then ending distracted driving, about 266 on the police record. But the largest prize is not where more enforcement is surest to pay. The best-measured gains are on speed: automatic cameras cut fatal crashes near them by 47% (23% to 63%) and crashes killing or seriously injuring someone on section-controlled stretches by 41% (22% to 55%), and in Spain cut crashes on Barcelona's ring roads by 30% (20% to 38%). Spain already breath-tests 6.7 million drivers a year, and the studies suggest more of the same buys little, so the alcohol and drug prize lies in reaching the few drivers who cause it. Enforcement against distraction has the weakest evidence of the three. For every lever most of the gain is on interurban roads; for speed, on conventional roads.

The ceiling: each factor removed

Each lever is taken to its limit with its own model: the speed-law simulator with every driver above the limit slowing to it, and the alcohol and drugs and distraction models with no driver affected. The ranges come from each model's risk evidence. The three cannot be added: a crash with a drunk, speeding driver is avoided once, not twice. Removing all three together, if they act independently, avoids 1,017 deaths a year, 57% of the total; alcohol and speed often go together, so the true combined figure is if anything lower.

The three problems have different shapes, and the shape decides what enforcement can do. Drink-driving is rare on the road, 2.1% of drivers, but each drunk driver is very dangerous, so a small group causes a large share of deaths. The Guardia Civil already breath-tests about 6.7 million drivers a year, of whom 1.2% are positive; what decides the gain is whether the tests reach the few drivers who drink heavily, at the times they drive. Speeding is the opposite: 57% of cars on conventional roads are over the limit, each by a few km/h, so the gain comes from moving everyone a little, which automatic cameras can do at scale. Distraction sits between them: 9% of drivers are seen holding a device, distraction of some kind is common, and it is the hardest of the three to detect from outside the car.

How far enforcement reaches each

To save 100 lives a year, enforcement would have to remove about 17% (16% to 20%) of drink- and drug-driving, about 20% (14% to 31%) of speeding (that share of the drivers above the limit slowing to it), or about 38% (26% to 38%) of distracted driving; the ranges come from the risk evidence. The studies below are the best available on each lever. They measure what an enforcement measure did to crashes where it was tried, not how much of the behaviour it removed, and all but one are from outside Spain.

Speed. Automatic cameras have the largest and most often replicated effects: fatal crashes near fixed cameras fall by 47% (23% to 63%), and crashes killing or seriously injuring someone on section-controlled stretches by 41% (22% to 55%). The one Spanish evaluation found crashes on Barcelona's ring roads fell by 30% (20% to 38%), with no change on the city's arterial streets, so the gain is on fast roads. It stays where the cameras are: the national gain depends on how many of the deaths happen on the roads they cover. Simply more of the enforcement already in place is the least certain result, a change of −9% (−34% to +26%) in people killed or seriously injured.

Alcohol and drugs. Breath-test checkpoints cut alcohol-related crashes by 17% (12% to 21%) where they were brought in. Spain has them: the Guardia Civil carried out 6.7 million tests in 2023, 1.2% of them positive. On doing more of the same the evidence points to small gains: patrols stopping the drivers the police suspect show −3% (−9% to +4%) in fatal crashes, and Norway's road safety planners assume three times as many random breath tests would cut fatal crashes by 3%. For drugs no study has measured what random testing does to crashes; in Norway a 60% higher chance of being caught went with 45% less drug-impaired driving. The prize is large because a few drivers, 2.1% of those stopped at random, are very dangerous; reaching them, at the times and places they drive, matters more than the number of tests. How far better targeting would go has not been measured.

Distraction. Bans on handheld phones cut crashes by 2% (0% to 4%); after bans on texting crashes changed by +5% (+2% to +8%). Where the police could enforce a full handheld ban on its own, driver deaths fell by 7% (3% to 10%), but all deaths changed by −2% (−6% to +1%), an interval that includes no change. A review of 32 studies of police enforcement of phone bans found it may cut phone use and fatal crashes, but with results that vary and are uncertain (TØI, ch. 8.14). Spain already bans using a phone at the wheel unless hands-free (Reglamento General de Circulación, art. 18.2).

For every lever most of the gain is on interurban roads, which carry 72% of deaths. Within them, for speed, conventional roads lead: everyone keeping to 90 km/h there saves 280 of the 335 interurban lives full speed compliance saves, so that is where cameras would pay most. In towns alcohol and drugs lead on the central estimates, 156 lives against 114 for speed and 42 for distraction. The urban speed figure is the least certain: it is 0 at the low end, because the evidence for town streets includes no effect, and 245 if every urban death were on a street at 30 km/h; DGT does not say which streets they happen on.

Conclusion

The largest prize is drink- and drug-driving, about 582 deaths a year if it were removed, against 449 for speeding and 266 for distraction on the police record. Saving 100 lives a year takes removing about 17% of drink- and drug-driving, 20% of speeding or 38% of distraction. Higher enforcement is surest to save lives on speed: automatic cameras cut fatal and serious crashes by 41% to 47% where they stand, including on fast roads in Spain, and their reach grows with every road they cover, above all conventional interurban roads. For alcohol and drugs Spain already runs about 6.7 million breath tests a year, and the evidence says more of the same buys little; the remaining gain depends on reaching the few heavy drinkers and drug users who cause most of those deaths, which no study here measures. Enforcement against distraction has the weakest evidence on both counts: the smallest recorded prize and bans that changed total deaths by −2% (−6% to +1%).

Limits. Each lever uses its own model, so the comparison inherits each one's limits: the speed model applies international dose-response evidence to measured Spanish speeds; the alcohol, drug and distraction models apply measured risks to the police record of fatal crashes. Urban speed is a range because DGT does not split urban deaths by the limit of the street. The combined figure assumes the factors act independently. Removing a share of a factor is not the same as an enforcement measure: how much of each factor a given measure removes is what the studies above estimate, on roads outside Spain.
