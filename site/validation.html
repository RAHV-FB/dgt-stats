Crash severity

External validation of the severity models

The Catalan severity model, the one behind the calculator, has been tested only within Catalonia: no other source records its inputs. On later years and on provinces left out of its fitting it ranked crashes better than a table of fatal shares by road and crash type, but its estimates of the fatal share were off for Barcelona, Girona and Tarragona when each was left out of its fitting, and for the city of Barcelona. A harmonised version of the original Catalan model (retired) ranked crashes elsewhere in Spain nearly as well as a model fitted there. Because Catalonia's serious crashes differ from the rest of Spain's in the mix of crash types and in how several fields are recorded, national use of the models is not established.

The Catalan severity model: ranking holds, estimates of the fatal share miss in several provinces

Every test on this page scores crashes that played no part in fitting the model. Ranking is measured by the ROC-AUC: given one fatal and one non-fatal crash, the share of pairs in which the fatal one gets the higher estimate, from 0.5 for chance to 1 for a perfect ranking. Each score is set beside a reference, the same kind of model fitted and cross-validated within the test population, which shows how well that population's crashes can be ranked at all. A small test population gives a weak reference, which a model fitted elsewhere on many more crashes can beat.

The Catalan severity model is the one behind the calculator (Severity model and calculator). It has been tested only within the Catalan file, on crashes on the roads a reader can choose. Each year of 2016–2023 was predicted by a model whose settings (penalty, form, the rule for roads through towns and whether each province has a starting level of its own) were chosen on the two years before it and whose coefficients were fitted on all earlier years, so no choice saw the year it predicts. Over the 11,611 crashes this gives a ROC-AUC of 0.74 (95% interval 0.73–0.75), against 0.71 for a table of fatal shares by road and crash type, and a mean estimate of 12.4% where 12.3% were fatal. On 2023 alone it scores 0.76, against 0.72 for the same model fitted and cross-validated on that year's 1,527 crashes. By province and kind of road the estimates were less close: the mean estimate fell outside the 95% interval of the observed share on urban streets in the province of Barcelona, 7.54% estimated and 6.70% observed (6.02–7.45%), on interurban roads in the province of Girona, 23.2% estimated and 26.8% observed (23.8–30.1%) and on interurban roads in the province of Tarragona, 26.7% estimated and 31.0% observed (27.8–34.4%).

With each province left out of the fitting in turn, and the settings chosen on the other three, it scores between 0.68 (Barcelona) and 0.77 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see: in every province within 0.05 of the same model fitted there, and above the table. Its estimates of the fatal share carry over less well. They were too high for Barcelona (11.2% estimated against 9.5% observed, 9.1% to 10.0%), too low for Girona (14.3% estimated against 17.2% observed, 15.9% to 18.5%) and too low for Tarragona (14.3% estimated against 17.0% observed, 15.7% to 18.3%); only for Lleida (16.4% estimated against 17.6% observed, 16.1% to 19.2%) did the estimate lie inside the observed interval. Fitted on the rest of Catalonia, with a starting level for each province and kind of road as in the published model (refitted without the city's crashes, though the choice to have them was made on years that include them), it ranks Barcelona city's crashes at 0.66, against 0.68 for the same model fitted in the city, but it estimates a fatal share of 5.9% where 10.2% were fatal. Once the city's earlier crashes are in the fitting, its estimates for the city's streets are close (8.1% against 8.5%).

No other source records its inputs: DGT's national records have no field for the road's owning network or the posted limit, and their road-type coding disagrees with the Catalan file's on the crashes both hold. The Catalan severity model therefore has no test outside Catalonia. The tests that follow are of other models.

Spain outside Catalonia: a version of the original model holds its ranking

DGT's records of crashes outside Catalonia are kept separately from the Catalan file, so they give a test on a source no Catalan model has seen. The model tested is the harmonised version of the original Catalan model (retired): the same method restricted to the eleven variables both sources record in the same way, none of them the road's owner, and fitted on the Catalan file alone. It serves only to measure transfer; it is not the published model. The outcome means the same in both sources: the Catalan file's fatal crashes match DGT's crashes with a death within 24 hours in every province and year both cover (Catalonia).

On the 67,971 crashes with a death or serious injury that DGT recorded elsewhere in Spain, the harmonised version has a ROC-AUC of 0.71 (95% interval 0.71–0.72), against 0.72 for a model of the same kind fitted on DGT's records outside Catalonia. Taken together, its estimates are close to calibrated, but not province by province: in 23 of the 46 provinces shown below, the observed fatal share differs from the mean estimate by more than 3 percentage points (19 higher, four lower; largest gap 15.7 points). Fatal crashes are commoner in the test (15.4%) than in the Catalan records it was fitted on (12.6%); its mean estimate, 14.5%, falls between the two. Its calibration slope, 1 when the estimates spread as widely as the outcomes, is 0.92: the estimates are a little more extreme than the outcomes warrant.

Across the 46 provinces with at least 30 fatal and 30 non-fatal crashes, the ROC-AUC runs from 0.61 (Cáceres) to 0.78 (Zaragoza), with a median of 0.68. These scores have no intervals, and the smallest provinces hold only 70 fatal crashes, so the order of provinces with similar scores means little.

Variables of the harmonised version

The eleven variables are year, month, day of the week, time of day, urban or interurban zone, crash type, lighting, weather, road surface, junction and number of vehicles. Road type was left out because its coding disagrees on the crashes both sources hold, and the speed limit, the types of vehicle involved and the location are not available in a comparable form. Averaged over the variables used, values recorded as not specified make up at most 0.7% of any source's records, although weather is unspecified in 6.1% of serious and fatal crashes outside Catalonia.

Catalonia's serious crashes differ from Spain's in type and in recording

A model can rank well in a population whose crashes look quite different from its own, so a successful transfer says nothing about how alike the populations are. DGT's records for 2016–2024 compare them on the same fields and the same outcome definitions, although each police force fills the fields in its own way. A death or serious injury occurs in a smaller share of injury crashes in Catalonia than elsewhere in Spain (6.8% against 10.3%), and fewer of those crashes are fatal within 24 hours (12.2% against 15.4%). Barcelona city lies further from the national pattern, at 2.7% and 9.1%. The lower severe share comes from the denominator: per 100,000 residents a year, Catalonia's records hold 314 injury crashes against 184 elsewhere, but about as many crashes with a death or serious injury (21.3 against 19.0). How completely each force records slight-injury crashes is part of that difference, and these data cannot separate it from real differences (how often crashes happen and how deadly they are).

Among crashes with a death or serious injury, four of the eleven variables compared differ by more than a limit set in advance: road alignment, crash type, weather and road surface. The largest gaps are in how crashes are recorded. DGT's code for an unknown road alignment covers 14.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 0.4% elsewhere, and weather is unspecified in 6.1% outside Catalonia but almost never inside it. Fewer Catalan crashes are run-off-road crashes (13.4% against 25.8%), and crash types too are coded by different police forces. Road type does not differ beyond the limit: conventional roads hold 35.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 36.1% elsewhere, counting every conventional road together, because DGT's records for Catalonia code them as dual carriageways until 2020 and as single carriageways from 2021 (coding breaks). Barcelona city's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's far more, on seven variables, led by road alignment, road type and urban or interurban zone.

The original Catalan model (retired), tested within Catalonia

Before the Catalan severity model, the project's Catalan model was gradient-boosted trees on the file's circumstance fields. Its strongest predictor was the road's owner, a field that turned out to record how a crash was documented rather than the road: a blank owner is far commoner on fatal records. That model was retired, and its tests are kept here as a record (model review); their scores include whatever the artefact contributed. For these tests the model is used without its province variable, so that a province left out of fitting is new to it. On the last year of the Catalan file (2023), held back from fitting and from every choice of settings, it scores 0.79, against 0.70 for a model fitted and cross-validated on the 1,732 crashes of that year alone. With each of the four provinces left out of fitting in turn, it scores between 0.69 (Barcelona) and 0.79 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see. That is better than the province's own model in Girona, Lleida and Tarragona, whose own records are few, and 0.03 below it in the province of Barcelona, which holds most of the crashes.

Barcelona city: the original model's ranking carries over, its estimates do not

Barcelona city is the hardest test inside Catalonia. Fitted on the rest of Catalonia, the original model ranks the city's crashes at 0.64, against 0.66 for a model fitted in Barcelona; the 95% interval of the difference, −0.05 to 0.02, includes zero. Both scores are well below the 0.76 that a model reaches when fitted and tested in the rest of Catalonia. That fall comes from the kind of crashes and the number available to fit on: at the same fitting size, Barcelona's crashes are harder to rank than the rest of Catalonia's mix of interurban and urban crashes, but not harder than its urban crashes (difference −0.04, 95% interval −0.08 to 0.00).

The ranking carries over to the city, but the probabilities do not: the original model's calibration slope there is 0.44, far from 1, so in Barcelona its scores order crashes without estimating their fatal share. The Guàrdia Urbana's own records for 2025, a second source for the city, hold 252 crashes defined compatibly with the Catalan file, only 11 of them fatal: too few for a ranking test. Their fatal share, 4.4% (95% interval 2.5–7.6%), is below the city's share in the Catalan file (10.5% over 2010–2023, 9.5% over 2020–2023). The original model restricted to Barcelona's variables, whose estimate for the city in the Catalan file is close (10.0%), predicts 10.0% for these crashes. The comparison mixes a change of year and of recording source with any error of the model, so it is weak evidence about transfer.

Within the city, the Barcelona person-severity model keeps its ranking when each of the ten districts is left out of fitting in turn: 0.86 on the district it did not see, the same to two decimals as in ordinary cross-validation on the same records. The model is kept for research only. It uses the crash type as recorded, including a term the Guàrdia Urbana uses almost only for serious crashes (Barcelona), so part of its ranking may come from how serious crashes are coded.

“Ranking held” means that the model ranks above chance with 95% confidence and, where a model fitted within the test population gives a reference, scores no more than 0.05 below it. It says nothing about whether the model's estimates of the fatal share were right, which the sections above test separately. The last column is “yes” only if no variable's distribution differs from Spain's by more than a Jensen–Shannon divergence of 0.02, a measure that is 0 for identical distributions. Both limits were set before any result was read. “Not run”: no second source holds the model's variables or the crashes it would need. “Too few cases”: Barcelona's own records for 2025 hold 11 fatal crashes. The test that fits the original model's specification on Barcelona city's crashes alone and scores the rest of Catalonia tests a model of the city, not a Catalan model, so no column counts it; it falls 0.18 short of its reference (detailed results below).
