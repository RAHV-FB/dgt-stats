Models

External validation of the severity models

Whether the severity models still rank crashes well when they are applied to a later year, to places left out of training and to another source's records.

This page asks whether a model trained in one place still works somewhere else. The main test took the Catalonia crash-severity model, kept only the ten variables that DGT's national crash records code in the same way, trained it on the Catalan file and applied it to the 67,971 crashes with a death or serious injury that DGT recorded elsewhere in Spain. Its ROC-AUC, which measures how well it ranks fatal crashes above the others on a scale from 0.5 (chance) to 1 (perfect), was almost the same as that of a model trained directly on those DGT records. That does not establish that the Catalonia model can be used nationally. The full model uses variables DGT does not record, Catalonia's serious crashes differ from the rest of Spain's in road type, crash type and recording practice, and in Barcelona city the model's probabilities did not carry over even where its ranking did.

Every test on this page scores crashes that played no part in training and sets the result beside a reference: a model of the same kind trained within the test population and cross-validated there. The reference shows how well that population's crashes can be ranked at all, and the difference between the two scores is what is lost by using a model trained elsewhere. A small test population gives a weak reference, which a model trained elsewhere on many more crashes can beat.

Tested on DGT records outside Catalonia

DGT's records of crashes outside Catalonia are kept separately from the Catalan file, so they give a test on a source the model has never seen. The test uses the harmonised Catalonia model: the Catalonia crash-severity model restricted to the ten variables both sources record in the same way, trained on the Catalan file alone. The outcome means the same in both: the Catalan file's fatal crashes match DGT's crashes with a death within 24 hours in every province and year both cover (Catalonia).

On these crashes the Catalonia-trained model has a ROC-AUC of 0.708 (95% interval 0.702–0.712), against 0.712 for a model of the same kind trained on DGT's records outside Catalonia, a difference of −0.004. Its probabilities are close to calibrated. Fatal crashes are commoner in the test (15.4%) than in the Catalan training records (12.6%); the model's mean prediction, 14.2%, falls between the two, and its calibration slope is 1.10, a little less extreme than the outcomes warrant.

Across the 46 provinces with at least 30 fatal and 30 non-fatal crashes, the ROC-AUC runs from 0.60 (León) to 0.79 (Zaragoza), with a median of 0.68.

Variables of the harmonised Catalonia model

The ten variables are year, month, day of the week, time of day, urban or interurban zone, crash type, lighting, weather, road surface and number of vehicles. Road type and junction were left out because their codings disagree on the crashes both sources hold, and the speed limit, the types of vehicle involved and the location are not available in a comparable form. Averaged over the variables used, values recorded as not specified make up at most 0.8% of any source's records, although weather is unspecified in 6.1% of serious and fatal crashes outside Catalonia.

Tested on a later year and on provinces left out

Within Catalonia the Catalonia crash-severity model keeps its ranking. For these tests it is used without its province variable, so that a province left out of training is new to it. On the last year of the Catalan file (2023), held back from training and from every choice of settings, it scores 0.79, against 0.70 for a model trained and cross-validated on the 1,732 crashes of that year alone (a different benchmark from the table on the models page, which happens to score 0.70 too). With each of the four provinces left out of training in turn, it scores between 0.69 (Barcelona) and 0.79 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see. That is better than the province's own model in Girona, Lleida and Tarragona, whose own records are few, and 0.03 below it in the province of Barcelona, which holds most of the crashes.

Tested on Barcelona city

Barcelona city is the hardest test inside Catalonia. Trained on the rest of Catalonia, the Catalonia crash-severity model ranks the city's crashes at 0.642, against 0.656 for a model trained in Barcelona: a difference of −0.015 (95% interval −0.054 to 0.021), too small to separate from zero. Both scores are well below the 0.764 that a model reaches when trained and tested in the rest of Catalonia, mainly because Barcelona's crashes are harder to rank and fewer of them are available to train a model.

The ranking carries over to the city, but the probabilities do not: the calibration slope there is 0.44, far from 1, so in Barcelona the model's scores order crashes without estimating their fatal share. The Guàrdia Urbana's own records for 2025, a second source for the city, hold 252 crashes defined compatibly with the Catalan file, only 11 of them fatal: too few for a ranking test. For these crashes the Catalonia model restricted to Barcelona's variables predicts a fatal share of 10.0%, against 4.4% observed (95% interval 2.5–7.6%), so it overstates the fatal share in the city.

Within the city, the Barcelona person-severity model keeps its ranking when each of the ten districts is left out of training in turn: 0.864 on the district it did not see, against 0.855 in ordinary cross-validation on the same records.

How the crash populations differ

A model can rank well in a population whose crashes look quite different from its own, so a successful transfer says nothing about how alike the populations are. DGT's records for 2016–2024, which use the same definitions in every province, show that they differ. A death or serious injury occurs in a smaller share of injury crashes in Catalonia than elsewhere in Spain (6.8% against 10.3%), and fewer of those crashes are fatal within 24 hours (12.2% against 15.4%). Barcelona city lies further from the national pattern, at 2.7% and 9.1%.

Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes also happen more often on dual carriageways (27.2% against 10.7% elsewhere in Spain) and less often on conventional roads (14.1% against 34.0%), and fewer are run-off-road crashes (13.4% against 25.8%). Five of the eleven variables compared differ by more than a limit set in advance: road alignment, road type, crash type, weather and road surface. Part of the difference is recording practice, which the data cannot separate from real differences in the crashes: DGT's code for an unknown road alignment covers 14.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 0.4% elsewhere, and weather is unspecified in 6.1% outside Catalonia but almost never inside it. Barcelona city's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's far more, on seven variables, led by road alignment, road type and urban or interurban zone.

What the tests support

The tests support ranking serious and fatal crashes with the Catalonia crash-severity model within Catalonia, and show that a version restricted to the variables DGT records alike ranks serious crashes elsewhere in Spain about as well as a model trained there, losing only 0.004 of ROC-AUC. The full Catalonia crash-severity model and the Barcelona person-severity model use variables or records that no second source holds, so both are tested only within their own files. The model's probabilities did not carry over to Barcelona city, and Catalonia's serious crashes differ materially from Spain's on five of the eleven variables compared. No model has been shown to be fit for use across Spain, so national use of the models is not established.
