Models

External validation of the severity models

Tests of the severity models on crashes from other years, places and data sources, and how Catalonia's and Barcelona's crashes compare with those of the rest of Spain.

The Catalonia severity model, restricted to the ten variables that DGT's national crash records code in the same way, ranks serious and fatal crashes elsewhere in Spain by their probability of being fatal almost as well as a model trained on those records. On the 67,971 such crashes recorded outside Catalonia, 10,457 of them fatal within 24 hours, this harmonised Catalonia model has a ROC-AUC of 0.708 (0.5 is chance and 1 a perfect ranking), against 0.712 for the model trained on DGT's records. Within Catalonia, the Catalonia severity model itself keeps its ranking in a later year and in each province left out of training. Trained on the rest of Catalonia, it scores 0.642 in Barcelona city, against 0.656 for a model trained on the city's own crashes: the city's crashes are harder to rank than the rest of Catalonia's, and applying the model there costs no measurable ranking. Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's in road type, crash type and recording practice, so national use of the models is not established.

Every test scores crashes that played no part in training and sets the score beside a reference: a model of the same kind trained within the test population and cross-validated there. The reference shows how much ranking that population allows; the difference between the scores measures what is lost by applying a model outside the population it learned from. A small test population gives a weak reference, which a model trained elsewhere on many more crashes can beat.

Validation on DGT records outside Catalonia

DGT's records of crashes outside Catalonia are kept separately from the Catalan file, so they provide an external test. It uses the harmonised Catalonia model: the Catalonia severity model restricted to the ten variables both sources record in the same way, trained on the Catalan file alone, with no Catalan crash in the test. The outcome means the same in both sources: the Catalan file's counts match DGT's 24-hour counts in every province and year both cover (Catalonia).

On these crashes the Catalonia-trained model has a ROC-AUC of 0.708 (95% interval 0.702–0.712), against 0.712 for a model of the same kind trained on DGT's records outside Catalonia, a difference of −0.004. Fatal crashes are commoner in the test (15.4%) than in the Catalan training records (12.6%). The model's mean prediction, 14.2%, falls between the two, and its calibration slope of 1.10 shows probabilities close to calibrated, slightly less extreme than the outcomes warrant.

The result holds when the test is varied: the model scores 0.701 when trained only on the Catalan years before DGT's records begin (2010–2015), and 0.706 when its training crashes are reweighted to the national mix of zone and crash type. The same Catalan crashes of 2016–2023 score 0.697 from the Catalan file and 0.696 from DGT's records, so the source of the record makes almost no difference.

Across the 46 provinces with at least 30 fatal and 30 non-fatal crashes, the ROC-AUC runs from 0.60 (León) to 0.79 (Zaragoza), with a median of 0.68.

Results by province outside Catalonia
Variables of the harmonised Catalonia model

The ten variables are year, month, day of the week, time of day, urban or interurban zone, crash type, lighting, weather, road surface and number of vehicles. Road type and junction were left out because their codings disagree on the crashes both sources hold, and the speed limit, the types of vehicle involved and the location are not available in a comparable form. Averaged over the variables used, values recorded as not specified make up at most 0.8% of any source's records, although weather is unspecified in 6.1% of serious and fatal crashes outside Catalonia.

Validation within Catalonia

Tested without its province variable, so that a province left out of training is new to it, the Catalonia severity model keeps its ranking inside Catalonia. On the final year of the Catalan file (2023), held back from training and model choice, it scores 0.79, against 0.70 for a model trained and cross-validated on the 1,732 crashes of that year alone (a different benchmark from the descriptive table on Severity models, which also scores 0.70). With each of the four provinces left out of training in turn, it scores between 0.69 (Barcelona) and 0.79 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see: above the province's own model in Girona, Lleida and Tarragona, whose own records are few, and 0.03 below it in the province of Barcelona, which holds most of the crashes.

Validation in Barcelona

Barcelona city is the hardest test inside Catalonia. Trained on the rest of Catalonia, the Catalonia severity model ranks the city's crashes at 0.642, against 0.656 for a model trained in Barcelona (a difference of −0.015) and 0.764 for a model trained and tested in the rest of Catalonia. The fall of 0.122 can be traced in three steps. Cutting a rest-of-Catalonia model's training set to the 2,543 crashes from which Barcelona's own model learns lowers its score by 0.054. At that equal size, Barcelona's crashes are ranked 0.053 less well than the rest of Catalonia's (95% interval 0.023–0.086): they are harder to rank. The last 0.015 separates Barcelona's own model from the one trained on 21,299 crashes elsewhere: the loss from applying a model outside the population it learned from, net of the gain from its larger training set. Its interval (−0.021 to 0.054) includes zero.

The difficulty is shared by urban crashes: at the same training size, the rest of Catalonia's urban crashes are ranked 0.039 less well than Barcelona's. Training the model on those urban crashes alone lowers its score in Barcelona by 0.036. The Catalonia-trained model's probabilities do not carry over to the city (calibration slope 0.44, where 1 is calibrated), so there its scores order crashes without estimating their fatal share. In the reverse direction, a model trained on Barcelona alone ranks the rest of Catalonia poorly (0.581, against 0.764).

The Guàrdia Urbana's own records for 2025 hold 252 crashes defined compatibly with the Catalan file, only 11 of them fatal: too few for a ranking test. For these crashes the Catalonia model restricted to Barcelona's variables predicts a fatal share of 10.0%, against 4.4% observed (95% interval 2.5–7.6%), so it overstates the fatal share in the city.

Barcelona's own crash records

Restricting the Catalonia severity model to the variables Barcelona records costs 0.045 of ROC-AUC within Barcelona (95% interval 0.011–0.079). On the city's crashes in the Catalan file, the model restricted to Barcelona's variables scores 0.569, against 0.612 for a model trained in Barcelona on the same variables. The crashes taken from the Guàrdia Urbana's records are those with a death within 24 hours or a hospital stay of more than 24 hours, compatible with the definition of the Catalan file. A ranking test needs at least 30 fatal and 30 non-fatal crashes, so no ROC-AUC is reported for them.

Within the city, the Barcelona person-severity model keeps its ranking when each of the ten districts is left out of training in turn: 0.864 on the district it did not see, against 0.855 in ordinary cross-validation on the same records.

Population differences

In DGT's records, which use the same definitions in every province, a death or serious injury occurs in a smaller share of injury crashes in Catalonia than elsewhere in Spain (6.8% against 10.3%), and fewer of those crashes are fatal within 24 hours (12.2% against 15.4%). Barcelona city lies further from the national pattern, at 2.7% and 9.1%.

Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes also occur more often on dual carriageways (27.2% against 10.7% elsewhere in Spain) and less often on conventional roads (14.1% against 34.0%), and fewer are run-off-road crashes (13.4% against 25.8%). Five of the eleven variables compared differ by more than a limit fixed in advance: road alignment, road type, crash type, weather and road surface. Part of the difference lies in recording practice, which the data cannot separate from differences in the crashes themselves: DGT's code for an unknown road alignment covers 14.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 0.4% elsewhere, and weather is unspecified in 6.1% outside Catalonia but almost never inside it. Barcelona city's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's far more, on seven variables, led by road alignment, road type and urban or interurban zone.

Scope of generalisation

A successful external test shows that the associations a model has learned order cases in much the same way in a population it has not seen, recorded by another body under its own procedures. The test on DGT's records meets that standard: the two sources are kept separately and define the outcome in the same way, and the harmonised Catalonia model loses only 0.004 of ROC-AUC against a model trained on the Spanish records.

Such a test leaves three questions open. It does not show that the training population resembles the population tested: Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes differ materially from Spain's on five of the eleven variables compared, and a model can rank well in a population whose composition differs from its own. It does not make the associations causal: a variable can rank fatal outcomes well because it travels with something the records do not hold (see Severity models). And it covers only the ten variables that both sources record alike. The Catalonia severity model itself and the Barcelona person-severity model rest on variables or records that no second source holds, so both are validated only within their own files.

The evidence therefore supports using the Catalonia severity model to rank serious and fatal crashes within Catalonia. No model has passed every kind of test, from held-out records to a comparison of its training crashes with Spain's, and none has been shown to be fit for use across Spain.
