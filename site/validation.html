Crash severity

External validation of the severity models

Whether the severity models still rank crashes well when they are applied to a later year, to places left out of training and to another source's records.

This page asks whether the study's severity models still rank crashes when they are applied to years, places and records they were not fitted on. ROC-AUC measures how well a model ranks fatal crashes above the others, on a scale from 0.5 (chance) to 1 (perfect). The calculator's model, the one the site publishes, was tested only within the Catalan file. It ranked later years' crashes better than a table of fatal shares by road and crash type, and the crashes of each province and of Barcelona city left out of its fitting about as well as the same model fitted there, but its estimates of the fatal share did not carry over to the province or the city of Barcelona. No other source records its inputs. A model restricted to the ten variables that DGT's national crash records code in the same way, trained on the Catalan file and applied to the 67,971 crashes with a death or serious injury that DGT recorded elsewhere in Spain, ranked them almost as well as a model trained on those DGT records. That does not establish that any of the models can be used nationally: the published model was not part of that test, and Catalonia's serious crashes differ from the rest of Spain's in the mix of crash types and in how several fields are recorded.

Every test on this page scores crashes that played no part in training and sets the result beside a reference: a model of the same kind trained within the test population and cross-validated there. The reference shows how well that population's crashes can be ranked at all, and the difference between the two scores is what is lost by using a model trained elsewhere. A small test population gives a weak reference, which a model trained elsewhere on many more crashes can beat.

The calculator's model

The calculator's model is the one the site publishes (Crash severity model and calculator). It has been tested only within the Catalan file, on crashes on the roads a reader can choose. Predicting each year of 2016–2023 with the model fitted on the years before it gives a ROC-AUC of 0.743 (95% interval 0.730–0.755) over the 11,611 crashes, against 0.709 for the table of fatal shares by road and crash type. On the last year alone it scores 0.755, against 0.732 for the same model fitted and cross-validated on that year's 1,527 crashes.

With each province left out of the fitting in turn, it scores between 0.66 (Barcelona) and 0.77 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see. In every province that is within 0.05 of the same model fitted and cross-validated there, and above the table. Its estimates carry over less well. With the province of Barcelona left out they run too high (11.7% predicted against 9.5% observed). Fitted on the rest of Catalonia, it ranks Barcelona city's crashes at 0.646, against 0.677 for the same model fitted in the city, but it estimates a fatal share of 5.9% where 10.2% were fatal. Once the city's earlier crashes are in the fitting, its estimates for the city's streets are close (7.9% against 8.5%).

No other source records the calculator's inputs: DGT's national records have no field for the road's owning network or the posted limit, and their road-type and junction codings disagree with the Catalan file's on the crashes both hold. The calculator's model therefore has no test outside Catalonia. The tests below, on other sources, use other models.

Tested on DGT records outside Catalonia

DGT's records of crashes outside Catalonia are kept separately from the Catalan file, so they give a test on a source the model has never seen. The test uses the harmonised Catalonia model: the project's original Catalan model restricted to the ten variables both sources record in the same way, none of them the road's owner, and trained on the Catalan file alone. The outcome means the same in both: the Catalan file's fatal crashes match DGT's crashes with a death within 24 hours in every province and year both cover (Catalonia).

On these crashes the Catalonia-trained model has a ROC-AUC of 0.708 (95% interval 0.702–0.712), against 0.712 for a model of the same kind trained on DGT's records outside Catalonia, a difference of −0.004. Its probabilities are close to calibrated. Fatal crashes are commoner in the test (15.4%) than in the Catalan training records (12.6%); the model's mean prediction, 14.2%, falls between the two, and its calibration slope is 1.10, a little less extreme than the outcomes warrant.

Across the 46 provinces with at least 30 fatal and 30 non-fatal crashes, the ROC-AUC runs from 0.60 (León) to 0.79 (Zaragoza), with a median of 0.68.

Variables of the harmonised Catalonia model

The ten variables are year, month, day of the week, time of day, urban or interurban zone, crash type, lighting, weather, road surface and number of vehicles. Road type and junction were left out because their codings disagree on the crashes both sources hold, and the speed limit, the types of vehicle involved and the location are not available in a comparable form. Averaged over the variables used, values recorded as not specified make up at most 0.8% of any source's records, although weather is unspecified in 6.1% of serious and fatal crashes outside Catalonia.

The original Catalan model, tested within Catalonia

Before the calculator, the project's Catalan model was gradient-boosted trees on the file's circumstance fields. Its strongest predictor was the road's owner, a field that turned out to record how a crash was documented rather than the road: a blank owner is far commoner on fatal records. That model was retired, and its tests are kept here as a record (model review). Their scores include whatever the artefact contributed. For these tests the model is used without its province variable, so that a province left out of training is new to it. On the last year of the Catalan file (2023), held back from training and from every choice of settings, it scores 0.79, against 0.70 for a model trained and cross-validated on the 1,732 crashes of that year alone. With each of the four provinces left out of training in turn, it scores between 0.69 (Barcelona) and 0.79 (Tarragona) on the province it did not see. That is better than the province's own model in Girona, Lleida and Tarragona, whose own records are few, and 0.03 below it in the province of Barcelona, which holds most of the crashes.

The original model on Barcelona city

Barcelona city is the hardest test inside Catalonia. Trained on the rest of Catalonia, the original model ranks the city's crashes at 0.642, against 0.656 for a model trained in Barcelona: a difference of −0.015 (95% interval −0.054 to 0.021), too small to separate from zero. Both scores are well below the 0.764 that a model reaches when trained and tested in the rest of Catalonia. That fall comes from the kind of crashes and the number available to train on: at the same training size, Barcelona's crashes are harder to rank than the rest of Catalonia's mix of interurban and urban crashes, but not harder than its urban crashes (difference −0.039, 95% interval −0.077 to −0.003).

The ranking carries over to the city, but the probabilities do not: the calibration slope there is 0.44, far from 1, so in Barcelona the model's scores order crashes without estimating their fatal share. The Guàrdia Urbana's own records for 2025, a second source for the city, hold 252 crashes defined compatibly with the Catalan file, only 11 of them fatal: too few for a ranking test. Their fatal share, 4.4% (95% interval 2.5–7.6%), is below the city's share in the Catalan file (10.5% over 2010–2023, 9.5% over 2020–2023). The Catalonia model restricted to Barcelona's variables, whose estimate for the city in the Catalan file is close (10.0%), predicts 10.0% for these crashes. The comparison mixes a change of year and of recording source with any error of the model, so it is weak evidence about transfer.

Within the city, the Barcelona person-severity model keeps its ranking when each of the ten districts is left out of training in turn: 0.864 on the district it did not see, against 0.855 in ordinary cross-validation on the same records. The model is kept for research only. It uses the crash type as recorded, including a term the Guàrdia Urbana uses almost only for serious crashes (Barcelona), so part of its ranking may come from how serious crashes are coded.

How the crash populations differ

A model can rank well in a population whose crashes look quite different from its own, so a successful transfer says nothing about how alike the populations are. DGT's records for 2016–2024 compare them on the same fields and the same outcome definitions, although each police force fills the fields in its own way. A death or serious injury occurs in a smaller share of injury crashes in Catalonia than elsewhere in Spain (6.8% against 10.3%), and fewer of those crashes are fatal within 24 hours (12.2% against 15.4%). Barcelona city lies further from the national pattern, at 2.7% and 9.1%. The lower severe share comes from the denominator: per 100,000 residents a year, Catalonia's records hold 314 injury crashes against 184 elsewhere, but about as many crashes with a death or serious injury (21.3 against 19.0). How completely each force records slight-injury crashes is part of that difference, and these data cannot separate it from real differences (how often crashes happen and how deadly they are).

Among crashes with a death or serious injury, four of the eleven variables compared differ by more than a limit set in advance: road alignment, crash type, weather and road surface. The largest gaps are in how crashes are recorded. DGT's code for an unknown road alignment covers 14.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 0.4% elsewhere, and weather is unspecified in 6.1% outside Catalonia but almost never inside it. Fewer Catalan crashes are run-off-road crashes (13.4% against 25.8%), and crash types too are coded by different police forces. Road type does not differ beyond the limit: conventional roads hold 35.0% of these crashes in Catalonia and 36.1% elsewhere, counting every conventional road together, because DGT's records for Catalonia code them as dual carriageways until 2020 and as single carriageways from 2021 (coding breaks). Barcelona city's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's far more, on seven variables, led by road alignment, road type and urban or interurban zone.

What the tests support

The tests support ranking serious and fatal crashes in Catalonia with the calculator's model, on later years and in places left out of its fitting, better than the table of fatal shares by road and crash type. Its estimates held on later years but not when the province or the city of Barcelona was left out. A version of the original Catalan model restricted to the variables DGT records alike ranks serious crashes elsewhere in Spain about as well as a model trained there, losing only 0.004 of ROC-AUC; it is not the published model. The calculator's model, the original model and the Barcelona person-severity model use variables or records that no second source holds, so they are tested only within their own files. Catalonia's serious crashes differ materially from Spain's on four of the eleven variables compared. No model has been shown to be fit for use across Spain, so national use of the models is not established.
