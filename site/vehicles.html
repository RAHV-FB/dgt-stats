Three rates, one denominator

For 2022, and only for 2022, DGT publishes an estimate of how far each type of vehicle is driven: its circulating fleet by type and age (vehicles with an inspection, insurance, ownership-change, re-registration or fine record in the previous ten years, which DGT says leaves out between 5% and 45% of the active register depending on type) multiplied by the mean annual kilometres modelled from roadworthiness-inspection odometer readings, readings taken across 2014–2023 and annualised over the interval between two inspections, so the distance is a normal-year average imputed to the 2022 fleet rather than a measurement of 2022 travel (see Limits). The same year's yearbook tables count the vehicles of each type involved in injury crashes and in fatal crashes, and the drivers and passengers of each type who died. Dividing the counts by the kilometres gives the three rates below for the six vehicle groups the two sources share (93% of the vehicles in fatal crashes, pedestrians not counted; bicycles, personal mobility vehicles, machinery and unknown vehicles have no kilometre denominator).

Per kilometre, motorcycles are in a fatal crash 10 times as often as cars (43.5 against 4.3 per billion km) and their riders die 18 times as often (40.4 against 2.2). Mopeds are second on both, at about 48% of the motorcycle rate (20.7 and 19.1). Heavy trucks (10.7) and buses (15.7) are in fatal crashes 2.5 and 3.7 times as often as cars per kilometre. Heavy-truck occupants die about as often per kilometre as car occupants (1.9, interval 1.4 to 2.5, against 2.2); bus occupants 2.2 times as often (4.8, but on 13 deaths, so the interval runs from 2.6 to 8.3). Vans and trucks up to 3,500 kg are in fatal crashes about as often as cars per kilometre (4.4 against 4.3), their occupants die less often (1.6 against 2.2), and they are in injury crashes less often per kilometre (183 against 331).

Per circulating vehicle, buses and heavy trucks lead by a wide margin: a heavy truck is in a fatal crash 10 times as often as a car (57.5 against 5.6 per 100,000). Most of that gap is distance. A heavy truck covers about 4 times the kilometres of a car in a year (53,598 against 13,073), so per kilometre the ratio falls to 2.5. Motorcycles and mopeds move the other way: they are driven little, so a modest rate per vehicle becomes the highest rate per kilometre. Which denominator is right depends on the question: per vehicle answers what a fleet of that size costs in fatal crashes, per kilometre what a trip of a given length costs.

The two measures separate because of who dies. When a motorcycle or a moped is in a fatal crash the dead are almost always its own riders (0.93 and 0.92 occupant deaths per fatal involvement). For cars the figure is 0.52, for buses 0.31 and for heavy trucks 0.18: in more than four of every five fatal crashes with a heavy truck, the people killed were in the other vehicle or on foot. A heavy truck's danger per kilometre is therefore mostly a danger to others, which a rate of its own occupants' deaths, the only per-type measure the microdata can give, would miss entirely.

The seven types in the kilometre table add up to 32,522,330 circulating vehicles, 91% of the 35,668,443 on the register in 2022. The two totals are not nested: the circulating fleet leaves out registered vehicles with no inspection, insurance, ownership or fine record in ten years, and the register includes agricultural machinery, trailers and other types with no kilometre estimate (industrial tractors are inside the heavy-truck row, and quadricycles inside the moped and motorcycle rows). Distance falls steeply with vehicle age for every type, and most steeply for heavy trucks: a truck under five years old covers 100,921 km a year, one over twenty 12,976. Any rate per vehicle therefore depends on how old the fleet is, which a rate per kilometre does not.

Deaths of car occupants fell by 78% between 2003 and 2013 and have been flat since; heavy-truck and bus occupant deaths fell in step with them. Deaths of motorcycle riders and passengers did not: after the drop to 2013 they climbed back to 441 in 2024, against an average of 424 a year in 1997–1999, and they were the second-largest group in 2024. The per-kilometre rates above describe a single year; without kilometre estimates for other years there is no way to say whether the motorcycle rate has risen or whether more kilometres are being ridden.

Limits

Four things to keep in mind. First, involvement by vehicle type exists only in the yearbook tables (type × zone × severity), never in the crash microdata, so nothing finer, such as involvement by hour, road type or crash type, is possible. Second, the kilometres are modelled, not measured: DGT annualises the odometer readings taken at roadworthiness inspections and imputes them to the circulating fleet; its methodology note reports that the models explain between 19% and 45% of the Gamma deviance in an individual vehicle's annualised kilometres (their R² of prediction runs from 0.16 to 0.55) and that the estimates are valid for aggregates only. They are also not a measurement of 2022 travel: DGT fits the models on inspection readings from 2014 to 2023, with the years used differing by vehicle type (cars 2017–2019, 2022 and 2023; heavy trucks 2018, 2019, 2022 and 2023; buses 2017–2019 and 2023), drops 2020 and 2021 as pandemic-anomalous, and annualises each reading over the interval between two favourable inspections, up to four years for cars and motorcycles, three for mopeds, two for vans, light trucks and industrial tractors and one for heavy trucks and buses; for 63% of the circulating fleet the modelled value is replaced by the real reading at the nearest inspection in time, of whatever year. The kilometres are therefore a normal-year annualised average imputed to the 2022 fleet, while the crash counts are 2022 alone. The intervals on this page come from the crash counts alone and treat the kilometres as known. Third, the groups must mean the same thing on both sides of the division. Vans and trucks up to 3,500 kg are one group because the crash record codes most light commercial vehicles as vans while the register splits them: taken separately (table below), trucks up to 3,500 kg would be in fatal crashes 39% as often as vans per kilometre, a gap with no plausible cause but the coding. Heavy trucks include tractor units and articulated vehicles because the kilometre table's heavy category is, per DGT's methodology note, the union of its trucks over 3,500 kg and its industrial tractors (the mapping table at the end gives the counts and their reconciliation). Fourth, the two sides of the division do not cover the same vehicles. The yearbook counts every vehicle in a crash on a Spanish road, including foreign-registered ones, while the fleet and the kilometres come from Spanish roadworthiness inspections and so cover Spanish-registered vehicles only; the odometer readings, in turn, include the kilometres those vehicles drive abroad, whose crashes are not in the numerator. Heavy trucks are the most exposed on both counts, since the group includes the tractor units that do international haulage, and the two errors push the rate in opposite directions, so the size and even the sign of the net effect are unknown.
