Spain

Vehicle type, distance driven and crash severity

Six vehicle types compared by their involvement in fatal crashes in Spain in 2022, per vehicle on the road and per kilometre driven, and by who dies in those crashes.

In 2022 a heavy truck (over 3,500 kg) was involved in a fatal crash 10.3× as often as a car per vehicle on the road, and 2.5× as often per kilometre driven. The two figures differ because each heavy truck is driven 4.1× as far as a car. Motorcycles show the opposite pattern. Each is driven 2,831 km a year against 13,073 km for a car, so a rate 2.2× a car's per vehicle becomes 10.1× a car's per kilometre.

The ranking of vehicle types therefore depends on the denominator. Per vehicle on the road, buses and heavy trucks have the highest rates of involvement in fatal crashes; per kilometre, motorcycles and mopeds do.

Motorcycles are involved in 8.6× as many injury crashes per kilometre as cars, and the share of those crashes that are fatal is only 1.2× a car's: their excess is mostly one of frequency. Heavy trucks are involved in fewer injury crashes per kilometre than cars (0.4×), but the share of those crashes that are fatal is 5.9× a car's: their excess is one of severity. Both profiles describe each vehicle as it is used, together with the roads, drivers and journeys that go with it.

Who dies in crashes involving heavy trucks

Involvement counts the crash, whoever died in it. The occupant columns of the first table count only the deaths of each vehicle's own occupants. Own occupants killed per fatal crash average 0.18 for a heavy truck, 0.52 for a car and 0.93 for a motorcycle. Every fatal crash has at least one death, so a figure of 0.18 means that in at least 82% of the fatal crashes involving a heavy truck, nobody in the truck died: everyone killed was in another vehicle or on foot.

The choice of measure reverses the comparison with cars. Counted by the deaths of their own occupants, heavy trucks have a lower rate per kilometre than cars (1.9 against 2.2 per billion km). Counted by involvement in fatal crashes, their rate is 2.5× a car's. A measure built from occupant deaths alone would miss most of the deaths in crashes involving heavy trucks.

How the crash counts and the kilometres are matched

DGT models the kilometres from odometer readings taken at roadworthiness inspections over several years, annualised and attached to the 2022 fleet. The crash counts include foreign-registered vehicles, and the kilometres include Spanish vehicles' travel abroad. Quadricycles count with mopeds and motorcycles in the kilometres but among other vehicles in the crash tables. Vans and light trucks form one group because the crash records and the vehicle register divide them differently; taken apart, light trucks would have 39% of a van's rate per kilometre, and the data cannot show how much of that gap is real and how much comes from the split.

LimitationsThe kilometres are DGT's estimates for 2022, which DGT describes as valid for aggregates rather than for individual vehicles. The estimates it published for 2024 are built by a different method and cannot be joined to them (see Trends since 2019), so the comparison covers a single year. The kilometres cover all roads, so rates per kilometre cannot be split between urban and interurban roads. The crash counts and the kilometres cover slightly different sets of vehicles. The intervals in the result tables reflect the crash counts only and treat the kilometres as exact.
