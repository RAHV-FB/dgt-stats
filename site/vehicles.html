Three rates, one denominator

For 2022, and only for 2022, DGT publishes an estimate of how far each type of vehicle is driven: the registered fleet by type and age multiplied by the mean annual kilometres modelled from roadworthiness-inspection odometer readings. The same year's yearbook tables count the vehicles of each type involved in injury crashes and in fatal crashes, and the drivers and passengers of each type who died. Dividing the counts by the kilometres gives the three rates below for the six vehicle groups the two sources share (93% of the vehicles in fatal crashes, pedestrians not counted; bicycles, personal mobility vehicles, machinery and unknown vehicles have no kilometre denominator).

Per kilometre, motorcycles are in a fatal crash 10 times as often as cars (43.5 against 4.3 per billion km) and their riders die 18 times as often (40.4 against 2.2). Mopeds sit close behind on both. Heavy trucks (10.7) and buses (15.7) are in fatal crashes 2.5 and 3.7 times as often as cars per kilometre. Heavy-truck occupants die less often per kilometre than car occupants (1.9 against 2.2); bus occupants 2.2 times as often (4.8, but on 13 deaths, so the interval runs from 2.6 to 8.3). Vans and trucks up to 3,500 kg (4.4) match cars on the fatal measures and are in injury crashes less often per kilometre (183 against 331).

Per registered vehicle, buses and heavy trucks lead by a wide margin: a heavy truck is in a fatal crash 10 times as often as a car (57.5 against 5.6 per 100,000). Most of that gap is distance. A heavy truck covers about 4 times the kilometres of a car in a year (53,598 against 13,073), so per kilometre the ratio falls to 2.5. Motorcycles and mopeds move the other way: they are driven little, so a modest rate per vehicle becomes the highest rate per kilometre. Which denominator is right depends on the question: per vehicle answers what a fleet of that size costs in fatal crashes, per kilometre what a trip of a given length costs.

The two measures separate because of who dies. When a motorcycle or a moped is in a fatal crash the dead are almost always its own riders (0.93 and 0.92 occupant deaths per fatal involvement). For cars the figure is 0.52, for buses 0.31 and for heavy trucks 0.18: in more than four of every five fatal crashes with a heavy truck, the people killed were in the other vehicle or on foot. A heavy truck's danger per kilometre is therefore mostly a danger to others, which a rate of its own occupants' deaths, the only per-type measure the microdata can give, would miss entirely.

The seven types in the kilometre table add up to 32,522,330 vehicles, 91% of the 35,668,443 registered in 2022; the rest are tractors, trailers, quadricycles and other vehicles without an estimate. Distance falls steeply with vehicle age for every type, and most steeply for heavy trucks: a truck under five years old covers 100,921 km a year, one over twenty 12,976. Any rate per registered vehicle therefore depends on how old the fleet is, which a rate per kilometre does not.

Deaths of car occupants fell by three quarters between 2003 and 2013 and have been flat since; heavy-truck and bus occupant deaths fell in step with them. Motorcyclist deaths did not: after the drop to 2013 they have climbed back to the levels of the late 1990s, and in 2024 they were the second-largest group after car occupants. The per-kilometre rates above describe a single year; without kilometre estimates for other years there is no way to say whether the motorcycle rate has risen or whether more kilometres are being ridden.

Limits

Three things to keep in mind. First, involvement by vehicle type exists only in the yearbook tables (type × zone × severity), never in the crash microdata, so nothing finer, such as involvement by hour, road type or crash type, is possible. Second, the kilometres are modelled, not measured: DGT annualises the odometer readings taken at roadworthiness inspections and imputes them to the registered fleet; its methodology note reports that the model explains between 19% and 45% of the variance across individual vehicles and that the estimates are valid for aggregates only. The intervals on this page come from the crash counts alone and treat the kilometres as known. Third, the groups must mean the same thing on both sides of the division. Vans and trucks up to 3,500 kg are one group because the crash record codes most light commercial vehicles as vans while the register splits them: taken separately (table below), trucks up to 3,500 kg would be in fatal crashes 39% as often as vans per kilometre, a gap with no plausible cause but the coding. Heavy trucks include tractor units and articulated vehicles because the kilometre table's heavy category is, per DGT's methodology note, the union of trucks over 3,500 kg (272,157 vehicles, 6.9 billion km) and industrial tractors (222,594 vehicles, 19.7 billion km).
