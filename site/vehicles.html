Drivers, vehicles and factors

Vehicle type, distance driven and crash severity

In 2022 a heavy truck (over 3,500 kg) was involved in a fatal crash 10.3 times as often as a car per vehicle on the road, but 2.5 times as often per kilometre, because each heavy truck is driven 4.1 times as far. A motorcycle is driven 2,831 km a year against a car's 13,073 km, so its rate rises from 2.2 times a car's per vehicle to 10.1 times per kilometre, the highest of any type. The motorcycle's excess comes mostly from being in more crashes, the heavy truck's from more of its crashes being fatal.

The ranking depends on the denominator

Per vehicle on the road, buses and heavy trucks have the highest rates of involvement in fatal crashes. Per kilometre, motorcycles have by far the highest, and their 95% interval is clear of every other type's. Mopeds and buses come next, but their rates rest on few vehicles in fatal crashes (39 and 42) and their intervals overlap, so their order is not established. Motorcycles are involved in 8.6 times as many injury crashes per kilometre as cars, and the share of those crashes that are fatal is 1.2 times a car's: their excess is mostly one of frequency. Heavy trucks are involved in fewer injury crashes per kilometre than cars (0.4 times a car's rate), but the share of those crashes that are fatal is 5.9 times a car's: their excess is one of severity. Both profiles describe each vehicle as it is used, together with the roads, drivers and journeys that go with it.

Most deaths in heavy-truck crashes were outside the truck

Involvement counts the crash, whoever died in it. The table below counts only the deaths of each vehicle's own occupants. Own occupants killed per vehicle in a fatal crash average 0.18 for a heavy truck, 0.52 for a car and 0.93 for a motorcycle. A figure of 0.18 means that for at least 82% of the heavy trucks in fatal crashes, nobody in the truck died: everyone killed was in another vehicle or on foot.

Counted by the deaths of their own occupants, heavy trucks have about the same rate per kilometre as cars (1.9 against 2.2 per billion km; the 95% intervals overlap). Counted by involvement in fatal crashes, their rate is 2.5 times a car's. A measure built from occupant deaths alone would miss most of the deaths in crashes involving heavy trucks.

How the crash counts and the kilometres are matched

DGT models the kilometres from odometer readings taken at roadworthiness inspections over several years, annualised and attached to the 2022 fleet. Quadricycles count with mopeds and motorcycles in the kilometres but among other vehicles in the crash tables. Vans and light trucks form one group because the crash records and the vehicle register divide them differently; taken apart, light trucks would have 39% of a van's rate per kilometre, and the data cannot show how much of that gap is real and how much comes from the split.

Limitations. The kilometres are DGT's estimates for 2022, which DGT describes as valid for aggregates rather than for individual vehicles. DGT's later release gives mean kilometres by vehicle type for 2023 and 2024 on the same basis (see Since 2019); this page matches the crash tables and the kilometres for 2022 only. The kilometres cover all roads, so rates per kilometre cannot be split between urban and interurban roads. The crash counts include foreign-registered vehicles driven in Spain, while the kilometres are those of Spanish-registered vehicles, including their travel abroad. The size of that mismatch cannot be measured from these sources, and its direction is unknown. It bears most on the rates of heavy trucks and buses, the types used for international haulage and coach travel. The intervals in the result tables reflect the crash counts only and treat the kilometres as exact.
