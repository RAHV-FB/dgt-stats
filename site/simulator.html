On the published evidence, the largest saving is not a new limit but today's limits kept: if every driver now above the limit on autopistas, autovías and conventional roads slowed to it, about 335 fewer people a year would die there (between 298 and 370), 280 of them on conventional roads. Raising the motorway limit to 140 km/h would cost lives even if every driver kept to it, +39 a year, because a higher limit lifts everyone's speed more than it removes speeding. And the yearly death count shows a change reliably only from about 184 deaths, so most speed laws have to be judged by the speeds they produce.

What the model is

A calculation, not a model fitted to Spanish crashes. Spanish crash records carry no speeds, so the effect of speed cannot be estimated from them; it is taken from published international evidence and applied to Spanish casualties and Spanish speeds. Each link is a sourced, published value:

  1. Today's casualties. Deaths, hospital admissions, other injuries and injury crashes a year on each kind of road, the mean of 2022–2024 in DGT's crash microdata.
  2. Today's speeds. Car speeds measured by radar on Spanish roads in 2022 for the EU Baseline project: the average, the share within the limit and the speed 85% of cars stay under. On conventional roads the average is 94.4 km/h and 57% of cars are above 90.
  3. The law. A new limit moves the average speed by only part of the change, as 143 before-and-after results of limit changes found; compliance brings a share of the drivers above the limit in force down to it.
  4. Casualties. The Power Model: when the average speed changes, deaths change by the ratio of the speeds to the power 4.6 on interurban roads, injury crashes to the power 1.6. A 1% rise in the average speed means about 4.7% more deaths.
  5. Could it be seen? A forecasting model fitted to Spain's monthly deaths, and tested against machine learning and naive forecasts on years it had not seen, says how large a change the yearly death count would show.

What you set: a limit for each kind of road, how far drivers' average speed follows a new limit, and how many drivers above the limit keep to it. What comes out: the new average speed and spread of speeds, deaths, admissions, other injuries and injury crashes a year, their value at DGT's own figures, the change in travel time, and the chance the next year's death count shows it. What it is not: a forecast of what a government will achieve. Links 1 to 4 say what follows if drivers behave as set; only link 5 is fitted to Spanish data, and it judges whether a change could be seen, not how large it is.

A higher limit that everyone keeps to

Would autopistas and autovías be safer at 140 km/h if everyone kept to it? The argument for it is that the average speed rises but nobody speeds and the traffic flows more evenly. The model can test the first two parts and measure the third.

It costs lives. On the evidence, 140 km/h kept by everyone means +39 (+33 to +44) deaths a year against today, and +418 injury crashes, while saving 26 million vehicle-hours. Two facts decide it. Raising a limit lifts the whole flow, not only the fast drivers: the 143 results put the rise in the average at 17% of the change in the limit, +3.5 km/h for +20. And few cars go above 140 today (10% on autopistas), so keeping everyone to it takes off only 1.4 km/h there and 0.6 on autovías. The rise beats the cut. Deaths would fall only if less than 2% of the 20 km/h reached the average speed.

Against the right comparison it costs more. If everyone can be made to keep to 140, everyone can be made to keep to 120, which saves 55 lives a year on these roads. Between the two, the extra 20 km/h of limit costs 94 lives a year for 64 million vehicle-hours, about 678,000 hours for each life.

Where it would break even. With drivers responding as they typically do and everyone keeping to the limit, the limit could rise to about 130 km/h before deaths exceeded today's: at 130 kept by everyone the change is +2 a year, the speeders removed balancing everyone else speeding up. Above that, the higher limit costs lives even with perfect compliance.

The flow of traffic. Keeping everyone to 140 narrows the spread of speeds on autopistas from 14.9 to 12.8 km/h. The Power Model counts only the average. A review of the evidence finds more crashes where speeds vary more (Aarts & van Schagen 2006) but gives no dose-response that could be applied here, so the page shows the spread and does not turn it into casualties. For the narrower spread to cancel the rise it would have to cut deaths on these roads by 10% on its own. And everyone keeping to 120 narrows the spread far more, to 7.9 km/h: on the average and on the spread, today's limit kept beats a higher limit kept.

What the model concludes

  1. Enforcing today's limits saves more than any single new limit. Everyone keeping to them: −335 (−370 to −298) deaths a year, 28% of those on these roads, worth about €1.0 billion a year at DGT's values. The largest single new limit, conventional roads at 70 km/h, saves 261 with the typical response; only lowering both limits at once saves more, up to 350 with autopistas and autovías at 100 km/h and conventional roads at 70 km/h.
  2. The saving is on conventional roads. 280 of the 335: they carry 850 of the 1,200 deaths, 57% of their cars are above 90 km/h, by 7.8 km/h a car on average, and they kill 3.38× as many people per kilometre driven as motorways.
  3. Motorway limits move little either way, and a higher one kept still costs lives. 110 km/h saves 41, 130 costs 31 and 140 costs 50 with the typical response; 140 kept by everyone costs 39, and the break-even limit kept by everyone is about 130 km/h. No change of the motorway limit alone would show in a year's count more than 25% of the time.
  4. 80 km/h on conventional roads is a real saving that the counts may not show. −123 (−138 to −108) deaths a year; 70 km/h −261. The first year's count would show the 80 km/h saving 45% of the time: a year without a visible fall would not mean the law had failed.
  5. On urban streets the direction is clear and the size is not. Streets at 50 km/h brought to 30 would take the average car from 46.5 to 39.3 km/h and deaths there by −40%, but the evidence for urban deaths runs from −67% to +9%; admissions to hospital, better pinned down, change by −29% (−42% to −13%).

Where the risk is

Divided into the kilometres the Ministerio de Transportes measures on interurban roads, conventional roads killed 3.38× as many people per kilometre as autopistas and autovías in 2023, and 3.42× over 2016–2023, although their traffic is slower: an average of 94.4 km/h against 117.2 on autovías and 121.3 on autopistas. Speed does not explain that gap: conventional roads also have two-way traffic without a barrier, junctions and direct access, which these data do not measure. What speed decides is how much each kind of road changes when its own speeds change, and that is what the simulator computes.

How each number is made

The baseline is the mean deaths, admissions, other injured and injury crashes of 2022–2024 by road class, from the crash microdata, which reconcile exactly with DGT's yearbook. Today's speeds are the free-flow speeds of cars measured by radar in 2022 for the EU Baseline project. A log-normal distribution through the share within the limit and the 85th percentile reproduces both, and its mean lands within 0.7 km/h of the measured one on every kind of road; it gives the share above any limit and the spread. The limits are those of the Reglamento General de Circulación and Real Decreto 970/2020. A new limit moves the average speed by the curve fitted to 143 before-and-after results: a cut of 10 km/h by −3.2 km/h (32%), a rise of 20 by +3.5 (17%); the whole distribution is scaled, so every driver moves in proportion. Compliance moves a share of the cars above the limit in force down to it, which lowers the average by that share of the average excess: 7.8 km/h a car on conventional roads, 6.2 on autopistas and 3.7 on autovías at today's limits. The Power Model, in Elvik's 2009 meta-analysis, turns the change in the average speed into casualties, more steeply for deaths than for injuries. Casualties are valued at DGT's 2024 value of a life and of an injury.

The forecasting model: could the counts show it?

A law is judged by comparing the deaths after it with the deaths there would have been without it, and the second number is a forecast. Its error decides what the comparison can see. What it is: a Poisson regression of each month's deaths on the month of the year, a trend over the four years before, the month's road fuel (the traffic) and its number of Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Fitted on 2021–2024, it says a month with 1% more road fuel has about 1.2% more deaths (0.5 to 2.0), and one more Saturday +8%. How it was chosen: four forms of the model and windows of three to eight years were scored on forecasts of 2006–2015 alone; the years after played no part and are the test. Beside it are scored two naive forecasts and a machine-learning model, gradient-boosted trees given the same inputs and tuned on the same years.

What the test shows. The model beats the trees in every set of years: a tree cannot extend a trend beyond the years it has seen, and with 48 monthly rows it fits the noise. In the lockdown years it beats last year's count by far (7.3% against 19.2%), because it knows the traffic fell. In the flat held-back years last year's count does slightly better (5.9% against 6.6%; the worst year is 2022, forecast from a window that includes the lockdowns), and trees with large leaves best of all (3.7%), but they lose in the years when traffic or the trend moves, and whether the years ahead will be flat is not known when the forecast is made. A law's effect has to be told apart from moving traffic and trends, so the model is the one used.

What it concludes. On the 1,200 deaths a year of the autopistas, autovías and conventional roads, the first year's count picks up a fall of 184 (15%) four times in five, and a rise of 217; smaller changes are picked up less often, not never. For all 1,284 interurban deaths the fall is 15%. Waiting does not help: summed over five years the threshold is 36%, because the trend drifts further from any extrapolation the longer it runs. So only the largest laws on this page would show in the counts, which is why the dated policy change on the 2006 page could not be settled from them, and why a speed law is checked by measuring speeds.

Conclusion

The evidence ranks the levers. Keeping to today's limits would save about 335 lives a year on Spain's interurban roads, 280 of them on conventional roads; lowering the conventional limit to 80 km/h about 123; and raising the motorway limit to 140 km/h would cost about 39 even with every driver keeping to it, 94 more than everyone keeping to 120. Only the largest of these, from about 184 deaths a year, would reliably show in the first year's death count, so a speed law has to be judged by the speeds it produces.

Limits. The Power Model is an aggregate relation estimated from before-and-after studies in other countries; it applies to the average speed of traffic and is less certain on urban streets, where its interval for deaths includes no effect. The speeds are free-flow speeds of cars on weekday daytime, measured in 2022; trucks, night traffic and congestion are not in them. Compliance moves each complying driver to exactly the limit, and the narrower spread it brings is shown but not counted, so the compliance figures are if anything low. The response to a new limit varies widely between roads, which is why it can be set by hand. Time is computed at free-flow speeds over the measured interurban kilometres of cars and other light vehicles, which leave out municipal interurban roads; free motorways are timed at the speeds of autovías, with which the Ministry's traffic table counts them. Other interurban roads (84 deaths a year) and urban streets as a national count are left out because no speed or street split is published for them.
