What a speed law would do

Set new speed limits, decide how far drivers follow them, and see what the published evidence implies for deaths and injuries on Spain's interurban roads, what that is worth, what it costs in time, and whether the death counts could ever show it.

Speed is the lever on the severity of crashes that the evidence measures best, and in Spain most cars on conventional roads drive above the limit: only 43% of those measured in 2022 kept to 90 km/h. Applied to the deaths on Spain's motorways and conventional roads, the published Power Model says that if every driver now above the limit kept to it, about 328 fewer people a year would die there (between 291 and 362), more than any change of limit on this page achieves. Lowering the limit on conventional roads from 90 to 80 km/h, with drivers responding as they typically do, would save about 123. Neither would be easy to see: a change smaller than about 184 deaths a year cannot be told from an ordinary year in the counts, and waiting longer makes it harder, not easier.

Read the rows against each other. Raising the motorway limit to 130 km/h would cost about 31 lives a year and save about 24 million vehicle-hours, about 756,000 hours for each life. Lowering it to 110 km/h would save about 41 lives for 35 million hours. Lowering the conventional limit to 80 km/h saves 123 lives for 40 million hours, about 327,000 hours each. Half of today's speeders keeping to the limit would save 175. The page puts no price on time: no Spanish official value of travel time was found to cite, and the trade-off is a choice, not a calculation. Lives are valued at DGT's own figure, €1,965,850 for each death prevented.

On urban streets the page gives no national count, because DGT does not publish how many urban casualties happen on streets at 30 km/h and how many at 50. On the streets still at 50, a 30 km/h limit with the typical response would take the mean car speed from 46.5 to 39.3 km/h and change deaths there by −40%; but the evidence for deaths on urban streets is weak enough that its interval runs from −67% to +9%. For admissions to hospital, which the evidence pins down better, the change is −29% (−42% to −13%).

Where the risk is

The Ministerio de Transportes measures how far traffic travels on Spain's interurban roads, by type of road. Divided into it, conventional roads killed 3.38× as many people per kilometre as motorways and autovías in 2023, and 3.42× over 2016–2023, although their traffic is slower: a mean of 94.4 km/h against 117.2. Speed is not what separates the two kinds of road; two-way traffic with no barrier between the directions is. Within each kind of road, speed is what decides how hard a crash is, and that is what the Power Model measures. Conventional roads are where the most deaths and the most speeding meet.

How each number is made

Four links, each with its source. The baseline is the mean deaths, admissions and other injured of 2022–2024 by road class, from the crash microdata, which reconcile exactly with DGT's yearbook. Today's speeds are the free-flow speeds of cars measured by radar in Spain in 2022 for the EU's Baseline project: the mean, the share within the limit and the speed 85% of cars stay under. A log-normal distribution through the last two reproduces both; its mean lands within 0.7 km/h of the measured one on every kind of road. A new limit moves the average speed by part of the change: the curve fitted to 143 before-and-after results puts a cut of 10 km/h at −3.2 km/h and of 20 km/h at −7.2. Compliance brings a share of the drivers above the limit down to it, which lowers the mean by that share of the average excess: 7.8 km/h per car on conventional roads, 3.7 on motorways. The Power Model turns the change in mean speed into casualties: a count changes by the ratio of the speeds raised to a power, larger for deaths than for injuries.

Could the counts show it?

A law is judged by comparing the deaths after it with the deaths there would have been without it, and the second number is a forecast. To know how good a forecast can be, a simple model was built and tested on years it had not seen: a Poisson regression of monthly deaths on the month of the year, a four-year trend, the month's road fuel and its number of Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Its form was chosen on the forecasts of 2006–2015 alone. On those years its error was 4.9%, against 11.7% for simply repeating last year's count and 14.6% for gradient-boosted trees given the same inputs, which cannot extend a trend. In the lockdown years it was 7.3% against 19.2%, because it knows the traffic fell. On the years held back, 2016–2019 and 2022–2024, it was 6.6% and last year's count 5.9%: in flat years nothing beats last year, and the model only earns its place when the trend or the traffic moves, which is when a law's effect has to be told apart from them. Its worst held-back year is 2022, forecast from a window that includes the lockdowns.

The forecast's own error sets the smallest effect a before-and-after comparison can detect. For deaths on interurban roads one year after a law it is 15%, about 195 deaths a year; summed over five years it is 36%, because the trend drifts further from any extrapolation the longer it runs. This is why the dated policy changes on the 2006 page could not be settled from the counts, and why a speed law's effect is checked by measuring speeds, which the Power Model then turns into casualties.

Conclusion

On the evidence, the largest saving within reach is not a new limit but the existing ones kept: if every driver now above the limit on motorways and conventional roads kept to it, about 328 fewer people would die each year, 27% of the deaths on those roads, worth about €1.0 billion a year at DGT's own values. The next largest is on conventional roads, the deadliest per kilometre. Raising motorway limits trades lives for hours at a rate the page shows but does not judge. Almost none of these changes would be visible in the national death count within a year, so a speed law has to be judged by the speeds it produces, not by waiting for the deaths.

Limits. The Power Model is an aggregate relation estimated from before-and-after studies in other countries; it applies to the mean speed of traffic and is less certain on urban streets, where its interval for deaths includes no effect. The speeds are free-flow speeds of cars on weekday daytime, measured in 2022; trucks, night traffic and congestion are not in them. Compliance that removes the highest speeds also narrows the spread of speeds, which the Power Model does not count, so the compliance figures are if anything low. The response to a new limit varies widely between roads, which is why it can be set by hand. Time is computed at free-flow speeds over the measured interurban kilometres, which leave out municipal interurban roads. Other interurban roads (84 deaths a year) and urban streets as a national count are left out because no speed or street split is published for them.
