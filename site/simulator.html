What a speed law would do

Set new speed limits, decide how far drivers follow them, and see what the published evidence implies for deaths and injuries on Spain's interurban roads, what that is worth, what it costs in time, and whether the death counts could ever show it.

Speed is the lever on the severity of crashes that the evidence measures best, and in Spain most cars on conventional roads drive above the limit: only 43% of those measured in 2022 kept to 90 km/h. Applied to the deaths on Spain's autopistas, autovías and conventional roads, the published Power Model says that if every driver now above the limit kept to it, about 335 fewer people a year would die there (between 298 and 370). With drivers responding to a new limit as they typically do, that is more than any single new limit on this page achieves, the largest being conventional roads at 70 km/h (261); only lowering both limits at once saves more, up to 350 with autopistas and autovías at 100 km/h and conventional roads at 70 km/h. Lowering the limit on conventional roads from 90 to 80 km/h would save about 123. The first year's death count on these roads would show full compliance almost every time, but a fall of 123 only 45% of the time: it picks up a fall of 184 four times in five, and smaller ones less often.

Read the rows against each other. Raising the limit on autopistas and autovías to 130 km/h would cost about 31 lives a year and save about 20 million vehicle-hours, about 650,000 hours for each life. Lowering it to 110 km/h would save about 41 lives for 30 million hours. Lowering the conventional limit to 80 km/h saves 123 lives for 37 million hours, about 300,000 hours each. Half of today's speeders keeping to the limit would save 179. The page puts no price on time: no Spanish official value of travel time was found to cite, and the trade-off is a choice, not a calculation. Lives are valued at DGT's own figure, €1,965,850 for each death prevented.

On urban streets the page gives no national count, because DGT does not publish how many urban casualties happen on streets at 30 km/h and how many at 50. On the streets still at 50, a 30 km/h limit with the typical response would take the mean car speed from 46.5 to 39.3 km/h and change deaths there by −40%; but the evidence for deaths on urban streets is weak enough that its interval runs from −67% to +9%. For admissions to hospital, which the evidence pins down better, the change is −29% (−42% to −13%).

Where the risk is

The Ministerio de Transportes measures how far traffic travels on Spain's interurban roads, by type of road. Divided into it, conventional roads killed 3.38× as many people per kilometre as autopistas and autovías in 2023, and 3.42× over 2016–2023, although their traffic is slower: a mean of 94.4 km/h against 117.2 on autovías and 121.3 on autopistas. Speed alone does not explain the gap: conventional roads also differ in two-way traffic without a barrier, junctions and direct access, none of which these data measure. Within each kind of road, speed is what decides how hard a crash is, and that is what the Power Model measures. Conventional roads are where the most deaths meet the most speeding of any interurban road, by the share of cars above the limit and by how far above it they drive.

How each number is made

Four links, each with its source. The baseline is the mean deaths, admissions and other injured of 2022–2024 by road class, from the crash microdata, which reconcile exactly with DGT's yearbook. Today's speeds are the free-flow speeds of cars measured by radar in Spain in 2022 for the EU Baseline project: the mean, the share within the limit and the speed 85% of cars stay under. A log-normal distribution through the last two reproduces both, and its mean lands within 0.7 km/h of the measured one on every kind of road. The limits are those of the Reglamento General de Circulación and Real Decreto 970/2020. A new limit moves the average speed by part of the change: the curve fitted to 143 before-and-after results puts a cut of 10 km/h at −3.2 km/h and of 20 km/h at −7.2. Compliance brings a share of the drivers above the limit down to it, which lowers the mean by that share of the average excess: 7.8 km/h per car on conventional roads, 6.2 on autopistas and 3.7 on autovías. The Power Model, in Elvik's 2009 meta-analysis, turns the change in mean speed into casualties, more steeply for deaths than for injuries. Casualties are valued at DGT's 2024 value of a life and of an injury.

Could the counts show it?

A law is judged by comparing the deaths after it with the deaths there would have been without it, and the second number is a forecast. To know how good a forecast can be, a simple model was built and tested on years it had not seen: a Poisson regression of monthly deaths on the month of the year, a four-year trend, the month's road fuel and its number of Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Its form was chosen on the forecasts of 2006–2015 alone. On those years its error was 4.9%, against 11.7% for simply repeating last year's count and 7.5% for gradient-boosted trees given the same inputs and tuned on the same years, which cannot extend a trend; the trees did worse than the model on every kind of road and in every set of years. In the lockdown years the model's error was 7.3% against 19.2%, because it knows the traffic fell. On the years held back, 2016–2019 and 2022–2024, it was 6.6% and last year's count 5.9%: in flat years last year's count did better than the model, which earns its place when the trend or the traffic moves, the years in which a law's effect has to be told apart from them. Its worst held-back year is 2022, forecast from a window that includes the lockdowns. Trees whose leaves hold at least 8 months, worse on the selection years (10.3%), would have done best of all on the held-back years (3.7%) and worse than the model in the lockdowns (14.2%); leaves of 12 and 20 months did worse again on the held-back years (5.9% and 7.1%). A setting that wins only in flat years cannot be picked in advance, because whether the years ahead will be flat is not known when the forecast is made.

The forecast's own error sets how large an effect a before-and-after comparison picks up. For all deaths on interurban roads one year after a law, the fall it detects four times in five (a two-sided test at 5%) is 15%, about 195 of 1,284 deaths a year; on the 1,200 deaths of the autopistas, autovías and conventional roads the simulator covers it is 184, and a rise needs to reach 217. Smaller changes are picked up less often, not never: the preset table gives each one's chance. Summed over five years the threshold is 36%, because the trend drifts further from any extrapolation the longer it runs. This is why the dated policy changes on the 2006 page could not be settled from the counts, and why a speed law's effect is checked by measuring speeds, which the Power Model then turns into casualties.

Conclusion

On the evidence, and with drivers responding to a new limit as they typically do, the largest saving within reach of any one measure is not a new limit but the existing ones kept: if every driver now above the limit on autopistas, autovías and conventional roads kept to it, about 335 fewer people would die each year, 28% of the deaths on those roads, worth about €1.0 billion a year at DGT's own values. The largest saving from a new limit is on conventional roads, the deadliest per kilometre. Raising the motorway limit trades lives for hours at a rate the page shows but does not judge. Full compliance would show in the first year's death count on those roads almost every time. Of the new limits, conventional roads at 70 km/h would show 99% of the time and at 80 km/h 45% of the time, and no change of the motorway limit alone more than 25% of the time. A speed law has to be judged by the speeds it produces, not by waiting for the deaths.

Limits. The Power Model is an aggregate relation estimated from before-and-after studies in other countries; it applies to the mean speed of traffic and is less certain on urban streets, where its interval for deaths includes no effect. The speeds are free-flow speeds of cars on weekday daytime, measured in 2022; trucks, night traffic and congestion are not in them. Compliance that removes the highest speeds also narrows the spread of speeds, which the Power Model does not count, so the compliance figures are if anything low. The response to a new limit varies widely between roads, which is why it can be set by hand. Time is computed at free-flow speeds over the measured interurban kilometres of cars and other light vehicles, which leave out municipal interurban roads; free motorways are timed at the speeds of autovías, with which the Ministry's traffic table counts them. Other interurban roads (84 deaths a year) and urban streets as a national count are left out because no speed or street split is published for them.
