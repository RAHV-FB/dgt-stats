Road safety in Spain

What changes when you stop counting crashes and start measuring road risk: seven questions answered from DGT data, each with the denominator that decides it, and a simulator of speed laws built on what they show.

A count of crashes or deaths says how many. It does not say how dangerous the roads were, because it moves with how much people drove, who was driving and in what. This site divides the same DGT counts by residents, licence holders, vehicles, kilometres and road fuel, and asks what changes. On every question below the answer changes size, and on several it changes sign. Put together, the answers point the same way: what decides how many people die is less how often crashes happen than how hard they are, and the one lever on that which a law reaches is speed.

Finding 1Since 2019, death risk has not measurably changed; serious injury has risen

Deaths in 2024 were +1.7% on 2019 as a count, −1.9% per resident, −3.4% per registered vehicle and +3.5% per tonne of road fuel, all within an ordinary year's variation. People admitted to hospital rose +11.0%, beyond it.

Annual DGT totals over INE residents, the driver census, the registered fleet and CORES road fuel, indexed to 2019, with intervals that allow for the year-to-year scatter of each count.

Finding 2The pandemic dip was less driving; since then the decline has stalled

As a count, 2020 deaths were −24% against the pre-pandemic trend and back on it by 2022. Per tonne of road fuel 2020 was on trend (0.97×): the fall was the traffic. Per tonne of fuel 2024 looks +16% above the trend; per kilometre measured on interurban roads, 2023 is +5%, within it. Fuel overstates the rise because each tonne now carries more traffic.

Joinpoint quasi-Poisson trends fitted to 1993–2019 and projected, re-run on the Ministerio de Transportes' measured interurban vehicle-kilometres.

Finding 3The summer peak is mostly traffic; the autumn excess is not

Raw deaths in July are 1.22× and in August 1.14× the average month. Per unit of petrol, which cars and motorcycles burn, they are 1.06× and 0.98×. In the April 2020 lockdown deaths fell 68% and petrol sales 76%: fewer deaths, not safer roads.

Monthly deaths since 2014 against monthly road fuel, petrol and toll-motorway traffic, in quasi-Poisson models with year and month effects.

Finding 4Older and male drivers crash little more for their driving; they die more when they do

Per kilometre, car drivers aged 75 and over are involved in injury crashes 1.02× as often as those aged 35 to 54, but are killed 3.93× as often once involved. Male car drivers are involved 1.41× as often as women per licence holder, about what the only Spanish travel survey by sex implies, and killed 2.57× as often once involved.

DGT driver tables over the driver census and DGT's 2024 kilometres by owner age; MOVILIA trips by sex as a bounded travel proxy.

Finding 5A heavy truck is ten times a car, or two and a half times, depending on the divisor

Per circulating vehicle a heavy truck is in a fatal crash 10.3 times as often as a car. Per kilometre driven it is 2.5 times. For every fatal crash a truck is in, 0.18 of its own occupants die, so most of the danger a truck carries is to other people.

DGT's 2022 kilometre estimates divided into the same year's involvement counts, with exact Poisson intervals.

Finding 6Where speed is recorded, a crash is twice as likely to kill

Injury crashes in which the police recorded inappropriate speed kill 3.41× as many people per crash as the rest, and 2.00× once the comparison is made on the same kind of road in the same year. That is an association: the record is written after the fact, and is likelier to be written when someone has died.

DGT's speed-factor report against totals from the microdata for the same provinces, which reproduce the report's own totals exactly.

Finding 7Alcohol is rising on interurban roads; distraction cannot be trended in towns

Recorded alcohol went from 5.5% to 8.2% of interurban injury crashes between 2014 and 2023, and inappropriate speed from 10.0% to 6.9% of all of them, both on a consistent record. Urban distraction jumps and falls by half in single years, and drugs are too few to compare.

DGT's concurrent-factor tables, with a recording-break rule applied to every year-to-year change before any trend is read.

Finding 8Keeping to today's limits would save more lives than any new limit

On the published Power Model and the speeds measured in Spain in 2022, if every driver now above the limit on motorways and conventional roads kept to it, about 328 fewer people a year would die there. A 80 km/h limit on conventional roads would save about 123. A validated forecasting model shows why neither would be visible in a year's death count: anything under about 184 deaths a year is not.

Spanish baselines and measured speeds, published dose-response evidence and DGT's values of a life, every number sourced.

What connects them

Wherever the data let risk be split into how often people crash and how badly they are hurt when they do, the difference lies in the second. Drivers of 75 and over, and men, crash about as often as their driving predicts and die far more often once in a crash. A crash with speed recorded kills twice as often as another on the same kind of road. Conventional roads kill 3.38× as many people per kilometre as motorways in 2023, though their traffic is slower. The national series says the same about the past: between 1996 and 2024 deaths per tonne of road fuel fell 76%, injury crashes per tonne only 13%, and deaths per injury crash 73%.

How hard a crash is depends on the energy in it and on the body that absorbs it. Age is the body; speed is the energy, and it is the one a law reaches, the one the evidence measures best and the one on which Spanish drivers stray furthest from the rule: in 2022 only 43% of cars measured on conventional roads kept to 90 km/h. That is why the last page is a simulator of speed laws, and why it says plainly what the counts cannot show.

What this site does not claim

It attributes no cause from Spanish data. The public microdata have one row per crash and no driver, vehicle or person records, so every factor here is an association, and a policy or campaign effect is never read off a time series. The simulator applies dose-response evidence measured elsewhere to Spanish baselines. Two analyses built earlier in the project are kept as supporting material because they are careful but outside this question: a model of crash severity and a test of the 2006 points licence whose headline did not survive its own falsification tests. Road design and municipal hotspots are not analysed: the files carry no road geometry, traffic volume or coordinates.

How to read the numbers

Counts are DGT's consolidated figures. An injury crash is one with at least one person killed or injured, and deaths are counted within 30 days. Every rate names its denominator, because the denominator is usually where the answer comes from. Sources, definitions, the reconciliation checks and the assumptions tested are on the data page.
