Road safety in Spain

A statistical study of road deaths and injuries in Spain from 1993 to 2024, based on national DGT and INE statistics, the Servei Català de Trànsit's file of crashes with a death or serious injury (2010–2023) and Barcelona's crash and casualty records for 2025.

A count of road deaths says how many people died. Whether the roads have become safer depends on what the count is divided by. Deaths per resident measure the burden on a population. Licence holders, registered vehicles and kilometres driven measure exposure: who could be on the road and how much they drive, with road fuel sold standing in for kilometres where these are missing. Deaths per unit of exposure are in turn the product of two factors, how often crashes happen and how deadly a crash is once it has happened, and the two often point in different directions.

Two results show the difference. Per vehicle on the road, a heavy truck is in a fatal crash 10.3× as often as a car; per kilometre driven, 2.5× as often, because each truck is driven 4.1 times as far. Between 1996 and 2024, deaths per tonne of road fuel fell 76%, mostly through fewer deaths per injury crash (down 73%) and much less through fewer injury crashes per tonne (down 13%), although the split depends on how completely slight injuries are recorded (Long-run trends).

National trends

Over the long run, road deaths in Spain fell most steeply between 2003 and 2013, by 10.9% a year, and from 2013 to 2019 they no longer fell. Since 2019 the change in deaths has stayed within ordinary year-to-year variation under every denominator: by 2024 it was +1.7% as a count, −1.9% per resident, +3.5% per tonne of road fuel, +0.7% for driver deaths per licence holder and +1.7% for occupant deaths per registered vehicle. Hospital admissions after a crash rose 11.0%, beyond that variation; the published tables cannot tell whether crashes became more serious or admissions were recorded more completely.

In 2020 the count of deaths fell 24% below its pre-pandemic trend, outside the trend's 95% prediction range, while deaths per tonne of road fuel stayed within theirs: over the year as a whole, deaths fell roughly in line with fuel sales. In 2023 and 2024 deaths per tonne of fuel were 17% and 16% above their trend, beyond its range, but fuel sold only stands in for distance driven. On interurban roads, where vehicle-kilometres are measured, deaths per kilometre in 2023 were 5% above their pre-pandemic trend, within its range (−4% to +15%).

Part of the summer peak follows fuel sales: July has 1.22× the deaths of an average month, and 1.12× per tonne of road fuel.

Frequency and severity across drivers, vehicles and roads

The split into crashes per unit of exposure and deaths per crash shows whether a group's excess of deaths comes from crashing more often or from deadlier crashes. For older drivers and heavy trucks the excess lies in how deadly a crash is; for young drivers and motorcycles it lies in how often crashes happen. Men and conventional roads differ on both counts, men mainly in how deadly a crash is and conventional roads mainly in how often crashes happen.

Car drivers aged 75 and over who are in an injury crash are killed 3.93× (2.98–5.18) as often as those aged 35 to 54. Kilometres by age exist only for cars registered to owners of each age, so the per-kilometre ratios for drivers are given as ranges; men and women are compared per licence holder, since no source gives kilometres by sex.

The rows for vehicle types count vehicles in fatal crashes, whoever died. In the fatal crashes a heavy truck is in, its own occupants account for 0.18 deaths per crash, so in at least 82% of them everyone killed was outside the truck. Conventional roads have more deaths per measured kilometre than motorways and dual carriageways, mostly through more crashes per kilometre (Long-run trends).

In Spain outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, an injury crash in which the police recorded inappropriate speed has 2.00× (1.69–2.36) the deaths of other crashes on the same kind of road in the same year, an association in a record written after the crash. Among the recorded factors, alcohol rose from 5.5% to 8.2% of interurban injury crashes between 2014 and 2023, and inappropriate speed fell from 10.0% to 6.9% of all injury crashes, both recorded consistently throughout; distraction on urban streets and drug-related crashes cannot be followed across the period.

Crash records and severity models in Catalonia and Barcelona

The Catalan file holds every crash with a death or serious injury: 24,478 between 2010 and 2023, of which 12.6% were fatal (19.2% on interurban roads and 6.9% on urban streets). Barcelona's records list the people in each crash: in 2025, 6.5% of pedestrians and 3.3% of motorcyclists in recorded crashes were seriously or fatally injured, against 3 of 4,671 car drivers (0.06%). Neither source measures exposure, so these shares describe how severe the outcome was once a crash had been recorded.

From these records the study built three models that rank recorded cases by severity. Each was tested once on later records not used to fit it or choose its settings, and compared with a simple table of the share of severe outcomes in each group. Ranking is measured by the ROC-AUC, the probability that a model scores a randomly chosen severe case above a less severe one: 0.5 is chance and 1 a perfect ranking. Two of the three rank better than their tables.

The Catalonia severity model estimates which crashes with a death or serious injury were fatal. On the crashes of 2023, the file's last year, it reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.79 against 0.70 for a table of fatal shares by crash subtype and by urban or interurban zone, and its predicted probabilities agree with the observed fatal shares.

The Barcelona person-severity model estimates which people in Barcelona crashes were seriously or fatally injured. On the final months of 2025 it reaches 0.84 against 0.78 for a table of shares by road-user role and vehicle group. Its probabilities are right on average but do not match the observed shares across groups of people (calibration slope 0.63, where 1 is perfect agreement), so it is used only to rank people. The third, the Barcelona crash model, ranked crashes no better than a table of the share with a serious or fatal injury by accident type (0.74 against 0.73), so the study reports that table in its place.

External validation

A model can rank cases well in the population it was trained on and fail elsewhere. In the external validation, the Catalonia severity model, restricted to the variables both sources record in the same way, was trained on the Catalan file and applied to 67,971 crashes with a death or serious injury recorded by DGT outside Catalonia, 10,457 of them fatal within 24 hours. It ranks those crashes almost as well as a model trained on the DGT records themselves: a ROC-AUC of 0.708 against 0.712.

Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes differ from the rest of Spain's, however: in DGT's own records, 12.2% of them were fatal within 24 hours in Catalonia, against 15.4% elsewhere. Use of the model across Spain is therefore not established. Within Catalonia, the Catalonia severity model trained on the rest of the region scores 0.642 on Barcelona city's crashes, against 0.656 for a model trained on the city's own crashes (difference −0.015): the change of population itself costs no measurable ranking, and Barcelona's crashes are harder to rank than the rest of Catalonia's even for a model trained there.

Further analyses, data and methods

An association analysis of DGT crash records describes which recorded circumstances go with a fatal outcome once an injury crash has happened. A monthly deaths forecast is slightly less accurate than repeating the previous year's count in ordinary years (6.6% error against 5.9%) and far more accurate in the lockdown years (7.3% against 19.2%); a comparison with its forecast detects a fall of 15% or a rise of 17% in one year's deaths four times in five. The study of the 2006 points-based licence estimates a fall in monthly deaths of about 7% when it came into force. A simpler step model fitted at 15 Julys gives 2006 the largest fall, but only narrowly, and against forecasts made before each July it ranks fourth of 15, so the series cannot attribute the fall to the licence.

Data sources and scope describes each source and sets out the limits of the data; the sources share no record identifier, so no record is linked across them. The methodology defines the terms and explains how rates, police records and models are built and read. Every comparison in the study between an outcome and a circumstance is an association, and none estimates a causal effect.
