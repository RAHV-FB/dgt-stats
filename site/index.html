Road safety in Spain

What changes when you stop counting crashes and start measuring road risk: seven questions answered from DGT data, each with the denominator that decides it, and models of speed, distraction and alcohol that ask which enforcement would save most.

A count of crashes or deaths says how many. It does not say how dangerous the roads were, because it moves with how much people drove, who was driving and in what. This site divides the same DGT counts by residents, licence holders, vehicles, kilometres and road fuel, and asks what changes. On every question below the answer changes size, and on several it changes sign. Each answer can then be split in two, how often crashes happen and how deadly they are, and the split shows where the extra deaths come from: for older drivers, men and heavy trucks it is how deadly the crash is; for young drivers, motorcycles and conventional roads it is mostly how often crashes happen. Speed acts on both, and the last page asks what a speed law would do.

Finding 1Since 2019, death risk has not measurably changed; recorded hospital admissions have risen

Deaths in 2024 were +1.7% on 2019 as a count, −1.9% per resident, −3.4% per registered vehicle and +3.5% per tonne of road fuel, all within an ordinary year's variation. People admitted to hospital rose +11.0%, beyond it.

Annual DGT totals over INE residents, the driver census, the registered fleet and CORES road fuel, indexed to 2019, with intervals that allow for the yearly scatter of each count.

Finding 2The pandemic dip was less driving; since then risk per kilometre has not jumped

As a count, 2020 deaths were −24% against the pre-pandemic trend and back on it by 2022. Per tonne of road fuel 2020 was on trend (0.97×): the fall was the traffic. On interurban roads in 2023, deaths per tonne of fuel were +13% on trend, outside its interval, but per kilometre measured +5%, inside it. The counts cannot yet tell whether the pre-2020 decline went on or stalled; they rule out a jump.

Joinpoint quasi-Poisson trends fitted to 1993–2019 and projected, re-run on the Ministerio de Transportes' measured interurban vehicle-kilometres.

Finding 3The summer peak is mostly traffic; the autumn excess is not

Raw deaths in July are 1.22× and in August 1.14× the average month. Per unit of petrol, which cars and motorcycles burn, they are 1.06× and 0.98×. In the April 2020 lockdown deaths fell 68% and petrol sales 76%: fewer deaths, not safer roads.

Monthly deaths since 2014 against monthly road fuel, petrol and toll-motorway traffic, in quasi-Poisson models with year and month effects.

Finding 4Young drivers die more per km because they crash more; older drivers because a crash kills them more often

Per kilometre, car drivers aged 75 and over are killed 3.99× as often as those aged 35 to 54: 1.02× the crashes per km (but 1.43× those of drivers aged 65 to 74) × 3.93× the deaths per crash. Drivers aged 18 to 34: 2.43×, from 2.57× the crashes × 0.95× the deaths per crash. Men, per licence holder: 3.63×, from 1.41× the crashes, about their extra travel, × 2.57× the deaths per crash.

DGT driver tables over the driver census and DGT's 2024 kilometres by owner age; MOVILIA trips by sex as a bounded travel proxy.

Finding 5A heavy truck is ten times a car, or two and a half times, depending on the divisor

Per circulating vehicle a heavy truck is in a fatal crash 10.3 times as often as a car. Per kilometre driven it is 2.5 times. For every fatal crash a truck is in, 0.18 of its own occupants die, so most of the danger a truck carries is to other people.

DGT's 2022 kilometre estimates divided into the same year's involvement counts, with exact Poisson intervals.

Finding 6Where speed is recorded, a crash is twice as likely to kill

Injury crashes in which the police recorded inappropriate speed kill 3.41× as many people per crash as the rest, and 2.00× once the comparison is made on the same kind of road in the same year. That is an association: the record is written after the fact, and is likelier to be written when someone has died.

DGT's speed-factor report against totals from the microdata for the same provinces, which reproduce the report's own totals exactly.

Finding 7Alcohol is rising on interurban roads; distraction cannot be trended in towns

Recorded alcohol went from 5.5% to 8.2% of interurban injury crashes between 2014 and 2023, and inappropriate speed from 10.0% to 6.9% of all of them, both on a consistent record. Urban distraction jumps and falls by half in single years, and drugs are too few to compare.

DGT's concurrent-factor tables, with a recording-break rule applied to every year-to-year change before any trend is read.

Finding 8Keeping to today's limits saves more than any single new limit; a higher limit kept still costs lives

On the published evidence and the speeds measured in Spain, if every driver now above the limit on autopistas, autovías and conventional roads kept to it, about 335 fewer people a year would die there, 280 of them on conventional roads; 80 km/h on conventional roads would save about 123. Motorways at 140 km/h with every driver keeping to it would cost 39. A validated forecast says the first year's count would show full compliance almost every time and 80 km/h 45% of the time.

Spanish baselines and radar speeds, published dose-response evidence and DGT's values of a life, all sourced; a Poisson forecast tested against machine learning.

Finding 9Distraction is in the most fatal crashes, but causes fewer deaths than alcohol

If no driver were distracted, about 266 fewer people a year would die, 15% of 1,779. The police record distraction in 35% of interurban fatal crashes, more than any other factor, but a distracted driver is about twice as likely to crash, so only half of those crashes are caused by it. If the record misses as much distraction as cameras in cars find, the figure could reach 640.

DGT's record of the factors in fatal crashes, with the crash risk measured by cameras in drivers' own cars (Dingus et al., 2016).

Finding 10Drink- and drug-driving is the largest avoidable share of deaths

Without drink- or drug-driving about 582 fewer people a year would die (497 to 644), 33% of deaths: 479 from alcohol and 104 from drugs. A driver over the limit is in 29% of interurban fatal crashes, and most of them are far over it, so almost all of those deaths are caused by the alcohol.

DGT's record of fatal crashes, the forensic toxicology of killed drivers (INTCF) and the risks the EU's DRUID project measured.

Finding 11Alcohol is the largest prize; speed cameras are the surest gain

Removing each factor would save about 582 lives a year for alcohol and drugs, 449 for speeding and 266 for distraction; saving 100 takes removing 17%, 20% or 38% of each. Automatic speed cameras cut fatal and serious crashes by 41% to 47% where they stand, in Spain too; Spain already runs 6.7 million breath tests a year, and phone bans changed total deaths by too little to measure. Most of every gain is on interurban roads.

The three models side by side, with the published evaluations of each kind of enforcement.

What connects them

Every comparison on the site can be split the same way: deaths for a given exposure are crashes for that exposure times deaths per crash. Set side by side, the splits show that the extra deaths do not all come from the same place.

For older drivers, men and heavy trucks the excess is in how deadly a crash is: the body that absorbs it, or the mass that strikes the other party. For young drivers, motorcycles and conventional roads it is mostly in how often crashes happen. The long run fell through severity: between 1996 and 2024 deaths per tonne of road fuel fell 76%, injury crashes per tonne 13% and deaths per injury crash 73%, a split that depends on how completely slight injuries are recorded, though their product does not.

Speed acts on both factors, and more on the second: in the Power Model a 1% rise in the average speed on interurban roads brings about 1.6% more injury crashes and 4.7% more deaths. It is also the factor on which Spanish drivers stray furthest from the rule: in 2022 57% of cars measured on conventional roads were above 90 km/h. That is why the site ends with a simulator of speed laws, set beside models of distraction and of alcohol and drugs, and says how likely the counts are to show a change.

What this site does not claim

It attributes no cause from Spanish data. The public microdata have one row per crash and no driver, vehicle or person records, so every factor here is an association, and a policy or campaign effect is never read off a time series. The models apply dose-response evidence and crash risks measured elsewhere to Spanish baselines and to the police record of fatal crashes. Two analyses built earlier in the project are kept as supporting material because they are careful but outside this question: a model of crash severity and a test of the 2006 points licence whose headline did not survive its own falsification tests. Road design and municipal hotspots are not analysed: the files carry no road geometry, traffic volume or coordinates.

How to read the numbers

Counts are DGT's consolidated figures. An injury crash is one with at least one person killed or injured, and deaths are counted within 30 days. Every rate names its denominator, because the denominator is usually where the answer comes from. Sources, definitions, the reconciliation checks and the assumptions tested are on the data page.
