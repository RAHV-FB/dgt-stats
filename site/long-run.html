Spain

Long-run trends in road deaths, 1993–2024

Road deaths in Spain since 1993 as a count, per registered vehicle, per tonne of road fuel and, on interurban roads, per measured kilometre, with trends fitted up to 2019 and projected forward.

Road deaths in Spain fell slowly until 2003, steeply for the next 10 years, and have not fallen since 2013. Between 1996 and 2024 deaths per tonne of road fuel sold fell 76%, mostly through deaths per injury crash, which fell 73%; injury crashes per tonne fell only 13%. Set against the trend fitted up to 2019 and projected forward, the count of deaths in 2020 was 24% lower, outside the trend's range, while deaths per tonne of road fuel stayed within their own range: over the year as a whole, deaths fell roughly in line with fuel sales. In 2023 and 2024 deaths per tonne of fuel were 17% and 16% above their trend, beyond its range, but on interurban roads deaths per measured kilometre in 2023 were 5% above their pre-pandemic trend, within its range.

The trend from 1993 to 2019

Each series is described by a trend that changes by a constant percentage each year, with up to three turning points placed where the data put them. The turning points describe the series; the model attaches no cause to them. Each trend is fitted up to 2019 and projected forward on the assumption that its last segment continued, with a 95% prediction interval: the range within which a year would be expected to fall if the trend had continued with its ordinary scatter.

The three measures describe the same history, with turning points that fall within 2 years of each other: a slow decline to 2002–2003, then a fall of between 10.7% and 13.4% a year over the next 9–10 years. Between 1993 and 2013 the annual count fell 74%, from 6,378 deaths to 1,680. In the last segment the count and occupant deaths per registered vehicle no longer fall (both intervals include no change), while deaths per tonne of road fuel still fall 2.2% a year.

Trend specification

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with quasi-likelihood errors: the variance is the mean multiplied by a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never set below 1. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel are fitted as death counts with the logarithm of the fleet or of fuel as an offset, and the figure multiplies their trends back by each year's fleet or fuel. For each number of turning points from none to three, every placement that leaves segments at least four years long is fitted and the best kept. The number of turning points is chosen by QBIC, a Bayesian information criterion in which the Poisson log-likelihood is divided by the dispersion of the largest model, taking the simplest model within 2 points of the lowest. The dispersion factors of the chosen models are 7.3 for the count, 4.5 per registered vehicle and 2.8 per tonne of fuel. Segment intervals are 95% confidence intervals for the annual change. The 95% prediction interval of a projected year combines the uncertainty of the fitted trend with the overdispersed scatter of a single year around it.

Crash frequency and severity, 1996–2024

Deaths per tonne of road fuel are the product of two factors that can move separately: injury crashes per tonne, which measure how often crashes happen for a given amount of traffic, and deaths per injury crash, which measure how deadly a crash is once it has happened. Between 1996 and 2024 the first fell 13% and the second 73%, so most of the 76% fall in deaths per tonne lies in how deadly crashes are.

The split depends on how completely crashes with only slight injuries are recorded: if fewer of them are recorded, crashes per tonne fall and deaths per crash rise by the same factor, leaving their product unchanged. The fall in deaths per tonne is therefore the firmer result, and its division between frequency and severity the more fragile one.

The count of deaths has been back within its range since 2021. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle stayed below their range in 2020 and 2021: the fleet counts vehicles whether they are used or not, and it grew in 2020 while deaths fell.

The excess of deaths per tonne of fuel in 2023 and 2024 rests on the projection's assumption that the kilometres a tonne of fuel represents (see road fuel as a measure of traffic) kept changing after 2019 at their 2011–2019 pace, an assumption no series can check on all roads. In a hypothetical case where kilometres per tonne grew an extra 1% a year from 2020, deaths per tonne of fuel would be 13% above the trend in 2023, still outside the range, and 10% above it in 2024, inside it; with an extra 2% a year, 8% and 5% above, both inside the range. The 2023 excess would fall within the range with an extra 1.4 percentage points a year, and the 2024 excess with 0.7. These growth rates are illustrative assumptions.

Interurban roads: deaths per measured kilometre

The Ministerio de Transportes measures, by traffic counts, the vehicle-kilometres travelled each year on the interurban roads of the State, the regions and the provincial councils. Divided into interurban deaths, they give the only available series whose numerator and denominator cover nearly the same roads. The kilometres leave out roads run by municipalities and other bodies, which DGT's crash records identify: between 8.7% and 11.2% of interurban deaths each year from 2016 to 2024 occurred on them, 10.1% in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023. The rate's level is therefore too high by about that share, but the comparison between 2019 and 2023 moves by less than 1%. Before 2016 the share cannot be measured.

The same trend model, applied to interurban deaths per measured kilometre over 2008–2019 (the kilometre series is comparable only from 2008), places the last turning point in 2013 and finds a fall of 1.8% a year after it. Projected forward with each year's kilometres, 2023 comes out at +5% against the trend, within its range of −4% to +15%. In 2020, when measured kilometres fell 22%, deaths per kilometre were on trend (1.00×).

Divided instead by national road fuel, with a trend fitted the same way, the same deaths were 13% above their trend in 2023, beyond its range. Fuel is sold for every road, towns included, so this ratio serves only as a check on road fuel as a measure of traffic, and it shows the two measures parting: measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national road fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% a year over 2019–2023 (from 8,741 to 9,420).

The measured kilometres also separate road types. Counting only deaths and injury crashes on the networks whose traffic is measured, conventional roads in 2023 had 7.4 deaths per billion vehicle-kilometres and motorways and dual carriageways 2.1: 3.45× as many on conventional roads. That ratio combines 2.28× as many injury crashes per kilometre with 1.51× as many deaths per injury crash, so more of the difference lies in how often crashes happen than in how deadly they are.

Fuel and kilometres after 2019

From 2020 the two measures that relate deaths to traffic disagree. Measured kilometres are the better denominator, but they cover only the interurban networks and end in 2023. Fuel covers every road, but its projection rests on an assumption about kilometres per tonne that cannot be checked, and on interurban roads kilometres per tonne of fuel grew faster after 2019 than before. If they did so on all roads, part of the excess in deaths per tonne of fuel would reflect more driving per tonne. Up to 2023, interurban deaths per kilometre show no rise beyond their trend's range; on all roads, deaths per kilometre are not measured.

Limitations. The 30-day death series is used as DGT publishes it for every year since 1993; whether its definition changed over that period has not been checked.
