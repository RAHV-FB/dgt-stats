Spain

Long-run trends in road deaths, 1993–2024

Road deaths in Spain since 1993 as a count, per registered vehicle, per tonne of road fuel and, on interurban roads, per measured kilometre, with trends fitted up to 2019 and projected forward.

Road deaths in Spain fell slowly until 2003, then by 10.9% a year for the next 10 years. Since 2013 the fitted trend shows no clear rise or fall. Between 1996 and 2024, deaths per tonne of road fuel sold fell 76%, mostly because crashes became less deadly: deaths per injury crash fell 73% and injury crashes per tonne 13%. Against the trend fitted up to 2019, the count of deaths in 2020 was 24% lower, outside the trend's range, while deaths per tonne of fuel stayed within theirs, because fuel sales fell too. In 2023 and 2024 deaths per tonne of fuel were 17% and 16% above their trend, beyond its range; on interurban roads, where kilometres are measured directly, deaths per kilometre in 2023 were 5% above their trend, within its range.

The trend from 1993 to 2019

The count of deaths, occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel tell the same story, with turning points within 2 years of each other: a slow decline to 2002–2003, then a fall of between 10.7% and 13.4% a year over the next 9–10 years. Between 1993 and 2013 the annual count fell 74%, from 6,378 deaths to 1,680. Since then neither the count nor occupant deaths per registered vehicle has a clear trend (both intervals include no change), while deaths per tonne of road fuel still fell 2.2% a year up to 2019.

Each trend changes by a constant percentage a year between up to three turning points placed where the data put them; the turning points describe the series and say nothing about causes. Each is fitted up to 2019 and projected forward with a 95% prediction interval: the range a year would be expected to fall in if the last segment had continued with its ordinary scatter.

How the trends were fitted

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with quasi-likelihood errors: the variance is the mean multiplied by a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never set below 1. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel are fitted as death counts with the logarithm of the fleet or of fuel as an offset, and the figure multiplies their trends back by each year's fleet or fuel. For each number of turning points from none to three, every placement that leaves segments at least four years long is fitted and the best kept. The number of turning points is chosen by QBIC, a Bayesian information criterion in which the Poisson log-likelihood is divided by the dispersion of the largest model, taking the simplest model within 2 points of the lowest. The dispersion factors of the chosen models are 7.3 for the count, 4.5 per registered vehicle and 2.8 per tonne of fuel. Segment intervals are 95% confidence intervals for the annual change. The 95% prediction interval of a projected year combines the uncertainty of the fitted trend with the overdispersed scatter of a single year around it.

Crash frequency and severity, 1996–2024

Deaths per tonne of road fuel are the product of two parts that can move separately: injury crashes per tonne (crash frequency for a given amount of traffic) and deaths per injury crash (severity once a crash has happened). Between 1996 and 2024 injury crashes per tonne fell 13% and deaths per injury crash 73%, so most of the 76% fall in deaths per tonne came from crashes becoming less deadly.

The split depends on how completely crashes with only slight injuries are recorded: if fewer of them are recorded, crashes per tonne fall and deaths per crash rise by the same factor, leaving their product unchanged. The fall in deaths per tonne is therefore firmer than its division between frequency and severity.

The count of deaths has been back within its range since 2021. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle stayed below their range in 2020 and 2021, because the fleet counts vehicles whether they are used or not, and it grew in 2020 while deaths fell. The excess of deaths per tonne of fuel in 2023 and 2024 assumes that the kilometres a tonne of fuel represents (see road fuel as a measure of traffic) kept changing after 2019 at their 2011–2019 pace, which no series can check on all roads.

If kilometres per tonne of fuel changed faster

In a hypothetical case where kilometres per tonne grew an extra 1% a year from 2020, deaths per tonne of fuel would be 13% above the trend in 2023, still outside the range, and 10% above it in 2024, inside it; with an extra 2% a year, 8% and 5% above, both inside the range. The 2023 excess would fall within the range with an extra 1.4 percentage points a year, and the 2024 excess with 0.7. These growth rates are illustrative assumptions.

Interurban roads: deaths per measured kilometre

The Ministerio de Transportes measures, by traffic counts, the vehicle-kilometres travelled each year on the interurban roads of the State, the regions and the provincial councils. Divided into interurban deaths, they give the only available series whose numerator and denominator cover nearly the same roads. The kilometres leave out roads run by municipalities and other bodies, which DGT's crash records identify: between 8.7% and 11.2% of interurban deaths each year from 2016 to 2024 occurred on them, 10.1% in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023. The rate's level is therefore too high by about that share, but the comparison between 2019 and 2023 moves by less than 1%. Before 2016 the share cannot be measured.

The same trend model, applied to interurban deaths per measured kilometre over 2008–2019 (the kilometre series is comparable only from 2008), places the last turning point in 2013 and finds a fall of 1.8% a year after it. Projected forward with each year's kilometres, 2023 comes out at +5% against the trend, within its range of −4% to +15%. In 2020, when measured kilometres fell 22%, deaths per kilometre were on trend (1.00×).

Divided instead by national road fuel, with a trend fitted the same way, the same deaths were 13% above their trend in 2023, beyond its range. Fuel is sold for every road, towns included, so this ratio serves only as a check on road fuel as a measure of traffic, and it shows the two measures parting: measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national road fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% a year over 2019–2023 (from 8,741 to 9,420). If kilometres per tonne grew that much faster on all roads, part of the recent excess in deaths per tonne of fuel would reflect more driving per tonne. On all roads, deaths per kilometre are not measured.

The measured kilometres also separate road types. Counting only deaths and injury crashes on the networks whose traffic is measured, conventional roads in 2023 had 7.4 deaths per billion vehicle-kilometres and motorways and dual carriageways 2.1: 3.45× as many on conventional roads. That ratio combines 2.28× as many injury crashes per kilometre with 1.51× as many deaths per injury crash, so more of the difference lies in how often crashes happen than in how deadly they are.

LimitationsThe 30-day death series is used as DGT publishes it for every year since 1993; whether its definition changed over that period has not been checked.
