Over time

Long-run trends in road deaths, 1993–2024

Road deaths in Spain fell slowly until 2003, then by 10.9% a year to 2013, and have shown no clear rise or fall since: 1,680 people died in 2013 and 1,785 in 2024. Deaths per tonne of road fuel sold, which stands in for traffic, fell 76% between 1996 and 2024. Whether the last years are worse depends on the comparison. Against 2019, deaths per tonne of fuel in 2024 were 3.5% higher, within ordinary year-to-year variation (Since 2019). Against their 2011–2019 decline projected forward, they were 16% above the trend in 2023 and 14% in 2024, outside its range, or 10% and 8% if the trend starts in 2013, at the edge of the range in 2023 and within the range in 2024. On interurban roads, deaths per measured kilometre in 2023 were 5% above their trend, within its range.

Deaths fell steeply from 2003 to 2013 and have levelled off since

The count of deaths, occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel turn at about the same time, within 2 years of each other: a slower decline to 2002–2003 (0.7% a year as a count, 4.4% to 4.8% a year as rates), then a fall of between 10.7% and 13.4% a year over the next 9–10 years. Between 1993 and 2013 the annual count fell 74%, from 6,378 deaths to 1,680. Since then neither the count nor occupant deaths per registered vehicle has a clear trend (both intervals include no change), while deaths per tonne of road fuel fell 2.2% a year from 2011 to 2019 (1.2% a year over 2013–2019). Fitted on its own, the count of 2013–2019 rises 1.2% a year (95% interval −0.2% to +2.7%), so a slight rise since 2013 cannot be ruled out.

Each trend changes by a constant percentage a year between up to three turning points placed where the data put them; the turning points describe the series and say nothing about causes. The intervals in the table take the turning points as known.

How the trends were fitted

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with quasi-likelihood errors: the variance is the mean multiplied by a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never set below 1. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel are fitted as death counts with the logarithm of the fleet or of fuel as an offset, and the figure multiplies their trends back by each year's fleet or fuel. For each number of turning points from none to three, every placement that leaves segments at least four years long is fitted and the best kept. The number of turning points is chosen by QBIC, a Bayesian information criterion in which the Poisson log-likelihood is divided by the dispersion of the largest model, taking the simplest model within 2 points of the lowest. The dispersion factors of the chosen models are 7.3 for the count, 4.5 per registered vehicle and 2.8 per tonne of fuel. Segment intervals are 95% confidence intervals for the annual change, from Student's t with the fit's residual degrees of freedom.

The projection continues the last segment: the trend of the years from the last turning point to 2019, refitted on those years alone. Its scatter is that of those years: 1.5 for the count (the factor that Since 2019 uses for an ordinary year's variation in deaths), 2.8 per registered vehicle and 1.6 per tonne of fuel, where the whole fit's factors also carry the poorer fit of the early years. The 95% prediction interval of a projected year combines the uncertainty of the refitted trend with the scatter of a single year around it, with Student's t quantiles for the five, five and seven residual degrees of freedom of the three refits.

Deaths per tonne of fuel fell by three quarters, but the records cannot split the fall between fewer crashes and less deadly ones

Deaths per tonne of road fuel are the product of two parts that can move separately: casualties of a given kind per tonne (how often, for a given amount of traffic) and deaths per such casualty (how deadly). Between 1996 and 2024 deaths per tonne fell 76%. Counted by injury crashes, crashes per tonne fell 13% and deaths per injury crash 73%. Counted by people admitted to hospital, admissions per tonne fell 79% and deaths per admission rose 15%. By the first split most of the fall is in how deadly a crash is; by the second all of it is in how often people are seriously hurt.

Each split depends on how completely the lesser casualties are recorded: if fewer of them are recorded, the frequency falls and the deaths per casualty rise by the same factor, leaving their product unchanged. The injury-crash series has two steps inside these years: injury crashes rose 13% from 1997 to 1998 (21% on interurban roads), and urban injury crashes rose 26% between 2013 and 2016 while interurban ones fell 2%.

The count of deaths was 25% below its trend in 2020 and 18% below in 2021, outside the range, and has been within it since 2022; in 2024 it was 8% below the trend. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle were 25% below their trend in 2020, outside its range, and 15% below in 2021, within it: their range is wider because occupant deaths are fewer and scatter more around their 2013–2019 trend. Whether a year this far below trend lies outside the range depends mostly on the scatter it is read against: with the continuous trend and the scatter of the whole 1993–2019 fit, which the poorer fit of the early years inflates, the count in 2021 lies within its range and the per-vehicle measure outside it (table of other projections below).

Deaths per tonne of fuel stayed within their range in 2020 and 2021, because fuel sales fell too, and were 16% and 14% above the trend in 2023 and 2024, outside its range. Against 2019 itself, deaths per tonne of fuel in 2024 were 3.5% higher, within the variation of an ordinary year (Since 2019). The two readings differ because the trend kept falling after 2019, about 2% a year and 10% in all by 2024: the trend asks whether the decline of 2011–2019 went on, the comparison with 2019 whether the rate rose.

The excess in 2023 and 2024 depends on where the last segment of the trend starts. The search puts the last turning point in 2011; deaths per tonne fell 10% from 2011 to 2013 and then 1.2% a year over 2013–2019, so a trend started in 2013 leaves smaller excesses (table below). Refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly, the search finds the same turning points, and 2023 and 2024 still lie above the range. The excess also assumes that the kilometres a tonne of fuel represents kept changing after 2019 at their pace of 2011–2019, which no series can check on all roads (road fuel as a measure of traffic). Had they grown faster by 1.3 percentage points a year from 2020, the 2023 excess would fall within the range; for 2024, 0.6 points would be enough. On interurban roads, where kilometres are measured, kilometres per tonne of national fuel did grow faster by more than that (below).

On interurban roads, deaths per measured kilometre stayed within their trend's range

The Ministerio de Transportes measures, by traffic counts, the vehicle-kilometres travelled each year on the interurban roads of the State, the regions and the provincial councils. Dividing interurban deaths by them gives the only available series whose numerator and denominator cover nearly the same roads. The latest year, 2023, is partly estimated: the yearbook keeps the regional and provincial networks' lengths of the year before and takes the State network's traffic as the reference for all networks. Over 2008–2019 (the kilometre series is comparable only from 2008) the same trend model places the last turning point in 2013, and the trend refitted to 2013–2019 falls 1.8% a year. Projected forward with each year's kilometres, 2023 comes out 5% above the trend, inside its range of ±12%. In 2020, when measured kilometres fell 22%, deaths per kilometre were on trend (a ratio of 1.00).

The kilometres leave out roads run by municipalities and other bodies, which DGT's crash records identify: between 8.7% and 11.2% of interurban deaths each year from 2016 to 2024 occurred on them, 10.1% in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023. The rate's level is therefore too high by about that share, but the comparison between 2019 and 2023 moves by less than 1%. Before 2016 the share cannot be measured.

Divided instead by national road fuel, with a trend fitted the same way, the same deaths were 13% above their trend in 2023, against a range of ±12%. Fuel is sold for every road, towns included, so this ratio serves only as a check on road fuel as a measure of traffic, and it shows the two measures parting: measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national road fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% a year over 2019–2023 (from 8,741 to 9,420), 1.4 percentage points a year faster. Had kilometres per tonne grown that much faster on all roads, the 2023 excess in deaths per tonne of fuel (above) would fall within its range. The other source points the other way: DGT's inspection-based kilometres, which start in 2022, put kilometres per tonne of fuel 3.0% lower in 2024 than in 2022 (road fuel as a measure of traffic). On all roads, deaths per kilometre are not measured.

Conventional roads have 3 to 4 times the deaths per kilometre of motorways

The measured kilometres also separate road types. Counting only deaths and injury crashes on the networks whose traffic is measured, conventional roads, single and dual carriageway, had 7.41 deaths per billion vehicle-kilometres in 2023 and motorways (autopistas and autovías) 2.15: 3.45 times as many on conventional roads (95% interval 3.03–3.92). Over 2016–2023 the ratio ran from 3.04 to 3.84. In 2023 it combines 2.28 times as many injury crashes per kilometre with 1.51 times as many deaths per injury crash; that split depends on how completely injury crashes are recorded on each kind of road (frequency and severity).

Limitations. DGT estimated the 30-day deaths of 1993 to 2010 from deaths within 24 hours, with correction factors drawn from following a sample of people admitted to hospital, and has counted them since 2011 by matching crash records with the register of deaths (DGT's accident yearbook, annex on the revised method). The trends refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly throughout, place the turning points of the count and of deaths per tonne of fuel in the same years (the table of other projections above).
