Spain

Long-run trends in road deaths, 1993–2024

Road deaths in Spain since 1993 as a count, per registered vehicle, per tonne of road fuel and, on interurban roads, per measured kilometre, with trends fitted up to 2019 and projected forward.

Road deaths in Spain fell slowly until 2003, then by 10.9% a year for the next 10 years. Since 2013 the fitted trend shows no clear rise or fall. Between 1996 and 2024, deaths per tonne of road fuel sold fell 76%; how that fall divides between fewer crashes and less deadly ones depends on which casualties are counted. Against the trend of 2013–2019 continued, the count of deaths was 25% lower in 2020 and 18% lower in 2021, outside the trend's range, and has been within it since 2022. Deaths per tonne of fuel stayed within their range in 2020, because fuel sales fell too. Projected from their 2011–2019 decline, deaths per tonne of fuel in 2023 and 2024 were 16% and 14% above the trend, outside its range; projected from 2013–2019, when the decline was slower, they were 10% and 8% above it, at the edge of the range in 2023 and within the range in 2024. On interurban roads, where kilometres are measured directly, deaths per kilometre in 2023 were 5% above their trend, within its range.

The trend from 1993 to 2019

The count of deaths, occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel tell the same story, with turning points within 2 years of each other: a slower decline to 2002–2003 (0.7% a year as a count, 4.4% to 4.8% a year as rates), then a fall of between 10.7% and 13.4% a year over the next 9–10 years. Between 1993 and 2013 the annual count fell 74%, from 6,378 deaths to 1,680. Since then neither the count nor occupant deaths per registered vehicle has a clear trend (both intervals include no change), while deaths per tonne of road fuel fell 2.2% a year from 2011 to 2019 (1.2% a year over 2013–2019).

Each trend changes by a constant percentage a year between up to three turning points placed where the data put them; the turning points describe the series and say nothing about causes. The intervals in the table take the turning points as known. Fitted on its own, the count of 2013–2019 rises 1.2% a year (95% interval −0.2% to +2.7%), so a slight rise since 2013 cannot be ruled out. Each trend is fitted up to 2019 and its last segment projected forward with a 95% prediction interval: the range a year would be expected to fall in if the last segment had continued with its ordinary scatter.

How the trends were fitted

Each series is a segmented log-linear Poisson regression (a joinpoint model) with quasi-likelihood errors: the variance is the mean multiplied by a dispersion factor estimated from the data and never set below 1. Occupant deaths per registered vehicle and deaths per tonne of road fuel are fitted as death counts with the logarithm of the fleet or of fuel as an offset, and the figure multiplies their trends back by each year's fleet or fuel. For each number of turning points from none to three, every placement that leaves segments at least four years long is fitted and the best kept. The number of turning points is chosen by QBIC, a Bayesian information criterion in which the Poisson log-likelihood is divided by the dispersion of the largest model, taking the simplest model within 2 points of the lowest. The dispersion factors of the chosen models are 7.3 for the count, 4.5 per registered vehicle and 2.8 per tonne of fuel. Segment intervals are 95% confidence intervals for the annual change, from Student's t with the fit's residual degrees of freedom.

The projection continues the last segment: the trend of the years from the last turning point to 2019, refitted on those years alone. Its scatter is that of those years: 1.5 for the count, 2.8 per registered vehicle and 1.6 per tonne of fuel, where the whole fit's factors also carry the poorer fit of the early years. The 95% prediction interval of a projected year combines the uncertainty of the refitted trend with the scatter of a single year around it, with Student's t quantiles for the five, five and seven residual degrees of freedom of the three refits.

Crash frequency and severity, 1996–2024

Deaths per tonne of road fuel are the product of two parts that can move separately: casualties of a given kind per tonne (how often, for a given amount of traffic) and deaths per such casualty (how deadly). Between 1996 and 2024 deaths per tonne fell 76%. Counted by injury crashes, crashes per tonne fell 13% and deaths per injury crash 73%. Counted by people admitted to hospital, admissions per tonne fell 79% and deaths per admission rose 15%. By the first split most of the fall is in how deadly a crash is; by the second all of it is in how often people are seriously hurt. The fall in deaths per tonne is firm; how it divides cannot be told from these series.

Each split depends on how completely the lesser casualties are recorded: if fewer of them are recorded, the frequency falls and the deaths per casualty rise by the same factor, leaving their product unchanged. The injury-crash series has two steps inside these years: injury crashes rose 13% from 1997 to 1998 (21% on interurban roads), and urban injury crashes rose 26% between 2013 and 2016 while interurban ones fell 2%.

Occupant deaths per registered vehicle were 15% below their trend in 2021, against 18% for the count, but within their range, which is wider because occupant deaths are fewer and scattered more around their 2013–2019 trend. Whether a year this far below trend lies outside the range depends mostly on the scatter it is read against: with the continuous trend and the scatter of the whole 1993–2019 fit, which the poorer fit of the early years inflates, the count in 2021 lies within its range and the per-vehicle measure outside it (table below).

The excess of deaths per tonne of fuel in 2023 and 2024 depends on where the last segment of its trend starts. The search puts the last turning point in 2011; deaths per tonne fell 10% from 2011 to 2013 and then 1.2% a year over 2013–2019. Refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly, the search finds the same turning points, and 2023 and 2024 still lie above the range. Against 2019 itself, deaths per tonne of fuel in 2024 were 3.5% higher, within the variation of an ordinary year (Trends since 2019): that comparison sets 2024 against the level of 2019, this one against where the decline of 2011–2019 would have taken it. The comparison with the trend assumes that the kilometres a tonne of fuel represents (see road fuel as a measure of traffic) kept changing after 2019 at their pace of 2011–2019; the comparison with 2019 assumes that they did not change. No series can check either on all roads.

If kilometres per tonne of fuel changed faster

Take a hypothetical case where kilometres per tonne grew an extra 1% a year from 2020, and divide deaths by the kilometres that implies. Against the per-fuel trend, deaths per implied kilometre would be 12% above the trend in 2023, still outside the range, and 9% above it in 2024, inside it; with an extra 2% a year, 7% and 4% above, both inside the range. The 2023 excess would fall within the range with an extra 1.2 percentage points a year, and the 2024 excess with 0.6. These growth rates are illustrative assumptions.

Interurban roads: deaths per measured kilometre

The Ministerio de Transportes measures, by traffic counts, the vehicle-kilometres travelled each year on the interurban roads of the State, the regions and the provincial councils. Divided into interurban deaths, they give the only available series whose numerator and denominator cover nearly the same roads. The kilometres leave out roads run by municipalities and other bodies, which DGT's crash records identify: between 8.7% and 11.2% of interurban deaths each year from 2016 to 2024 occurred on them, 10.1% in 2019 and 9.9% in 2023. The rate's level is therefore too high by about that share, but the comparison between 2019 and 2023 moves by less than 1%. Before 2016 the share cannot be measured.

The same trend model, applied to interurban deaths per measured kilometre over 2008–2019 (the kilometre series is comparable only from 2008), places the last turning point in 2013, and the trend refitted to 2013–2019 falls 1.8% a year. Projected forward with each year's kilometres, 2023 comes out 5% above the trend, inside the trend's 95% prediction range of ±12%. In 2020, when measured kilometres fell 22%, deaths per kilometre were on trend (1.00×).

Divided instead by national road fuel, with a trend fitted the same way, the same deaths were 13% above their trend in 2023, against a range of ±12%. Fuel is sold for every road, towns included, so this ratio serves only as a check on road fuel as a measure of traffic, and it shows the two measures parting: measured interurban kilometres per tonne of national road fuel grew 0.5% a year over 2011–2019 and 1.9% a year over 2019–2023 (from 8,741 to 9,420). If kilometres per tonne grew that much faster on all roads, part of the recent excess in deaths per tonne of fuel would reflect more driving per tonne. On all roads, deaths per kilometre are not measured.

The measured kilometres also separate road types. Counting only deaths and injury crashes on the networks whose traffic is measured, conventional roads, single and dual carriageway, had 7.41 deaths per billion vehicle-kilometres in 2023 and motorways (autopistas and autovías) 2.15: 3.45× as many on conventional roads. That ratio combines 2.28× as many injury crashes per kilometre with 1.51× as many deaths per injury crash, so more of the difference lies in how often crashes happen than in how deadly they are.

LimitationsDGT estimated the 30-day deaths of 1993 to 2010 from deaths within 24 hours, with correction factors drawn from following a sample of people admitted to hospital, and has counted them since 2011 by matching crash records with the register of deaths (DGT's accident yearbook, annex on the revised method). The trends refitted on deaths within 24 hours, which the police count directly throughout, place the turning points of the count and of deaths per tonne of fuel in the same years (the table of other projections above).
