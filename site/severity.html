Associations in DGT crash records

Which recorded circumstances go with a fatal outcome once an injury crash has happened, and how those associations hold up under sensitivity fits: a description of DGT's records, not a predictive model.

Supporting analysis. This page describes associations in DGT's crash records: which recorded circumstances go with a fatal outcome, given an injury crash. It is not a predictive model and not a model of crash causation, which these data cannot support: DGT's national crash microdata have no driver, vehicle or speed records, and their audit keeps them out of model training (see the sources page). The models are on the models page.

Once an injury crash has happened, the conditions a driver would call dangerous go with a lower chance that someone dies. A wet road carries 0.56 times the odds of a death of a dry one, a junction 0.75 times the odds of a stretch away from one. Rain and a wet surface are one association counted twice, worth about 0.56 on its own. These are associations in the police record of crashes that happened, given an injury crash. It says nothing about how often crashes happen.

This page describes associations in DGT's records; it is not the project's predictive model. Levels that record a missing value rather than what happened are among the strongest terms of the regression (alignment “unknown”, odds ratio 0.12), which is one reason DGT's file is used here to describe associations and not to train a model that predicts severity.

Testing the finding

The description is two logistic regressions on every injury crash of 2016 to 2024, one for a death and one for a death or a hospitalisation. The predictors are the circumstances the police record: zone, road type, crash type, junction, lighting, weather, surface, alignment, time of day, weekend, number of vehicles and year.

The first objection is that weather and road surface measure much the same thing, so a model carrying both splits one association between two columns. That is what happens. With surface dropped, rain moves from 0.86 (0.72–1.03) to 0.56 (0.49–0.65). With weather dropped, a wet surface moves from 0.62 (0.52–0.74) to 0.56 (0.49–0.64). The right reading is a single wet-conditions association, an odds ratio of about 0.56.

The second objection is that adverse weather falls in particular places. Fitting interurban roads and urban streets separately holds the road context fixed instead of adjusting for it. The wet-surface odds ratio stays: 0.63 (0.54–0.74) on interurban roads and 0.57 (0.39–0.82) on urban streets. The junction association, 0.75 (0.69–0.81) overall, is present in every stratum too. Hail and snow behave differently. They give 0.63 (0.46–0.85) in the full model but 0.99 (0.62–1.57) on conventional roads alone, where the interval covers no difference at all.

Hail and snow are only 1,577 crashes, and 80% of them are on interurban roads, so it is fair to suspect a few mountain provinces. That is not what the data show. Dropping the 3 provinces that record most of them moves the odds ratio only to 0.66 (0.46–0.93). The road type is what moves it, and on conventional roads alone the association disappears.

Missing values and who recorded the crash

Some levels stand for a missing value: a field's own “unknown” code or “not specified”. They describe how the form was filled in, which differs between police forces and years, so they are in the model only so that no crash is dropped. They are flagged as nuisance terms in the coefficient tables (is_nuisance) and left out of the figure below and of every headline. One of them sits almost entirely in one place: road alignment “unknown” is 34.7% of the crashes in Cataluña's four provinces against 0.5% elsewhere, so 95.7% of its 78,990 crashes are Catalan, while Cataluña has 24.9% of all crashes. Its odds ratio, 0.12, cannot be read as a property of the road: it marks crashes recorded mostly by Cataluña's forces. The weather and surface unknowns are almost all outside Cataluña (at most 0.07% Catalan).

A recording practice that goes with the outcome could lean on the other coefficients through such a level, so the fatal model was refitted without Cataluña's 217,966 crashes. All 32 other odds ratios stay inside the full model's interval. The largest moves are in zone and road type, which have to be read together: zone “urban crossing” goes from 4.18 to 2.57. The wet-surface ratio moves from 0.62 to 0.72 and the junction ratio from 0.75 to 0.70, both still below 1; alignment “unknown” itself moves from 0.12 to 0.44 once most of its crashes are gone.

The other coefficients

The two largest ratios are for kinds of crash. A head-on collision carries 5.7 times the odds of a death of a side collision, and a pedestrian struck 6.4 times, against 0.75 for a junction and 0.56 for a wet road. As a check that the associations carry across years, the regressions were fitted on 2016–2022 without a year term and scored on 2023–2024: the area under the ROC curve is 0.80 for a death and 0.69 for a death or a hospitalisation, far better than chance at ranking, while the fitted probabilities improve little on the training years' share of each outcome given to every crash: the Brier score is 4.2% better for a death and 5.3% better for a death or a hospitalisation.

Conclusion

Wet conditions and junctions are associated with materially lower odds that an injury crash kills someone: about 0.56 and 0.75 times the reference odds, holding the other recorded circumstances constant. Both survive every sensitivity fit run here, the refit without Cataluña's recording included, so treat them as established associations in the record. The hail and snow result does not survive, so treat it as unresolved. The levels that stand for a missing value are not findings. None of this identifies a mechanism, and none of it says anything about how likely a crash is in the first place.

Limits. DGT's national microdata are one row per crash with no driver, vehicle or person fields, so nothing here says who was driving, how fast or whether alcohol was involved, and no mechanism for any of these associations can be tested with them. A model of recorded crashes describes which recorded crashes end badly, not the risk of crashing. Refitting one year at a time, 32 of the 90 year-by-term estimates fall outside the full model's interval, the most in 2023 and 2024 (8 and 7), when DGT changed how junctions and urban road types are coded (see the data page); road type and zone have to be read together. The levels that stand for a missing value, 6 of them (5 estimated, 1 with too few events or repeating another column), are flagged in the coefficient table. Levels with fewer than 500 crashes are merged into their reference: on all years for the full model, on the training years alone for the holdout check.
