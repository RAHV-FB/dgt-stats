What the models are

Two logistic regressions on every injury crash of 2016–2024: one for a fatal outcome, one for a serious outcome (death or hospitalisation). The predictors are the circumstances the police record for the crash itself: zone, road type, crash type, junction, lighting, weather, surface, alignment, time of day, weekend (Saturday, Sunday and Friday from 20:00), number of vehicles and year. Missing states are kept as their own levels (not specified and a field's explicit unknown code, each apart) and no crash is dropped. Two rules fold levels together: a level with fewer than 500 crashes is merged into the reference (the grouping table at the end says which); and alignment's 'not applicable' code — 546,619 crashes, exactly the street zone, where alignment is not recorded — folds into 'straight', because as a level of its own it would repeat the zone predictor. The alignment reference is therefore 77% street crashes with no recorded alignment, and 'curve' and 'unknown' are read against that mixture. A third exception applies to one fit at a time: a level whose crashes all share one outcome carries no odds ratio and counts with the reference there. Intervals are clustered by province.

What they are not. The microdata carry no driver or person fields and no vehicle types, only the number of vehicles in the crash, so nothing here says who was driving, how fast, or whether alcohol was involved; the factor interactions the project was originally framed around, such as alcohol × speed, cannot be estimated from this file. And a model of recorded crashes describes which recorded crashes turn out badly, not the risk of crashing in the first place.

Refitting the fatal model one year at a time, 37 of the 90 year-by-term estimates fall outside the interval of the full model; 2023 alone accounts for 9 of them (pedestrian struck, urban crossing, interurban road, dual carriageway, motorway, run-off or overturn, rear-end or chain collision, conventional, other road). The yardstick is strict: the full model's intervals are narrow because they pool nine years, while a single year of fatal crashes gives wide ones. One pattern is not noise: in 2024 every road-type effect collapses towards 1 while every zone effect rises above its full-model estimate, which matches the change in how road type is coded that year (the share of crashes coded to other road types rose sharply; see the data page). Road type and zone should be read together, not separately. Two other levels follow reporting practice rather than risk: the urban motorway or dual carriageway zone, which is coded in 0.6–0.7% of crashes in 2016–2018 but 0.1% or less from 2019 apart from 0.4% in 2021, so its estimate is mostly a 2016–2018 one; and the junction field, whose at-junction share moves from 39% before 2023 to 44% from 2023, mostly in Barcelona, so its level pools two recording regimes. The road-type levels span the 2021 interurban recoding as well: code 5 (carretera convencional de doble calzada) is 45% of the dual carriageway level's crashes up to 2020 but 17% from 2021, the rest having moved into the conventional level, so those two odds ratios pool two coding regimes; see the data page.

Four missing-state levels are filing markers rather than circumstances. Alignment 'unknown' is 96% Catalan (61% of Lleida's crashes, 54% of Tarragona's, 52% of Girona's, 29% of Barcelona's and under 3% in every other province); weather 'not specified' is 97% Madrid and 97% 2016–2018 (10% of crashes a year then, under 0.5% from 2019); road surface 'not specified' is 85% Gipuzkoa and lighting 'not specified' is 90% Gipuzkoa and Santa Cruz de Tenerife. The models carry no province term, so these odds ratios and marginal effects measure which force recorded the crash rather than what the road was like; the four are marked 'recording practice' in the tables above.
