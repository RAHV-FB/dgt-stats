Once an injury crash has happened, the conditions a driver would call dangerous go with a lower chance that someone dies. A wet road carries 0.56 times the odds of a death of a dry one, a junction 0.75 times the odds of a stretch away from one. Rain and a wet surface are one effect counted twice, worth about 0.56 on its own. All of this concerns how badly a crash ends, given that one has happened. It says nothing about how often crashes happen.

Testing the finding

The models are two logistic regressions on every injury crash of 2016 to 2024, one for a death and one for a death or a hospitalisation. The predictors are the circumstances the police record: zone, road type, crash type, junction, lighting, weather, surface, alignment, time of day, weekend, number of vehicles and year.

The first objection is that weather and road surface measure much the same thing, so a model carrying both splits one effect between two columns. That is what happens. With surface dropped, rain moves from 0.86 (0.72–1.02) to 0.56 (0.49–0.64). With weather dropped, a wet surface moves from 0.62 (0.52–0.74) to 0.56 (0.48–0.64). The right reading is a single wet-conditions effect of about 0.56.

The second objection is that adverse weather falls in particular places. Fitting interurban roads and urban streets separately holds the road context fixed instead of adjusting for it. The wet-surface effect stays: 0.63 (0.54–0.73) on interurban roads and 0.57 (0.39–0.82) on urban streets. The junction effect, 0.75 (0.69–0.81) overall, is present in every stratum too. Hail and snow behave differently. They give 0.62 (0.45–0.85) in the full model but 1.15 (0.73–1.80) on conventional roads alone, where the interval covers no effect at all.

Hail and snow are only 1,577 crashes, and 80% of them are on interurban roads, so it is fair to suspect a few mountain provinces. That is not the explanation. Dropping the three provinces that record most of them moves the odds ratio only to 0.65 (0.45–0.93). The road type is what moves it, and on conventional roads alone the effect disappears.

Why might visibly dangerous conditions produce less severe crashes?

These are mechanisms the literature proposes, not results this analysis demonstrates. The DGT microdata carry no speed, no driver and no vehicle information, so nothing here can show which of them is at work.

Drivers appear to compensate when the risk is visible. Naturalistic-driving data show lower speeds and longer headways in heavy rain (Ahmed & Ghasemzadeh (2018), “The impacts of heavy rain on speed and headway behaviors”, Transportation Research Part C 91, 371–384); survey work finds drivers also postpone or re-route trips, avoid overtaking and drive more cautiously when the weather is bad or forecast to be (Kilpeläinen & Summala (2007), “Effects of weather and weather forecasts on driver behaviour”, Transportation Research Part F 10(4), 288–299). A modest speed reduction matters more than it sounds, because the relationship between speed and fatal outcomes is steeply non-linear (Elvik, Vadeby, Hels & van Schagen (2019), “Updated estimates of the relationship between speed and road safety”, Accident Analysis & Prevention 123, 114–122; for pedestrians, Rosén & Sander (2009), “Pedestrian fatality risk as a function of car impact speed”, Accident Analysis & Prevention 41, 536–542). At junctions the plausible mechanisms are similar and more mundane: approach speeds are lower, conflict points are expected and signalled, and drivers are more often already braking when the impact happens.

Compensation is not the only candidate. Which trips are made changes with the weather, and so does who makes them. Traffic is denser and slower. The mix of vehicles and of road types differs. A police officer's judgement of the conditions is recorded after the event. The review literature on weather and road safety treats all of these as open (Theofilatos & Yannis (2014), “A review of the effect of traffic and weather characteristics on road safety”, Accident Analysis & Prevention 72, 244–256).

The other coefficients

The large effects are the expected ones. A head-on collision carries 5.8 times the odds of a death of a side collision, and a pedestrian struck 6.4 times, against 0.75 for a junction and 0.56 for a wet road. Fitted on 2016 to 2022 and scored on 2023 and 2024, the fatal model reaches an area under the ROC curve of 0.80 and the serious model 0.69.

Conclusion

Wet conditions and junctions are associated with materially lower odds that an injury crash kills someone: about 0.56 and 0.75 times the reference odds, holding the other recorded circumstances constant. Both survive every sensitivity fit run here, so treat them as established associations. The hail and snow result does not survive, so treat it as unresolved. None of this identifies a mechanism, and none of it says anything about how likely a crash is in the first place.

Limits. The microdata are one row per crash with no driver, vehicle or person fields, so nothing here says who was driving, how fast or whether alcohol was involved, and the factor interactions this project was first framed around cannot be estimated. A model of recorded crashes describes which recorded crashes end badly, not the risk of crashing. Refitting one year at a time, 37 of the 90 year-by-term estimates fall outside the full model's interval, mostly in 2024, when DGT changed how urban road types are coded (see the data page); road type and zone have to be read together. Four levels that stand for a missing value track which force recorded the crash rather than what the road was like, and are marked as such in the full coefficient table.
