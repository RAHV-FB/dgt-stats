The July 2006 break

Monthly road deaths fell around the points-based licence. How much of that was the policy, how much the trend already under way, and how much July?

Something did happen to Spanish road deaths around July 2006, when the points-based licence came in. It is smaller and less exceptional than a straight-line model says. Fitting the pre-trend the earlier months actually prefer cuts the step from −12% to −7% (−13% to −1%), and a forecast made before each July finds 2006 only the fourth most abnormal July of 15. What the series supports is a step of roughly seven per cent that no single measure can be credited with.

How the pre-trend was chosen, and why it matters

An interrupted time series fits the months before a change, projects them forward and asks whether the months after sit below. Everything therefore depends on what is projected. Earlier versions of this study used one straight trend through 2000–2006. Tested on the pre-intervention months alone, with no post-period involved, that straight line is worse than a trend with a single kink by 16 points of AIC, and the kink the data pick is at Aug 2003, where the decline steepened. Put that kink in and the estimated step at July 2006 falls by about 5 percentage points. The dotted line on the chart is the straight-line counterfactual; the gap between the two counterfactuals is the disagreement.

Was July 2006 unusual for a July?

Spanish road deaths peak every July and August. A placebo distribution built by moving the break to arbitrary months cannot answer whether the summer of 2006 was unusual, so this one places the break at 1 July of every year with a clean window: the same model, the same 60 months before and 17 after, at Julys the points licence cannot explain.

July 2006 is the largest fall of the 15, but only just. July 2004 gives −7.2% and July 2001 −5.5%, and the intervals overlap. Rank 1 of 15 is a one-sided empirical p-value of about 0.07: suggestive, not decisive.

Two tests without a model

Take the twelve months from each July and divide by the twelve months before it. Both sides then contain one of every calendar month, so seasonality cancels exactly and no model is involved. Across July 2006 that ratio is −11.4%, a large fall, and the fourth largest of the 27 years that can be measured. Three years in the recession that followed were larger. The series was falling steeply either side of 2006, which is the same problem the pre-trend test found, seen without a regression.

The second test fits the 60 months before each July, with a trend and seasonality and no intervention term, then forecasts the 17 months after it. The question is how far the observed months fall below that forecast. After July 2006 they fall 7% below, which sounds decisive until the same exercise is run at the other Julys: 2006 comes fourth of 15. The months after July 2004 were further below their own forecast, and so were two other years. This is the single clearest reason not to report a twelve per cent policy effect.

Exposure, and everything else that changed

A fall in deaths can be a fall in traffic. Two Spanish series are monthly and reach back past 2006: CORES's national road-fuel consumption, which covers every road, and the Ministerio de Transportes' vehicle-kilometres on the state toll-motorway network, which is a direct traffic measurement on a small and changing part of it. Adding either as a covariate barely moves the estimate: −8% with fuel and −7% with toll traffic, against −7% without either. Neither is vehicle-kilometres on all Spanish roads by month, which does not exist, so they rule out a traffic-volume explanation rather than measuring exposure properly.

Conclusion

Two different claims have to be kept apart. That the death series changed unusually around July 2006: partly supported. A step of about 7% survives the specification the pre-period prefers and the largest of the calendar-matched July placebos, but the out-of-sample test and the seasonality-free transition both put 2006 inside the range of ordinary years. That the points licence caused it: not supported by this series at all. The speed-camera programme was rolling out over the same two years, the Penal Code reform followed seventeen months later, and the recession after that.

And the step is fragile. Of the 13 specifications in the table above, 5 give an interval that includes no change at all: piecewise pre-trend with the knot fixed at january 2004, interurban roads only, urban streets only, level change only, no slope term and post-period to december 2009 with a second break in december 2007. Twelve per cent is not what this series supports; seven, with a wide interval, is about as much as it will carry.

Limits. Monthly deaths are overdispersed and serially correlated; standard errors are Newey–West with twelve lags and a negative-binomial fit is in the table above. Choosing the pre-trend by AIC on the pre-period is a selection step, so the table shows the alternatives: every knot from 2002 to 2005 gives between −6% and −10%, and a knot at January 2004 gives an interval that includes zero. A separate study of the January 2019 speed-limit cut on conventional roads is not published here: its control design fails a placebo break placed in January 2017, which produces a divergence of its own, so no claim can be made from it. Its fits are kept as evidence of the negative result (placebos, specifications).
