Spain · supporting analysis

The 2006 points-based licence

Spain's monthly road deaths from 2000 to 2007, before and after the points-based driving licence came into force.

Monthly road deaths in Spain fell around 1 July 2006, when the points-based driving licence came into force. The size of the fall depends on how the earlier trend is projected. A straight-line trend gives a step of −12%; the trend the earlier months themselves favour, which steepens in August 2003, gives −7% (95% interval −13% to −1%). When a step model with a straight-line trend is fitted at each of 15 Julys on a common window, 2006 shows the largest fall, but only narrowly; against forecasts made before each July it ranks fourth of 15. The series supports a modest fall in deaths around the change. It gives no basis for attributing that fall to the licence.

The before-and-after estimate

A monthly national series can test only a change with a known date, and even then the test is weak. An interrupted time-series analysis fits the months before a change, projects them forward and measures how far the months after fall below the projection. The model is a Poisson regression of monthly deaths within 30 days, from January 2000 to November 2007, with a term for each calendar month, a pre-existing trend, and a step and a change of slope at July 2006. A straight line through the months before the change gives a step of −12%.

The choice of pre-trend

The straight line can be tested against alternatives on the months before the change alone, so the choice does not look at the outcome. Among trends with a single bend, the best fit places the bend at August 2003, where the decline steepened, and it fits the earlier months better than the straight line by 16 points of AIC. (Akaike's information criterion rewards fit and penalises extra parameters; lower is better, and a difference of more than 10 points is usually read as strong evidence.) With the bend in place, the estimated step at July 2006 shrinks by about 5 percentage points, to −7%.

Comparison with other Julys

Spanish road deaths peak every July and August, so a break placed on 1 July may partly capture an ordinary summer movement. A placebo test repeats the estimate at Julys when the licence cannot have acted, each with a straight-line trend on a common window of 60 months before and 17 after; years whose window contains July 2006 or the pandemic are left out. If the 2006 fall is exceptional, it should stand out among these Julys.

On this common window July 2006 gives the largest fall of the 15, −9.8%, but only narrowly: July 2004 gives −7.2% and July 2001 −5.5%, and their intervals overlap. A rank of 1 of 15 corresponds to a one-sided empirical p-value of about 0.07, which is suggestive but short of conventional significance.

A second test uses no step at all. For each July, a model with a trend and calendar months is fitted to the 60 months before it and used to forecast the 17 months after; the measure is how far the recorded months fall below that forecast. After July 2006 they fall 7% below it, a rank of 4 of 15 among the Julys: the months after July 2004 fell further below their own forecast, and so did those after July 2001 and 2000. This is the clearest evidence against reading the straight-line estimate of −12% as an effect of the licence.

Twelve months either side of each July

A check without any model divides the deaths in the twelve months from each July by those in the twelve months before it. Each side contains one of every calendar month, so seasonality cancels exactly. Across July 2006 deaths fell 11.4%, the fourth largest fall of the 27 years that can be measured; the larger falls all came later, in 2008, 2009 and 2012. The series was falling steeply on both sides of 2006, the same pattern the choice of pre-trend revealed.

Traffic and other changes around 2006

A fall in deaths can reflect a fall in traffic. Two monthly series reach back before 2006: CORES's national road-fuel consumption, which covers every road, and traffic on the state toll-motorway network, measured directly on a small part of it as its average daily intensity (vehicles a day on the average kilometre, so that newly opened sections do not inflate it). Adding either series to the model barely moves the estimate: −8% with fuel and −8% with toll-motorway intensity, against −7% without either. Neither measures the kilometres driven on all roads, so this shows only that the step does not follow these two series.

Other changes overlap the licence. The decline in deaths had already steepened in 2003, before any of them. Automatic speed cameras were being installed on the main road network under the national road-safety plan over the same years. The reform of the Penal Code that made serious speeding and drink-driving criminal offences came into force in December 2007, 17 months after the licence, and the recession that followed reduced traffic and freight.

Sensitivity to the specification

The estimate was repeated under 12 specifications. The straight-line pre-trend gives the largest fall of all of them. Five give an interval that includes no change: a bend fixed at January 2004, interurban roads alone, urban streets alone, a step without a change of slope and the window extended to December 2009 with a second break in December 2007. A fall of about 7%, with a wide interval, is as much as the series supports.

The 2019 speed limit on conventional roads

The lower speed limit on conventional roads was examined with a different design, comparing conventional roads with motorways and dual carriageways month by month. That design fails its placebo test: a break placed in January 2017, before the change, produces a divergence of its own (+12%, with an interval that excludes zero), so no estimate of the speed-limit change is reported. The fits are published (placebos, specifications).

The fall and the licence

The monthly series shows a fall in deaths of about 7% around July 2006. The fall survives the pre-trend the earlier months favour and is the largest of the July placebos, though narrowly, while the forecast test and the twelve-month comparison place 2006 within the range of other years. Whether the fall was unusual is therefore only partly settled. That the points licence caused it is a separate claim, which the series cannot test: speed cameras, the Penal Code reform and the recession overlapped or closely followed the licence, and a single national series cannot separate their effects from the licence's.

Limitations. Monthly deaths vary more than a Poisson model assumes and are correlated from month to month; the intervals use Newey–West standard errors with 12 lags, and a negative binomial model gives a similar step (−7%). Choosing the pre-trend by AIC is itself a selection step, which is why the alternative pre-trends and specifications are reported.
