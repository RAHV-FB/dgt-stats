The 2006 points-based licence

Spain's monthly road deaths from 2000 to 2007, before and after the points-based driving licence came into force: how large the fall was, how unusual, and why it cannot be attributed to the licence.

Spain's points-based driving licence came into force on 1 July 2006. Monthly road deaths fell around that date, but the series cannot show that the licence caused the fall. A model of monthly deaths with a step at the change estimates a change in deaths of −7% (95% interval −13% to −1%) when the earlier trend is projected the way the earlier months favour, and −12% with a straight-line trend. Compared with the same estimate at other Julys, the fall was large but not exceptional: the largest of 15, but only narrowly, and fourth of 15 against forecasts made before each July. Deaths had already begun to fall faster in 2003, and speed cameras, a reform of the Penal Code and the recession came close enough to the licence that one national series cannot separate their effects.

The estimate

An interrupted time series fits the months before a change, projects them forward and measures how far the months after fall below the projection. The model here is a Poisson regression of monthly deaths within 30 days, from January 2000 to November 2007, with a term for each calendar month, a trend, and a step and a change of slope at July 2006. The result depends on how the earlier trend is projected. A straight line through the months before the change gives a step of −12%. But the earlier months themselves favour a trend that bends: among trends with one bend, the best fit places it at August 2003, where the decline steepened, and it fits those months better than the straight line by 16 points of AIC (a measure of fit that penalises extra terms; a difference of more than 10 is usually read as strong evidence). The choice uses only the months before the change. With the bend in place the estimated step is −7%.

How unusual the fall was

Spanish road deaths peak every July and August, so a break placed on 1 July can pick up an ordinary summer movement. The same step model, with a straight-line trend on a common window of 60 months before and 17 after, was therefore fitted at every July whose window avoids July 2006 and the pandemic. July 2006 gives the largest fall of the 15, −9.8%, but only narrowly: July 2004 gives −7.2% and July 2001 −5.5%, and their intervals overlap. Ranking first of 15 corresponds to a one-sided p-value of about 0.07, short of conventional significance.

Two checks that use no step point the same way. Forecasting the 17 months after each July from the 60 months before it, the months after July 2006 fall 7% below their forecast, fourth of 15 Julys: the months after July 2004, 2001 and 2000 fell further below their own forecasts. Dividing the deaths in the twelve months from each July by those in the twelve months before it, which cancels the seasons, deaths fell 11.4% across July 2006, the fourth largest fall of the 27 years that can be measured; the larger falls all came later, in 2008, 2009 and 2012.

Why the fall cannot be attributed to the licence

The step does not follow the available traffic series: adding national road-fuel consumption (CORES) or traffic intensity on the state toll motorways to the model barely moves it, to −8% in both cases, against −7% without either. But neither series measures kilometres on all roads, and other changes overlap the licence. The decline had already steepened in 2003. Automatic speed cameras were being installed on the main road network over the same years. The Penal Code reform that made serious speeding and drink-driving criminal offences came into force in December 2007, 17 months after the licence, and the recession that followed reduced traffic and freight. A single national series cannot separate these from the licence.

The estimate was repeated under 12 specifications. The straight-line pre-trend gives the largest fall of all of them, and five give an interval that includes no change: a bend fixed at January 2004, interurban roads alone, urban streets alone, a step without a change of slope and the window extended to December 2009 with a second break in December 2007. A fall of about 7%, with a wide interval, is as much as the series supports.

The 2019 speed limit on conventional roads

The lower speed limit on conventional roads was examined with a different design, comparing conventional roads with motorways and dual carriageways month by month. That design fails its placebo test: a break placed in January 2017, before the change, produces a divergence of its own (+12%, with an interval that excludes zero), so no estimate of the speed-limit change is reported. The fits are published (placebos, specifications).

Limitations. Monthly deaths vary more than a Poisson model assumes and are correlated from month to month; the intervals use Newey–West standard errors with 12 lags, and a negative binomial model gives a similar step (−7%). Choosing the pre-trend by AIC is itself a selection step, which is why the alternative pre-trends and specifications are reported.
