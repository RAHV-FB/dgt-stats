Over time

The 2006 points-based licence

Spain's points-based driving licence came into force on 1 July 2006. Monthly road deaths fell around that date, but the series cannot show that the licence caused the fall, or that the fall lasted. A model of monthly deaths finds an immediate step of −7% in July 2006 (95% interval −13% to −1%), after which deaths drifted back to the path projected from the earlier trend by about July 2007. Averaged over the 17 months to November 2007, deaths were 2.5% below the projection (95% interval −7.8% to +3.2%). Only a straight-line projection of the earlier trend, which fits the earlier months less well, gives a lasting fall (−9.5% on average). The intervals are too narrow: at 6 of the 14 other Julys that can be tested, where nothing was introduced, the same kind of model finds a step whose 95% interval excludes zero. Against those Julys the 2006 step is the largest of 15, but only narrowly, and the shortfall against forecasts made before each July is the third largest of 15.

An immediate fall of about 7% that did not last

The model is a Poisson regression of monthly deaths within 30 days from January 2000 to November 2007, with a term for each calendar month, a trend, and a step and a change of slope at July 2006. It projects the months before the change forward and measures how far the months after fall below that projection. After the step of −7%, the slope changes by +7.5% a year (95% interval +1.4% to +14.0%), so fitted deaths meet the projection again in about July 2007 and end the window 2.3% above it. That is why the average over the 17 months, −2.5%, is smaller than the step.

The result depends on how the earlier trend is projected. A straight line through the months before the change gives a step of −12% and an average of −9.5% (−14.3% to −4.5%). The earlier months favour a trend that bends once, in August 2003, where the decline steepened: it fits them better than the straight line by 7 points of QAIC, a measure of fit that penalises extra terms and allows for monthly deaths varying more than a Poisson model assumes. A difference of that size favours the bend but is not decisive. The choice uses only the months before the change.

Falls nearly as large occur in Julys when nothing changed

Spanish road deaths usually peak in July or August: one of the two was the deadliest month in 27 of the 32 years from 1993 to 2024. A break placed on 1 July can therefore pick up an ordinary summer movement. The same step model, with a straight-line trend on a common window of 60 months before and 17 after, was fitted at every July from 1998 to 2018 whose window does not include July 2006 or the pandemic; that leaves out the Julys of 2005 and 2007–2011. July 2006 gives the largest fall of the 15, −9.8%, but only narrowly: July 2004 gives −7.2% and July 2001 −5.5%, and their intervals overlap. If the licence had changed nothing, July 2006 would still rank first one time in 15, a one-sided p-value of about 0.07. That is the smallest this test can give with 15 Julys, so it cannot reach conventional significance; the narrow margin over the next July is the better guide.

The same fits show that the model's own intervals are too narrow. At 6 of the 14 other Julys, where nothing was introduced, the 95% interval lies entirely on one side of zero; an interval with the coverage it claims would do so about 0.7 times in 14. The placebo steps vary 2.5 times as much as the model's standard errors imply. An interval set by that spread puts the July 2006 step at −21% to +3%, which includes no change. The other intervals on this page come from the same kind of model, so they are probably too narrow as well.

Two checks that fit no step agree. Forecasting the 17 months after each July from the 60 months before it, the months after July 2006 fall 7.1% below their forecast, the third largest shortfall of the 15 Julys; the months after July 2004 and 2001 fell further below their own forecasts. Comparing the twelve months from each July with the twelve months before it, which cancels the seasons, deaths fell 11.4% across July 2006, the fourth largest fall of the 27 years that can be measured. The larger falls all came later, in 2008, 2009 and 2012. This comparison does not remove the trend: over 2003–2013 deaths fell about 11% a year, so a fall of this size across one July is what the trend alone would give.

Why the fall cannot be attributed to the licence

The available traffic series do not explain the step: adding national road-fuel consumption (CORES) or traffic intensity on the state toll motorways to the model barely moves it, to −8% in both cases, against −7% without either. Neither series, though, measures kilometres on all roads. Inside the fitted window the step competes with two other explanations: the decline had already steepened in 2003, and Julys when nothing was introduced show steps nearly as large. The window ends in November 2007, so it leaves out the Penal Code reform that made serious speeding and drink-driving criminal offences, in force from December 2007, 17 months after the licence, and the recession that followed. Extended to December 2009, with a second step at the reform, the model puts the step at July 2006 at −0.8% (−9.4% to +8.7%), and over that longer period the reform and the recession cannot be separated from the licence.

The estimate was repeated under 12 specifications. The straight-line pre-trend gives the largest fall of all of them, and five give a step whose interval includes no change: a bend fixed at January 2004, interurban roads alone, urban streets alone, a step without a change of slope and the window extended to December 2009 with a second break in December 2007. Averaged over the 17 months, every specification except the straight-line pre-trend gives a change whose interval includes no change.

The 2019 speed limit on conventional roads

The lower speed limit on conventional roads was examined with a different design, comparing conventional roads with motorways and dual carriageways month by month. That design fails its placebo test: a break placed in January 2017, before the change, produces a divergence of its own (+12%, with an interval that excludes zero), so no estimate of the speed-limit change is reported. The fits are published (placebos, specifications).

Limitations. Monthly deaths vary more than a Poisson model assumes and are correlated from month to month; the intervals use standard errors that allow for both (Newey–West, 12 lags), and a negative binomial model gives a similar step (−7%). Even so, the July placebos show these model-based intervals to be too narrow. Choosing the pre-trend by QAIC is itself a selection step, which is why the alternative pre-trends and specifications are reported.
