Drivers, vehicles and factors

Police-recorded inappropriate speed and crash severity

Outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, injury crashes where police recorded inappropriate speed had 3.41 times as many deaths per crash as other injury crashes in 2016–2023, partly because they cluster on roads where any crash is deadlier. Compared within each road type, the ratio ran from 1.22 times on dual carriageways to 5.90 times on urban streets; their weighted summary is 2.00 times (95% interval 1.69–2.36). These are associations in police records, not estimates of what speed causes.

More deaths per crash on every road type

In 2023, police recorded inappropriate speed in 6.9% of injury crashes outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, and those crashes accounted for 21.8% of the deaths. They are concentrated on interurban roads other than motorways and dual carriageways, where any crash is more often fatal and where most of their deaths occur, so comparing them with other crashes on the same kind of road and in the same year lowers the ratio.

The ratio was largest on urban streets, where an ordinary injury crash rarely kills (0.73 deaths per 100 crashes, against 4.32 with speed recorded), and smallest on dual carriageways. The road-type ratios differ by more than chance, so the all-roads summary of 2.00 describes no single kind of road: it is weighted towards other interurban roads, and its interval is widened to allow for how much the ratios differ. How the ratios are estimated is set out in the technical notes.

Deaths per 100 injury crashes on each road type, for every recorded crash in Spain, can be explored by region and year in the crash statistics explorer.

The ratio describes police records, not measured speeds

Inappropriate speed is a recorded factor: a judgement the police record about a crash, usually once its outcome is known (recorded factors). No file records how fast anyone was travelling. The ratio shows that crashes with speed recorded were deadlier than other crashes on the same kind of road. It does not show that speed caused those crashes or their deaths, nor separate speed from other factors recorded in the same crash.

Recording could bias the ratio either way. Police may look harder for speed when someone has died, which would raise it, while speed that played a part but went unrecorded leaves those crashes among the others, which would lower it. The data cannot measure either bias.

The driver tables cannot show a trend in speeding

DGT's yearbook tables, which cover all of Spain, record for each driver in an injury crash whether the police noted a speed infraction. From 2016, about half of drivers have no speed status recorded (52% in 2016 and 52% in 2024, against 17% in 2014). The two obvious readings therefore point in opposite directions: the share of all drivers with a speed infraction fell from 6.7% in 2014 to 4.3% in 2024, while the share among drivers with a recorded status rose from 8.1% to 9.1%.

The speed report's crash series, used above, has no break of this kind; Recorded factors follows its trend.
