Spain

Police-recorded inappropriate speed and crash severity

Deaths per injury crash where the police recorded inappropriate speed, compared with other crashes on the same kind of road, from DGT's speed report and crash records.

In Spain outside Catalonia and the Basque Country, police recorded inappropriate speed in 6.9% of injury crashes in 2023, and those crashes accounted for 21.8% of the deaths. Over 2016–2023, crashes with speed recorded had 3.41 times as many deaths per crash as other injury crashes. Part of that difference is where they happen: they are concentrated on interurban roads other than motorways and dual carriageways, where any crash is more often fatal. Compared with other crashes on the same kind of road in the same year, they had about twice as many deaths: 2.00 times (95% interval 1.69–2.36). This is an association in police crash records. It does not estimate how many crashes or deaths speeding caused.

Crashes with speed recorded had more deaths per crash on every road type. The difference was largest on urban streets, where an ordinary injury crash rarely kills (0.73 deaths per 100 crashes): there, crashes with speed recorded had 5.90 times as many deaths. On interurban roads other than motorways and dual carriageways the ratio was 1.75, and on dual carriageways 1.22. The figure of 2.00 for all roads compares crashes within the same road type and year and summarises these ratios, with an interval widened to allow for how much they vary.

How the speed report and the crash records are combined

DGT's speed report, the Informe temático Factor Velocidad, leaves out Catalonia and the Basque Country, which keep their own crash records. It gives crashes and deaths with speed recorded by road type, but totals of all crashes only for all roads, interurban roads and urban streets. DGT's crash records for the same provinces reproduce those three totals exactly in every year from 2016 to 2023, and supply the totals for each interurban road type. The report's interurban categories are matched to the road types of the crash records by name (autopistas to motorways, autovías to dual carriageways, the rest to other interurban roads); that match is checked only through the interurban total, not road type by road type.

The adjusted ratio comes from a quasi-Poisson model of deaths per crash with road type and year. It brings the crude ratio of 3.41× down to 2.00×; on a logarithmic scale, road type and year account for 43% of the crude ratio.

What a speed record shows

Inappropriate speed in these data is a judgement police officers record about a crash after it has happened, usually once the outcome is known; no file records how fast anyone was travelling. The record shows only that crashes in which the police recorded speed were deadlier than other crashes. It does not show that speed caused those crashes, or that it caused the deaths in them. A crash can carry several recorded factors, and this comparison does not separate speed from the others. The ratio could also be distorted in either direction: police may look harder for speed when someone has died, which would raise it, while speed that played a part but went unrecorded leaves those crashes among the others, which would lower it. The data measure neither effect.

The 2016 break in the driver tables

DGT's yearbook tables also record, for each driver involved in an injury crash, whether the police noted a speed infraction. From 2016, about half of drivers have no speed status recorded (52% in 2016 and 52% in 2024, against 17% in 2014), so the driver tables cannot be used as a trend in speeding. The two obvious readings point in opposite directions: the share of all drivers with a speed infraction fell from 6.7% to 4.3% between 2014 and 2024, while the share among drivers with a recorded status rose from 8.1% to 9.1%. The crash series used above, from the speed report, shows no break of this kind (recorded factors tests each series for breaks).
