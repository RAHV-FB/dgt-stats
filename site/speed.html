What the sources record

Nothing in the open data measures speed. Two sources record a judgement about it. The yearbook's driver tables say, for each driver involved in an injury crash, whether the police recorded a speed infraction, no speed infraction, or nothing at all. DGT's thematic report on the speed factor counts crashes in which inappropriate speed was recorded as a concurrent factor for any road user, and it covers fifteen of the seventeen regions: Cataluña and País Vasco, which run their own police forces, are excluded. The two count different things over different territories, so this page never adds them together.

Drivers with a recorded speed infraction

The first thing the tables show is not about speed. In 2014, 17% of drivers had no speed record; in 2016 the share jumped to 52% and it has stayed there (52% in 2024; 54% on urban streets, 49% on interurban roads). A share of drivers with an infraction over all drivers therefore fell for reasons that have nothing to do with driving: 6.7% in 2014 to 4.3% in 2024. Among drivers who do have a record the share went the other way, from 8.1% to 10.9% in 2016 and 9.1% in 2024, which says as much about which drivers get a record as about speed. Interurban roads carry the higher rate throughout: 14.3% of interurban drivers with a record against 5.8% of urban ones in 2024.

In the report's territory, inappropriate speed was recorded in 5,070 injury crashes in 2023, 7% of the total, down from 10% in 2014. The factor is an interurban one: 14% of interurban injury crashes against 3% of urban ones. Distraction and illegal manoeuvres are recorded far more often, as on the driver tables above. The report's shares are rounded to whole percentages.

Conventional and other interurban roads take 55% of the speed-factor crashes and 66% of the deaths. By posted limit the crashes split between the 30 km/h streets (19%) and the 90 km/h roads (18%), but the deaths do not: 27% of them are on 90 km/h roads, 11% on 30 km/h streets. The 30 km/h count itself rose from 199 to 958 crashes as cities extended the limit from 2021, which changes what a 30 km/h street is more than how people drive on it.

Cars are in 3,519 of the speed-factor crashes and motorcycles in 1,300, but motorcycle users are 37% of the deaths against 45% for car occupants. Drivers aged 15 to 34 are involved in 42% of the crashes, and class A (motorcycle) licence holders are 29% of the drivers killed while being 10% of the crashes.

Pooled over ten years, 36% of the speed-factor crashes fall on Saturdays and Sundays, and the single busiest cell is Sunday at 12:00 (762 crashes). This is the weekend-daytime pattern of leisure riding and driving on interurban roads, not the commuting peaks of the timing page.

Where speed already appears on this site

The policy page tests the 2019 cut of the conventional-road limit and finds nothing it can attribute to it. The severity models have no speed term, because the microdata have none; road type and darkness stand in for it. The timing page shows the hour and weekday pattern of all crashes, which the speed-factor grid above departs from.

Limits

Five things to keep in mind. First, both sources record a judgement, not a measurement: no speed, no limit exceeded by how much. Second, the report excludes Cataluña and País Vasco, 28% of Spain's injury crashes in 2024, and its shares are rounded. Third, about half of the drivers in the yearbook tables have no speed record since 2016, and the half that does is not a random sample: an infraction is more likely to be written down than its absence, so the shares among recorded drivers probably overstate the true share, by an amount nothing here can measure. Fourth, the driver tables count drivers and the report counts crashes; a crash with two drivers appears twice in one and once in the other. Fifth, nothing here is a rate: without knowing how many drivers speed without crashing, none of these shares says how dangerous speeding is, only how often it is recorded when a crash has happened.
