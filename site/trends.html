Over time

Road deaths and injuries in 2024 against 2019

Spain recorded 1,785 road deaths in 2024, 1.7% more than in 2019. Per resident they fell 1.9%, and per tonne of road fuel sold they rose 3.5%. Under every denominator, the change from 2019 lies within the ordinary year-to-year variation of the annual count. Set against the trend of 2011–2019 instead, when deaths per tonne of fuel were falling about 2% a year, the rate in 2023 and 2024 was 16% and 14% above the projected trend, outside its range, or 10% and 8% for a trend started in 2013 (Long-run trends). The number of people admitted to hospital after a crash rose 11.0% as a count, at the edge of ordinary variation, and 13.0% per tonne of road fuel, beyond it.

Injury crashes fell 2.0% as a count, 5.5% per resident and 0.3% per tonne of road fuel. Judged against pure chance alone, the falls as a count and per resident would be real changes; against the 2013–2019 scatter none of them stands out. That scatter does not describe an ordinary year for crashes. Between 2013 and 2016 recorded urban injury crashes rose 26% while interurban ones fell 2%, a step that a straight trend cannot follow, so the crash intervals are about 11 times as wide as pure-chance intervals and too wide to say whether the falls are larger than an ordinary year. DGT's yearbook does not split injury crashes by vehicle type, so they have no rate per licence holder or registered vehicle.

Per resident, and for drivers per licence holder and occupants per registered vehicle, the rise in hospital admissions stays within ordinary variation. Admissions rose while the number of injury crashes fell, so more people were admitted to hospital per crash. That can reflect more serious crashes, more complete tracing of admissions back to crashes, or both. A change in tracing would move the series without any change on the road, and the tables cannot separate the two.

How ordinary variation was measured

Annual counts move around their trend more than pure chance would make them. Over 2013–2019 the variance around the trend was 1.5 times the pure-chance (Poisson) variance for deaths, 7.7 times for hospital admissions and 68 times for injury crashes. Each interval in the table is widened by the square root of its own count's factor. Each factor rests on seven years and is itself uncertain: the 95% interval of the factor for deaths runs from 0.6 to 8.7. The intervals therefore use Student's t distribution with the trend fit's five residual degrees of freedom instead of the normal distribution, which widens them by 31%. The factor for deaths is the one Long-run trends uses to project the count from 2020.

The rates part because the population grew while fuel sales fell

Between 2019 and 2024 the population grew 3.7%, licence holders 3.0% and the registered fleet 5.2%, while road fuel sold fell 1.7%. The same deaths therefore fall per resident, a measure of the burden on everyone living in Spain whether or not they travel, and rise per tonne of fuel, a measure against the traffic that fuel stands for. Licence holders and registered vehicles measure who or what could be on the road, without saying how much each is used, and each divides only the casualties it could contain: pedestrians and cyclists hold no licence for their journey and travel in no registered vehicle.

Which casualties each denominator divides

Licence holders divide only the deaths and admissions of drivers, and the fleet only those of occupants, of motorcycles, cars, vans, trucks and buses. Bicycles and personal mobility vehicles are left out of both because they need neither a licence nor a registration. Mopeds are left out because it is not established whether the fleet counts them, and the two numerators are kept to the same vehicles. The yearbook's category of other vehicles is left out because it held personal mobility vehicles until they were given their own category.

Road fuel stands in for kilometres driven, which no source counts on all roads

No Spanish source counts vehicle-kilometres on all roads every year, and road fuel sold stands in for them. The kilometres a tonne represents can change with fuel economy, electric driving, the mix of freight and private travel, and fuel bought in Spain but burnt elsewhere, and no available series measures that drift on all roads since 2019. The change per tonne of fuel therefore cannot be converted into a change per kilometre. A hypothetical case shows how much depends on it: had kilometres per tonne grown 1% a year since 2019, deaths per kilometre would show a fall of 1.5% where deaths per tonne of fuel show a rise of 3.5%; at 2% a year, a fall of 6.2%. These growth rates are illustrative assumptions. The same drift bears on how far deaths per tonne of fuel lie above the pre-pandemic trend (Long-run trends). On interurban roads the Ministerio de Transportes measures the kilometres, and Long-run trends sets deaths per measured kilometre against their trend.

DGT's kilometre release of 2024 gives the mean distance driven per vehicle for 2022, 2023 and 2024 as one series, estimated from roadworthiness inspections, and its 2022 values are those of DGT's 2022 release. By those estimates a car was driven 13,073 km on average in 2022 and 12,295 km in 2024, and all vehicles together drove 1.6% fewer kilometres in 2024 than in 2022, while road fuel sold rose 1.5%. Over those years deaths per kilometre by DGT's estimate rose 3.9% and deaths per tonne of fuel 0.8%. The series starts in 2022, so it cannot be set against 2019 or the long run, and fuel remains the denominator on this page. The rates per kilometre on the vehicles page use its 2022 estimates, and those on the drivers page its 2024 estimates.

Limitations. The denominators are national totals that weight every resident, licence, vehicle and tonne of fuel alike. The intervals allow for the year-to-year variation of the casualty counts, and for the uncertainty of that variation, but treat every denominator as exact.
