Everything the four analyses rest on, in three charts: how far road deaths fell and when they stopped falling, who the dead are, and one place where DGT's own published series changes meaning halfway through.

Twenty years of decline, then a plateau

Deaths fell from 6,378 in 1993 to 1,680 in 2013 — a fall of 74% — and have moved sideways since, reaching 1,785 in 2024 with the lockdown year 2020 as the only real interruption. Deaths per 100 injury crashes have been flat for a decade too. This plateau is the backdrop to the policy page: the large gains were made before 2013. Roughly 72% of deaths are on interurban roads although most crashes are in towns, and darkness holds 25% of interurban injury crashes but 34% of interurban deaths.

Who dies

Pedestrians, cyclists, moped riders, motorcyclists and personal-mobility-vehicle users — the road users with no protective shell — are 51% of deaths. The four largest groups in 2024 were car occupants (698), motorcyclists (441), pedestrians (320) and cyclists (90). Motorcyclists are the group that has not followed the general decline, which is why the vehicles page reports their rate per kilometre.

A published series that changes meaning in 2016

DGT's driver tables record, for each driver in an injury crash, whether the police noted a speed infraction. In 2014, 17% of drivers had no speed status recorded at all. In 2016 that jumped to 52% and it has stayed there. The consequence is that the two obvious ways of reading the same table now point in opposite directions: the share of all drivers with a speed infraction fell from 6.7% to 4.3%, while the share among drivers who have a record rose from 8.1% to 9.1%. The first series is mostly measuring how many judgements were written down. The second is a proportion of a group that is not a random sample of drivers, since an infraction is more likely to be recorded than its absence.

Limits. None of these series is a risk. They count what the police recorded, not how much travelling was done, which is why the analyses that matter on this site divide by something. Neither speed source measures speed: both record an officer's judgement after the event, and DGT's separate speed-factor report covers fifteen of seventeen regions, so its figures are never added to these.
