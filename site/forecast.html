Monthly deaths: how large a change a year can show

A Poisson model of each month's deaths, fitted to the four years before the year it predicts, forecasts a year's deaths on Spain's roads with a root-mean-square error of 6.6% in years it had not seen, and of 7.3% in the lockdown years, when repeating last year's count was off by 19.2%. That error sets how large a change the yearly counts can show: against the forecast, one year of deaths on all roads shows a fall of 15% (264 deaths) or a rise of 17% four times in five. Smaller changes show less often, and waiting longer does not help.

Not one of the site's predictive models. In the held-back ordinary years repeating last year's count forecasts as well (5.9% against 6.6% for the model), so by the rule every model on this site must pass, beating its plain comparator on records it never saw, the model is not presented as a forecasting tool (models page). It is kept for what it shows: its traffic adjustment matters when traffic moves sharply, as in the lockdown years, and its error sets how large a change a year of counts can show.

What the model is

Any before-and-after reading of the death counts compares the deaths after a change with the deaths there would have been without it, and the second number is a forecast. This one is a Poisson regression of each month's deaths within 30 days on the month of the year, a linear trend over the four years before, the month's road fuel (CORES petrol plus diesel, standing for the traffic) and its number of Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. It is fitted separately for all roads, interurban roads and urban streets, only to DGT's monthly series and CORES fuel; no value in it comes from a study made elsewhere. Fitted on 2021–2024, a window that includes a lockdown year, a month with 1% more road fuel is associated with about 1.2% more deaths (0.5 to 2.0), and one more Saturday with +8% (−1% to +17%). These are associations within four years of months, not effects of traffic or of weekends.

How it was chosen and tested

4 forms of the model and windows of 3 to 8 years were scored by forecasting each year from the years before it. The form and the window were chosen on the forecasts of 2006–2015 alone; 2016–2019 and 2022–2024 played no part in the choice and are the test, and the lockdown years 2020–2021 are scored apart. Beside the model are two naive forecasts and gradient-boosted trees given the same inputs, their leaf size tuned on the same selection years.

What the test shows. The model beats the tuned trees in every set of years and in each zone: a tree cannot extend a trend beyond the years it has seen, and with 48 monthly rows it fits the noise. In the lockdown years it beats last year's count by far, since it is given each month's fuel. In the flat held-back years last year's count does slightly better (5.9% against 6.6%; the worst year is 2022, forecast from a window that includes the lockdowns), so as a forecast of an ordinary year the model is replaced by last year's count. It is still the reference for how large a change a year can show: that has to hold in years when traffic or the trend moves, and whether the years ahead will be flat is not known in advance.

A comparator disclosed, not chosen. Looking at the held-back years afterwards, trees with leaves of at least 8 months would have scored 3.7% there, better than every other forecast. That leaf size was found by looking at the test years, so its score there is not a test, and it was never a candidate; it also does worse than the model in the selection years (10.3%) and in the lockdowns (14.2%). It is reported so that the choice can be judged with it in view.

How large a change the counts can show

The forecast's error on a year's total splits into Poisson chance, which shrinks as the count grows, and a drift of the trend away from the forecast, measured here on every forecast the model made from 2006 on. Together they give the change in deaths that a comparison of the observed count with the forecast picks up four times in five at the 5% level. For one year of deaths on all roads that is a fall of 15% or a rise of 17%; a fall half that size, about 7%, shows only 27% of the time. Interurban roads need a fall of 15%, and urban streets, with fewer deaths and a less steady trend, 22%. Summing more years does not help, because the drift grows faster than the count: over five years the fall that shows four times in five on all roads is 36%.

Conclusion

Fitted only to Spain's own monthly deaths and road fuel, a small Poisson model forecasts a year's deaths a little less well than last year's count in flat years and far better when traffic or the trend moves, and gradient-boosted trees given the same inputs and tuned the same way do not beat it. Its error means that one year of national counts shows a change in deaths reliably only from about 15% down or 17% up, and urban counts only from about 22%. A smaller change, whatever its cause, can be real and still not show in a year's deaths; a year without a visible change is not evidence that nothing changed.

Limits. The form of the model was chosen on all roads; on urban streets alone the month-and-trend form scored better in the selection years (6.4% against 7.3%). The drift that sets the detectable change is measured on forecasts made from 2006 on, the selection years included, so it describes the whole period rather than the held-back years alone. Road fuel is national and is observed after any change being judged: for a change that itself alters how much people drive, the comparison speaks only of deaths for the traffic there was. The detectable change assumes the forecast's errors are as large after a change as before it, and a comparison with a forecast says whether deaths differ from it, not why.
