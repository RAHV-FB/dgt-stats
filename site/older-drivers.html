Age and driving exposure

Are older drivers more dangerous? Split the question into crashing per kilometre and dying once the crash happens, and the two halves give opposite answers.

Older drivers are not crashing more often for the distance they drive. Per kilometre, car drivers aged 75 and over are involved in injury crashes 1.02× (0.99–1.05) as often as drivers aged 35–54 — that is, about the same. What is different is the consequence: once involved, they are killed 3.93× (2.98–5.18) as often. Young drivers are the mirror image: 2.57× (2.54–2.61) the involvement rate per kilometre, and 0.95× (0.75–1.20) the chance of dying once involved.

Why the denominator decides the answer

DGT reports road deaths of people 65 and over per million inhabitants of that age. On that denominator drivers 75 and over die 1.23 times as often as drivers aged 35–54 — barely more — because most people over 75 do not drive at all. Per licence holder the ratio is 2.61, because a licence is not mileage. Per driver already in a crash it is 3.93 and per kilometre 3.99. The numerator is identical in all four; the denominator is the entire difference, which is why it has to be named every time. The last two agree because involvement per kilometre is about the same at both ages — the finding above, seen from the other side.

That is also why the per-resident rate says so little about driving. In 2024, 38% of residents aged 75 and over held a licence against 83% of those aged 45–54, and the gap between the sexes widens with age: 74% of men over 74 hold one and 13% of women. A per-resident rate for older women is describing passengers and pedestrians far more than drivers.

What the kilometres are

DGT's 2024 kilometre release estimates, for the whole circulating fleet, how far each vehicle category is driven, and breaks it down by the age band of the registered owner. That is what makes this comparison possible and what makes the middle-aged baseline and the older groups directly comparable: the same source, the same year, the same construction. It is the owner's age, not the driver's. A car registered to a person of 75 may be driven by a relative, and a car registered to a company has no age at all: those are 14% of all car kilometres and they leave the denominator while their drivers stay in the numerator. Since company cars are mostly driven by people of working age, leaving them out understates middle-aged exposure and so understates this comparison: spreading those kilometres over the bands from 18 to 64 would raise the 75-and-over ratio from 3.99 to 4.76.

Limits. Owner age is a proxy for driver age, and the two diverge most in the households where a car is shared. The kilometres are modelled from roadworthiness-inspection odometer readings and are valid for aggregates only; the intervals here come from the crash counts and treat them as known. The numerator counts drivers of cars on Spanish roads, including foreign-registered ones, while the denominator covers Spanish-registered cars only. And this is one year: it says how the age groups compare in 2024, not how that comparison has moved.
