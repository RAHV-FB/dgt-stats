Models

Predictive models of crash severity

Models trained on crash records from Catalonia and Barcelona rank recorded crashes, and the people recorded in them, by the severity of the outcome.

Two models rank recorded cases by severity better than a descriptive table of the same records, on later records not used to build them. The Catalonia severity model estimates which crashes with a death or serious injury in Catalonia were fatal, and its probabilities match the observed shares in the test year. The Barcelona person-severity model estimates which people in Barcelona crashes were seriously or fatally injured; it ranks people well, but its probabilities are not calibrated, so it is used for ranking only. A third model, the Barcelona crash model, ranked crashes no better than a table of the share of crashes with a serious or fatal injury by accident type, and the table is used instead.

Ranking and calibration

Each model was built on earlier records and tested once on later ones. The Catalonia severity model was fitted to crashes from 2010–2020, tuned on 2021–2022 and tested on 2023. Barcelona's records cover only 2025, so the two Barcelona models were fitted to its first nine months and tested on the last three.

Ranking is measured by the ROC-AUC: the probability that a randomly chosen case with the outcome (a fatal crash, or a person seriously or fatally injured) scores higher than one without it; 0.5 is chance and 1 a perfect ranking. Each model is compared with a descriptive table, fixed in advance, of the share of cases with the outcome in each group of the training records. Calibration is the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed shares: among cases given a probability of one in ten, about one in ten should have the outcome (Methodology).

The Catalonia severity model

The Catalonia severity model estimates, for each crash with a death or serious injury in the Catalan file, the probability that it was fatal, from the recorded circumstances of the road, the crash and its environment. Its test set is the 1,732 crashes of 2023, of which 209 (12.1%) were fatal.

On those crashes it ranks clearly better than the table of fatal shares by crash subtype and zone (40 groups): ROC-AUC 0.79 (95% interval 0.76–0.82) against 0.70, a gain of 0.09 (0.07–0.12). In the tenth of test crashes it ranks highest (174), 42.0% were fatal, 73 of the 209; in the lowest tenth, 1.1% were.

Its probabilities also match the observed shares: an average predicted fatal share of 11.9% against 12.1% observed, 44.8% against 42.0% in the highest tenth, and a calibration slope of 0.97, close to the value of 1 at which predictions are neither too spread out nor too compressed. Among Catalan crashes like those tested, the probabilities therefore estimate the fatal share of groups of similar crashes. They do not carry over to Barcelona city: trained on the rest of Catalonia and applied there, the same model has a calibration slope of 0.44.

Shuffling one variable at a time and measuring how far the test ROC-AUC falls shows what the model relies on. The largest falls come from the road's owner, the zone (urban street, interurban road or road through a town) and the crash subtype (0.07, 0.06 and 0.05). The first two are close, given how much the falls vary between shuffles, and they overlap: the owner is left blank for urban streets, so it also carries the urban–interurban distinction that the zone records. The zone and the crash subtype are the dimensions of the comparison table.

DGT's national crash records are filled in too unevenly between provinces to train a model (Data sources and scope), but they can test one. Restricted to the variables both sources record in the same way, the harmonised Catalonia model ranks fatal outcomes (a death within 24 hours) among 67,971 serious and fatal crashes DGT recorded elsewhere in Spain almost as well as a model trained on them (ROC-AUC 0.708 against 0.712). Catalonia's serious and fatal crashes differ from Spain's, however, so national use is not established (External validation).

The Barcelona person-severity model

The Guàrdia Urbana's records list each person involved in a Barcelona crash, with their role, the vehicle on their record, age and sex. The Barcelona person-severity model estimates the probability that a person was seriously or fatally injured. In the test months it scored 4,049 people; 58 of them (1.4%) had such an injury.

It ranks them better than the table of shares by road-user role and vehicle group (35 groups): ROC-AUC 0.84 (95% interval 0.81–0.87) against 0.78, a gain of 0.06 (0.02–0.11). With only 58 serious or fatal cases the gain is less precisely estimated than in Catalonia, and a single year of records allows no test on another year. The fifth of people it ranks highest (810) includes 39 of the 58; the two fifths it ranks lowest (1,620 people) include none.

Its probabilities are right on average (1.4% predicted and observed), but the calibration slope is 0.63: the predictions are too spread out, so the highest scores overstate the chance of serious injury and lower ones understate it (5.9% predicted on average in the highest fifth, 4.8% observed). The probabilities are therefore not reliable as the share of similar cases with a serious injury, and the model is used only to rank people.

The model relies above all on two variables, the vehicle group on the person's record and the accident type: shuffling either lowers the test ROC-AUC by about 0.15. The road-user role (driver, passenger or pedestrian) and where a pedestrian was struck follow well behind (about 0.02 each).

The test months hold too few serious cases to score groups of road users, so every person recorded in 2025 was scored by a version of the model fitted without their crash. The ROC-AUC is then 0.85 for everyone and can be computed for 13 groups with at least 20 serious or fatal cases. It is highest for people whose record names a car (0.94) and lowest for pedestrians (0.58), little better than chance, although pedestrians are the road users most often seriously hurt (6.5%, 67 of 1,032, against 1.6% for everyone). The model should not be relied on to rank pedestrians.

Barcelona crashes: shares by accident type

The Barcelona crash model estimated whether a recorded crash involved a serious or fatal injury. On the 1,994 crashes of the test months (58 with such an injury) it ranked no better than the table of shares by accident type: ROC-AUC 0.74 against 0.73, a difference of 0.00 (95% interval −0.04 to 0.05). The table is simpler and ranks as well, so it is used in place of the model; below, it is computed over all crashes of the year.

Prediction and explanation

Both models are observational predictions: they learn which recorded circumstances go with a severe outcome among crashes that have already happened, and are judged by whether that pattern holds on later records. The Catalonia severity model shows in which kinds of road and crash the outcome is most often fatal and, within the population on which its calibration was checked, gives the number of fatal crashes to expect. The Barcelona person-severity model orders people by their relative chance of serious injury, without estimating how many will be hurt.

A ranking of this kind does not identify causes, for three reasons. First, the records contain no measure of exposure, so each model estimates severity given that a crash happened and was recorded (Methodology). Second, the variables a model relies on are bound up with others the records do not contain: the road's owner, for example, cannot itself injure anyone, and stands for differences between roads, from whether they are urban to features the records do not measure. Third, part of each record is written after the outcome is known, such as the police's judgement of which conditions influenced a Catalan crash or the causes they record in Barcelona; versions of the models that add this information rank better by less than 0.01 and are not used for prediction. In Barcelona, nine of the 22 recorded causes are too rare to model, among them excessive or inappropriate speed (38 crashes in 2025) and drugs or medication (8); the Barcelona page describes them.

Separating the effect of one factor from the circumstances it travels with requires a design these records do not provide: a comparison of otherwise similar roads, for example, or a change in one factor while the others stay fixed. The models describe where severe outcomes concentrate among recorded crashes.

Technical details: test metrics, rules and checks

For each model a logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees were fitted, and the method that ranked the validation records better was used: gradient-boosted trees for the Catalonia severity and Barcelona person-severity models; logistic regression for the Barcelona crash model. The Barcelona models' settings were chosen by cross-validation within the training months that keeps all the records of one crash together.

A model was preferred to its descriptive table only if its ROC-AUC exceeded the table's by at least 0.02, with a 95% interval for the difference above zero. Its probabilities were treated as estimates only if the average prediction differed from the observed share by no more than 25% of that share and the calibration slope lay between 0.8 and 1.25. Both rules were fixed before any result was read.

PR-AUC is the average precision over all thresholds; its chance level is the share of test records with the outcome. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the predicted probabilities (lower is better). Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals that resample crashes.
