Models

Models of crash and injury severity

Three models trained on Catalan and Barcelona crash records, each judged on later records it had not seen and against a simple table of the same records: two are kept, one is replaced by its table.

The project fitted three predictive models to individual crash records and kept only those that ranked later, unseen records better than a simple table of the same data. The Catalonia crash-severity model ranks crashes with a death or serious injury by how likely they were to be fatal. It is kept: it clearly beats its table, and on the held-out crashes of 2023 its probabilities matched the observed fatal shares well overall. The Barcelona person-severity model orders people involved in crashes from lower to higher predicted severity. It is kept for ranking only: it beats its table, but its scores are too extreme to read as probabilities, and it ranks pedestrians little better than chance. The Barcelona crash-severity model ranked crashes no better than a table of accident types, so it was dropped and the table is reported instead. None of the models predicts whether a crash will happen; they work only on crashes that happened and were recorded.

Catalonia crash-severity model Decision: Kept

Unit
One crash in Catalonia in which someone was killed or seriously injured
Outcome
Anyone killed within 24 hours
Test records
1,732 crashes of 2023, 209 fatal
Simple benchmark ROC-AUC
0.70, a table of fatal shares by crash subtype × zone
Model ROC-AUC
0.79 (95% interval 0.76–0.82)
Decision
Kept: it ranks crashes better than its table, and its probabilities can be read as estimates for Catalan crashes like those of 2023

The model estimates the probability that a crash in the Servei Català de Trànsit's file was fatal from what the police recorded about the date and time, the road (its type, owner, zone, junction, priority rules and posted speed limit), the conditions (road surface, light, weather, wind, fog and any special traffic measures), the type of crash, the number and kinds of vehicles and pedestrians involved and the province. It cannot predict whether a crash will happen: the file contains only crashes in which someone was killed or seriously injured, so the model says nothing about how likely a journey, or an ordinary crash, is to end in a death.

It was fitted to the crashes of 2010–2020, tuned on 2021–2022 and tested once on the 1,732 crashes of 2023, of which 209 (12.1%) were fatal. On those crashes, a simple table of fatal shares by crash type and zone (urban street, interurban road or road through a town) reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.70. The model reaches 0.79 (95% interval 0.76–0.82), a gain of 0.09 (0.07–0.12). It therefore separates fatal from non-fatal serious crashes clearly better than the simple grouping does. ROC-AUC measures ranking: 0.5 is no better than chance and 1 is a perfect ranking. A score of 0.79 means that if one fatal and one non-fatal crash are picked at random, the model gives the fatal crash the higher score 79% of the time. In the tenth of 2023 crashes it ranked highest (174), 42.0% were fatal, 73 of the 209; in the lowest tenth, 1.1% were.

On the held-out Catalan crashes of 2023, the predicted probabilities matched the observed fatal shares reasonably well overall: 11.9% predicted on average against 12.1% observed, and 44.8% against 42.0% in the highest tenth. The calibration slope is 0.97, close to the value of 1 at which predictions are neither too spread out nor too compressed. That calibration should not be assumed for every place or subgroup. In Barcelona city it did not carry over: trained on the rest of Catalonia and applied there, the same model has a slope of 0.44.

To see what the model relies on, each variable was shuffled at random in the test crashes and the fall in ROC-AUC measured. Shuffling the road's owner lowered it by 0.07, the zone by 0.06 and the crash type by 0.05; no other variable lowered it as much. The road's owner and the zone overlap: the owner is left blank for urban streets, so it also carries the urban–interurban distinction that the zone records. A variable the model relies on is not necessarily a cause of severe crashes: the road's owner stands in for differences between roads that the file does not measure.

The model was also tested on a province left out of training, on Barcelona city, and, in a version restricted to the variables DGT records in the same way, on DGT's records of serious crashes in the rest of Spain (External validation).

Barcelona person-severity model Decision: Ranking only

Unit
One person involved in a crash attended by Barcelona's police in 2025
Outcome
Seriously injured (more than 24 hours in hospital) or killed
Test records
4,049 people, October–December 2025; 58 seriously or fatally injured
Simple benchmark ROC-AUC
0.78, a table of shares by road-user role × vehicle group
Model ROC-AUC
0.84 (95% interval 0.81–0.87)
Decision
Ranking only: it orders people better than its table, but its scores are too extreme to read as probabilities

Each person in the Guàrdia Urbana's records is a driver, a passenger or a pedestrian. The model uses the person's age, sex and role, the vehicle on their record and, for a pedestrian, where they were struck, the type of crash and the vehicles involved, the hour and day of the week and the district. It cannot predict who will be in a crash: everyone in the data was in one, and the records hold no measure of how much each group travels.

It was fitted to the first nine months of 2025 and tested on the last three, in which 58 of 4,049 people (1.4%) were seriously or fatally injured. A table of shares by road-user role (driver, passenger or pedestrian) and the vehicle on the person's record reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.78; the model reaches 0.84 (95% interval 0.81–0.87), a gain of 0.06 (0.02–0.11). It ranks people better than the table, although with only 58 serious or fatal cases the gain is less precisely estimated than in Catalonia, and a single year of records allows no test on a later year. The fifth of people it ranked highest (810) held 39 of the 58 serious or fatal injuries; the two fifths it ranked lowest (1,620 people) held none.

Its probabilities cannot be read literally. They are right on average (1.4% predicted and observed) but too extreme: in the highest fifth of scores the model predicted 5.9% against 4.8% observed, and lower scores understated the observed share. The calibration slope is 0.63, well below 1. The model is therefore used only to order records from lower to higher predicted severity.

It should not be used to rank pedestrians. Because the test months hold too few serious cases to score groups, every person recorded in 2025 was scored by a version of the model fitted without their crash. The ROC-AUC is then 0.58 for pedestrians, little better than chance, although pedestrians are the road users most often seriously hurt (6.5%, against 1.6% for everyone); for people whose record names a car it is 0.94. Its ranking has been shown only for people in crashes recorded by Barcelona's police in 2025.

Replaced by table: the model ranks crashes no better than the table, which is shown below instead

The model used the type of crash and the vehicles involved, the hour and day of the week and the district. On the 1,994 crashes of the test months (58 with a serious or fatal injury) it reached a ROC-AUC of 0.74, against 0.73 for a table of shares by accident type: a difference of 0.00 (95% interval −0.04 to 0.05). A ROC-AUC of 0.74 is well above chance, but the one-column table ranks crashes as well, so the table is used in place of the model. The table below is computed over all crashes of the year.

How the models were judged

Each model's benchmark was a table of the share of severe outcomes in each group of the training records, using the grouping a descriptive analysis would lead with, fixed before the test. A model was kept only if it beat its table by at least 0.02 of ROC-AUC, with a 95% interval for the difference above zero, and its probabilities were read as estimates only if their average was within 25% of the observed share and the calibration slope lay between 0.8 and 1.25. Both rules were set before any test result was read.

What the models do not show

The models rank recorded crashes and people by how severe the outcome was, using associations in the records. They do not explain why outcomes were severe, and the records contain no measure of how much anyone travels. Information recorded after the outcome was known, such as the causes the Barcelona police record, is left out of the models used for prediction.

Detailed model diagnostics

For each model a logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees were fitted, and the method that ranked the validation records better was used: gradient-boosted trees for the Catalonia crash and Barcelona person models; logistic regression for the Barcelona crash model. The Barcelona models' settings were chosen by cross-validation within the training months that keeps all the records of one crash together. PR-AUC is the average precision over all thresholds; its chance level is the share of test records with the outcome. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the predicted probabilities (lower is better). Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals that resample crashes.

Some causes recorded by the Barcelona police are too rare to model at all, among them excessive or inappropriate speed (38 crashes in 2025) and drugs or medication (8).

Replacing the broad place variable with the most detailed one raises the fit on the training records and lowers the test score, the pattern of a model that memorises places. With municipality in place of province, the Catalonia crash-severity model reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.89 on its training records but 0.78 on the test records, against 0.79 with province. With neighbourhood in place of district, the Barcelona person-severity model reaches a ROC-AUC of 1.00 on its training records but 0.81 on the test records, against 0.84 with district. The broad variables are used.
