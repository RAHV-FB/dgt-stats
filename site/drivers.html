Spain

Driver age and sex

How often car drivers of each age are involved in injury crashes for the distance they drive, how often a crash kills them, and how men and women compare.

Per kilometre driven, car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes in Spain in 2024 about 2.5 times as often as drivers aged 45–64 (95% interval 2.25–2.82). The kilometres by age are estimated from a Barcelona-area travel survey, and other defensible assumptions about them put the ratio anywhere from 1.6 to 3.6: young drivers are clearly involved more often, but by how much is uncertain. Drivers aged 65 and over were involved slightly more often than the middle-aged, 1.19 times (1.03–1.36; 0.99–1.65 under the other assumptions), and in Barcelona on working days, a separate check, at about the same rate (0.80–1.11, every interval including 1). For drivers aged 75 and over the estimate depends on how the 65-and-over kilometres are split: 1.1 to 3.1 times the middle-aged rate nationally, and not clearly above it in Barcelona. What sets older drivers apart is the outcome once a crash happens: 15.9 of every 1,000 car drivers aged 75 and over involved in an injury crash died within 30 days, against 4.6 at 45–64.

Involvement in crashes per kilometre driven

2.53×

Car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes 2.53 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (Spain, 2024; 95% interval 2.25–2.82; sensitivity range 1.64–3.63).

No national source counts kilometres by the age of the driver, so they are estimated. The EMEF, the working-day mobility survey of the province of Barcelona, records the car trips its respondents drove on one working day, with each trip's duration and, from 2021, its straight-line distance in bands; each trip's kilometres are estimated from those. From its 2022–2024 editions come each age group's car-driver kilometres per resident, by sex. Applied to the population of Spain and scaled to DGT's 2024 car kilometres from inspection odometer readings (less taxis and ride-hailing cars, whose drivers are outside both counts), they put 11.4% of car kilometres with drivers aged 18–29, 47.7% with 45–64 and 13.0% with 65 and over. The numerator is DGT's count of private-car drivers involved in injury crashes in 2024, whoever caused the crash.

Two of those choices concern the survey's older respondents. The share of them in work was about 4.3% in 2019–2021, the same as the census for Catalonia, but 6.0% to 8.3% in 2022–2024; setting it back to the census share moves the 65-and-over ratio from 1.19 to 1.23. And the survey does not record the work trips of people who drive for a living, most of them under 65: counting a quarter or a half of those trips as car trips raises the ratio to 1.26–1.33. Both point the same way, so the central figure for 65 and over is more likely too low than too high.

The kilometres by age rest on one region's survey transferred to Spain, so the ratios are estimates with ranges, not measurements. The method, its checks and every sensitivity analysis are set out in the exposure study.

A check in Barcelona city on working days

In Barcelona the comparison can be made inside one city on working days, without the transfer to Spain or an assumption about weekends. The Guàrdia Urbana's 2025 records give the age of 93.9% of the car drivers involved in injury crashes in the city on working days, uninjured drivers included; the EMEF gives the kilometres the survey area's residents drove inside the city on a working day in 2022–2024. The 248 working days of 2025 (weekdays less the Catalan and Barcelona public holidays) are counted. The part of a trip into or out of the city that lies inside it cannot be measured, so the table gives three versions of the kilometres, and only the ratios between ages are read.

Ages 75 and over

No source available to this study measures driving at 75 and over separately from 65–74; the figures in this section depend on an assumption and are labelled model-dependent.

The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, although the survey sampled 65–74 and 75 and over separately until 2016; a request for the split has been prepared. The Madrid household travel survey of 2018 records exact ages: there, residents aged 75 and over drove 0.34 times the distance per resident of those aged 65–74 if men and 0.28 times if women. Deaths once a crash has happened

3.4×

Car drivers aged 75 and over who were involved in an injury crash died within 30 days 3.4 times as often as drivers aged 45–64 (15.9 against 4.6 per 1,000 drivers involved, 2024).

This rate divides the car drivers killed within 30 days by all car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not, of the same age and year, so no measure of driving enters it. It is 4.1 per 1,000 at 18–29, 3.9 at 30–44, 4.6 at 45–64, 8.5 at 65–74 and 15.9 at 75 and over. Combined with involvement per kilometre, it means that drivers aged 65 and over were killed 2.9 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (2.21–3.82), although they were involved in crashes only 1.2 times as often: their excess deaths come mainly from the outcome once involved, not from being involved in more crashes.

The rate counts only the driver's own death. DGT's tables do not say how often other people in the same crashes died, so it cannot show whether older drivers' crashes are more dangerous to others; and involvement counts every driver in an injury crash, so neither rate says who caused it.

Why the former figure differed

This page used to divide the same drivers, taxi and ride-hailing drivers included, by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, the only kilometres by age that DGT publishes. With the age groups and reference used now, those owner kilometres put drivers aged 18–29 at 4.62 times the 45–64 rate and drivers aged 65 and over at 1.00; the driver-age kilometres give 2.53 and 1.19. (The figure the page used to show, 6.75, compared drivers aged 18–24 with 35–54.) A car's owner is often not its driver: young people drive cars registered to their parents, and older owners' cars are partly driven by others. The owner kilometres therefore credited too little driving to the young and too much to the old.

Men and women

The survey's kilometres can be split by sex and carried to Spain in the same way as by age. On that estimate men drove 66.4% of car kilometres in 2024, and per kilometre male private-car drivers aged 18 and over were involved in injury crashes about as often as female drivers (0.91 times; 0.85–0.98; 0.60–1.23 under the other regional profiles) but killed 2.6 times as often (2.10–3.38; 1.75–3.55). Per licence holder, pooling 2022–2024, men were involved 1.39 times as often as women (1.38–1.40) and, once involved, died 2.62 times as often (2.28–3.00); combined, they died at the wheel 3.63 times as often (3.17–4.15). So men's higher involvement per licence holder comes mostly from driving further, while their higher death rate holds per kilometre and once a crash has happened. The gap once involved holds in every age band.

Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders, the drivers of vans, trucks and buses and other motor vehicles, the gaps are wider: men died 6.42 times as often as women per licence holder and 3.23 times as often once involved. The licence holders are holders of any class of licence; counting only car-licence holders, which the census gives by sex and age for 2023–2024 only, raises the men's excess in deaths per licence holder from 3.49 to 3.57 in those years.
