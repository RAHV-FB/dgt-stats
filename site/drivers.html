Spain

Driver age and sex

How often car drivers of each age are involved in injury crashes for the distance they drive, how often a crash kills them, and how men and women compare.

Per kilometre driven, car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes about 2.6 times as often as drivers aged 45–64 in 2024 (95% interval 2.28–2.86). Drivers aged 65 and over were involved about as often as the middle-aged, or modestly more (1.16; 1.00–1.35), and in Barcelona on working days, where the comparison is matched in place and time, about as often or less. Above 75 the evidence is model-dependent: per kilometre, drivers aged 75 and over appear to be involved 1.3 to 2.2 times as often as the middle-aged, and drivers aged 65–74 at the middle-aged rate. What sets older drivers apart is the outcome once a crash happens: 15.9 of every 1,000 drivers aged 75 and over involved in an injury crash died within 30 days, against 4.6 at 45–64.

Involvement in crashes per kilometre driven

2.57×

Car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes 2.57 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (Spain, 2024; 95% interval 2.28–2.86; sensitivity range 1.64–3.65).

No national source counts kilometres by the age of the driver, so the kilometres are estimated. The EMEF, the annual working-day mobility survey of the province of Barcelona, records every car trip its respondents drive on one working day; from its 2022–2024 editions comes each age group's car-driver kilometres per resident, by sex. Applied to the population of Spain and scaled to DGT's 2024 car kilometres from inspection odometer readings (less taxis and ride-hailing cars, whose drivers are outside both counts), it puts 11.3% of car kilometres with drivers aged 18–29, 47.7% with 45–64 and 13.4% with 65 and over. The numerator is DGT's count of private-car drivers involved in injury crashes in 2024, whoever caused the crash.

The interval and the sensitivity range answer different questions. The interval is sampling and count error. The range is how far the ratio moves under other defensible choices: the age profile of another part of the province or of the Madrid household survey of 2018 in place of the province's, and a different age mix on weekends and holidays, which the survey does not cover. The transfer from one region to Spain matters most for the youngest and oldest groups, whose driving varies most between city and country. The level of the rates also depends on DGT's kilometre total (from 292 to 308 per billion km for the 65-and-over group across the variants), but their ratios do not.

The kilometres by age rest on one region's survey transferred to Spain, so the ratios are estimates with ranges, not measurements. The method, its checks and every sensitivity analysis are set out in the exposure study.

A check matched in place and time: Barcelona on working days

Barcelona allows a comparison that needs neither the transfer to Spain nor an assumption about weekends. The Guàrdia Urbana's 2025 records give the exact age of every driver involved in a crash in the city, uninjured drivers included, and its date; the EMEF gives the kilometres driven inside the city on a working day. The 248 working days of 2025 (weekdays less the Catalan and Barcelona public holidays) are matched to them. The part of a trip into or out of the city that lies inside it cannot be measured, so three denominators bracket it, and only the ratios between ages are read.

Ages 75 and over

No source available to this study measures driving at 75 and over separately from 65–74; the figures in this section depend on an assumption and are labelled model-dependent.

The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, although the survey sampled 65–74 and 75 and over separately until 2016; a request for the split has been prepared. The Madrid household travel survey of 2018 records exact ages: there, residents aged 75 and over drove 0.34 times the distance per resident of those aged 65–74 if men and 0.28 times if women. Deaths once a crash has happened

3.4×

Car drivers aged 75 and over who were involved in an injury crash died within 30 days 3.4 times as often as drivers aged 45–64 (15.9 against 4.6 per 1,000 drivers involved, 2024).

This rate divides the car drivers killed within 30 days by all car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not, of the same age and year, so no measure of driving enters it. It is 4.1 per 1,000 at 18–29, 3.9 at 30–44, 4.6 at 45–64, 8.5 at 65–74 and 15.9 at 75 and over. Combined with involvement per kilometre, it means that drivers aged 65 and over were killed 2.9 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (2.12–3.74), although they were involved in crashes only 1.2 times as often: their excess deaths come from the outcome once involved, not from being involved in more crashes.

The rate counts only the driver's own death. DGT's tables do not say how often other people in the same crashes died, so it cannot show whether older drivers' crashes are more dangerous to others; and involvement counts every driver in an injury crash, so neither rate says who caused it.

Why the former figure differed

This page used to divide the same drivers by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, the only kilometres by age that DGT publishes. On that basis drivers aged 18–24 were involved 6.75 times as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54, and drivers aged 65–74 0.71 times as often. A car's owner is often not its driver: young people drive cars registered to their parents, and older owners' cars are partly driven by others. The owner kilometres therefore credited too little driving to the young and too much to the old, which inflated the young drivers' rate and deflated the older drivers'. Measured by the driver's age, the young drivers' excess is about two and a half times, not nearly seven.

Men and women

No source records kilometres driven by sex, so men and women aged 18 and over are compared per licence holder and per driver involved, pooling 2022–2024. Male car drivers were involved in injury crashes 1.41 times as often as female drivers per licence holder (1.40–1.42) and, once involved, died 2.57 times as often (2.24–2.94). Combined, per licence holder, men aged 18 and over died at the wheel 3.63 times as often as women (3.17–4.15). The gap once involved holds in every age band. Involvement per licence holder combines how far men and women drive with how often they crash per kilometre, and no source separates the two.

Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders and the drivers of vans, trucks and buses, the gaps are wider: men died 6.42 times as often as women per licence holder and 3.23 times as often once involved. The licence holders are holders of any class of licence; counting only car-licence holders, published by sex for the latest years only, makes the men's excess slightly larger.
