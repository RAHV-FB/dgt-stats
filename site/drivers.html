Drivers, vehicles and factors

Driver age and sex

No national source records how far drivers of each age drive, so involvement in crashes per kilometre is an estimate, built from a working-day travel survey of the Barcelona area. Carried to Spain's population, that survey's working-day driving accounts for 51% of DGT's car kilometres, and the central estimate gives the rest the same age mix. On that estimate, car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes in Spain in 2024 2.53 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 2.2–2.8; sensitivity range, the span across the assumptions tested, 1.49–3.63), and more often under every assumption tested. Drivers aged 65 and over were involved 1.19 times as often (95% sampling interval 1.0–1.4; sensitivity range 0.85–1.82), so whether they are involved more or less often per kilometre is not established. For drivers aged 75 and over, whose kilometres no source for Spain separates from those at 65–74, the sensitivity range is 0.97 to 3.20 times the 45–64 rate, so the data do not establish that they are involved more often per kilometre than drivers aged 45–64, or by how much. If people aged 75 and over drive as much less than those aged 65–74 as in Madrid in 2018, on the same estimate they were involved about 2.1 times as often (95% sampling interval 1.6–2.6), a conditional estimate that holds only on that assumption. Counted directly, with no kilometres, 15.9 of every 1,000 car drivers aged 75 and over involved in an injury crash died within 30 days, against 4.6 at 45–64. Involvement counts every driver in a crash, whoever caused it, and the death rate only the driver's own death: no rate here shows who caused a crash or whether older drivers' crashes are more dangerous to others.

Crashes per kilometre: above the 45–64 rate at 18–29; at 65 and over it depends on assumptions

No national source counts kilometres by the age of the driver, so they are estimated, and the ratios per kilometre are estimates with ranges, not measurements; the method and its checks are set out in the exposure study. The EMEF, the working-day mobility survey of the province of Barcelona, records the car trips its respondents drove on one working day, with each trip's duration and, from 2021, its straight-line distance in bands, from which each trip's kilometres are estimated. Its 2022–2024 editions give each age group's car-driver kilometres per resident, by sex. Applied to the population of Spain and scaled to DGT's 2024 car kilometres from inspection odometer readings, less taxis and ride-hailing cars (whose drivers are outside both counts), they put 11.4% of car kilometres with drivers aged 18–29, 47.7% with 45–64 and 13.0% with 65 and over. Over the 248 working days of 2024 (weekdays less public holidays), the survey's driving comes to 51% of those kilometres. The central estimate gives the rest the same age mix; the tests below measure what the rest is made of and give it other age mixes. The drivers involved are DGT's count of private-car drivers in injury crashes in 2024.

Weekends and public holidays, 118 days in 2024, add 15% to 30% of DGT's total if each carries 60% to 100% of a working day's driving; MOVILIA 2006 counted 0.83 times as many car trips, drivers and passengers together, on an average weekend day as on a working day, and weekend trips of all modes lasted 1.24 times as long. Judged by fuel sales and toll-motorway traffic, the months outside the survey's fieldwork change the total by −1% to 2%. At their lowest these parts leave 29% of DGT's total unexplained; at their highest they exceed it by 3%. Earlier versions of this page divided the same drivers, taxi and ride-hailing drivers included, by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, the only kilometres by age that DGT publishes. On the age groups used here, those owner kilometres put drivers aged 18–29 at 4.62 times the 45–64 rate and drivers aged 65 and over at 1.00, against 2.53 and 1.19 here; the figure the page used to give, 6.75, compared drivers aged 18–24 with 35–54. A car's owner is often not its driver: young people drive cars registered to their parents, and older owners' cars are partly driven by others, so the owner kilometres credited too little driving to the young and too much to the old.

Deaths once a crash has happened: highest at 75 and over

This rate divides the car drivers killed within 30 days by all car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not, of the same age and year, so no measure of driving enters it. It counts only the driver's own death: DGT's tables do not say how often other people in the same crashes died. In 2024 it was 4.1 per 1,000 at 18–29, 3.9 at 30–44, 4.6 at 45–64, 8.5 at 65–74 and 15.9 at 75 and over, about 3.4 times the 45–64 rate.

Deaths per kilometre combine the two measures, so they carry the estimate of kilometres. On the central estimate, drivers aged 65 and over were killed 2.92 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 2.3–3.9; sensitivity range 2.09–4.46), while their involvement per kilometre was 1.19 times (sensitivity range 0.85–1.82). Under every assumption tested the first ratio is above the second, so most of the excess in deaths per kilometre comes from how often a crash kills an older driver, not from how often older drivers are in crashes.

Inside Barcelona on working days, involvement per kilometre can be compared without carrying the survey to Spain or assuming anything about weekends. The Guàrdia Urbana's 2025 records give the age of 93.9% of the car drivers involved in injury crashes in the city on working days, uninjured drivers included; the EMEF gives the kilometres the survey area's residents drove inside the city on a working day in 2022–2024, counted over the 248 working days of 2025 (weekdays less the Catalan and Barcelona public holidays). The part of a trip into or out of the city that lies inside it cannot be measured, so the table gives three versions of the kilometres, and only the ratios between ages are read.

75 and over: deaths once involved are counted; crashes per kilometre depend on how much less they drive

No source for Spain measures how far drivers aged 75 and over drive apart from those aged 65–74. The rates per kilometre below rest on an assumption about how the 65-and-over kilometres divide between the two ages: one is given as a conditional estimate, the others in the sensitivity range. The counts, the rates per licence holder and the deaths once involved need no kilometres.

Counted. In 2024, 4,455 car drivers aged 75 and over and 6,970 aged 65–74 were involved in injury crashes in Spain, and 71 and 59 of them died within 30 days: 15.9 and 8.5 per 1,000 involved, against 4.6 at 45–64 (Figure 2). These figures say how often a crash kills the driver. They do not say how often drivers of these ages are in crashes for the distance they drive, or who caused the crashes. Per 1,000 holders of a car licence, 2.84 drivers aged 75 and over were involved, against 2.13 at 65–74 and 2.98 at 45–64. Many licence holders aged 75 and over drive little or not at all, so these are not rates per driver. Every estimate per kilometre below is higher than the ratio of these rates, 0.95, because under every assumption tested licence holders aged 75 and over drive fewer kilometres than those aged 45–64.

Conditional estimate. The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, although the survey sampled 65–74 and 75 and over separately until 2016. Madrid's 2018 household travel survey records exact ages: there, residents aged 75 and over drove 0.34 times the car distance per resident of those aged 65–74 if men and 0.28 times if women. If people aged 75 and over in Spain in 2024 drive as much less as in Madrid, with the other assumptions as in the central estimate, drivers aged 75 and over were involved in injury crashes 2.06 times as often per kilometre as drivers aged 45–64 (95% sampling interval 1.6–2.6), and drivers aged 65–74 0.94 times (0.8–1.1). The interval allows for sampling error in both surveys and chance in the crash counts, and holds the assumption fixed. It may be too narrow or too wide: the Barcelona-area survey's resampling treats respondents as independent although the survey samples them in clusters, which makes an interval too narrow, and the Madrid survey's ignores the stratification of its sample, which makes one too wide. The estimate takes the Barcelona-area survey's kilometres at 65 and over as they stand: a mean over the province of Barcelona's residents of those ages, a slightly larger share of whom are aged 75 and over than in Spain. Standardised to Spain's mix of ages, as the Madrid profile in the sensitivity range is, they would make this figure and the 65–74 figure slightly lower. Under the other assumptions tested the figure for 75 and over runs from 0.97 to 3.20 (the sensitivity range, below).

Why this assumption, and what could move it
  • It uses only kilometres measured by exact age; the second split, Madrid's kilometres per licence holder, also needs DGT's counts of licence holders, taken six years after the survey.
  • Car-licence holding falls about equally from 65–74 to 75 and over in the province of Madrid, the province of Barcelona and Spain (holders per resident at 75 and over against 65–74, DGT, 2024: men 0.72, 0.72, 0.71; women 0.28, 0.28, 0.26). That is consistent with the assumption but does not test it: the Madrid survey has residents aged 65 and over driving much less, relative to those aged 45–64, than the Barcelona-area survey does. Each survey's profile carried to Spain's population gives 0.28 against 0.41 times as far per resident on working days; among each survey's own residents the figures are 0.29 and 0.40.
  • Some evidence points lower: licence holding at 75 and over has risen since 2018, more than at 65–74, and surveys in other European countries show a gentler fall in driving after 75 (see the exposure study).
  • Some points higher: the Barcelona-area survey's weighting does not separate 75 and over within 65 and over, so its 65-and-over sample may hold too few people aged 75 and over; at the most this would raise the figure to about 2.28, a case included in the sensitivity range. And applied to Spain's licence holders, the Madrid pattern implies that women aged 75 and over with a car licence drive 1.08 times as far as women aged 65–74 with one, while Spanish surveys of women's driving, though too uncertain to rule that out, point to less; less driving by these women would raise the figure.
  • Which way the figure errs is not known.

Across the assumptions. Four splits of the 65-and-over kilometres were tested: Madrid's, used for the estimate; Madrid's kilometres per licence holder applied to Spain's licence holders; an upper limit for men from the number of days they drive, with women driving as far per licence holder at 75 and over as at 65–74; and equal kilometres per licence holder at 65–74 and 75 and over. Each split was combined with every alternative for the 65-and-over kilometres. Of these, the region whose age profile stands in for Spain's (the province of Barcelona's, its four parts, Madrid's, or the province's carried per licence holder) was crossed with the age mixes for weekends and holidays and for the kilometres no part explains; trip distances, survey years, work driving, the older respondents' employment and age mix, and a weekend age mix on its own were varied one at a time, each with everything else as in the central estimate. Together they give a sensitivity range of 0.97 to 3.20 at 75 and over and 0.67 to 1.70 at 65–74. A sensitivity range is not an interval: it carries no probability, and it is not a limit, because the choices varied one at a time are not combined with each other or with the crossed ones, which would move both ends. The split with equal kilometres per licence holder stays in the range: women's driving does not rule it out, and the evidence against it for men comes from regional or dated surveys rather than a national measure of kilometres. The three allocations of the unexplained kilometres reported above stay out of it, for the reasons given there; at 75 and over, across the profiles and splits, they would give 1.04–1.94 with the same kilometres per licence holder at every age, 1.62–4.63 with no driving at 65 and over, and 1.10–2.56 with DGT's kilometres by the owner's age.

Among the other combinations tested, the lowest, about 1.2, combines Barcelona city's age profile, weekend age mixes for the kilometres outside the survey's working days and the RACC limit; this edge is set by that limit. Its 95% sampling interval, 0.9–1.8, includes the 45–64 rate. There drivers aged 75 and over would drive 42% of the kilometres DGT records for cars registered to owners of that age, against 105% at 45–64; owner figures cannot say who drove, so this is noted, not counted against it.

In Barcelona's working-day check, with no transfer to Spain (the 65-and-over kilometres are still divided by each split), the four splits give 0.80–1.70 at 75 and over across the three ways of counting trips that cross the city boundary, below the 45–64 rate in some combinations and above it in others. With 120 drivers aged 75 and over, the Madrid split's 95% sampling intervals (0.8–1.8, 1.2–2.3 and 1.0–2.0) are wide: one includes the 45–64 rate, one lies above it and one ends so close to it that another set of resamples could put its lower end on either side. The figures disagree with each other, so the check neither confirms nor rules out a rate above the 45–64 rate.

Two kinds of data would narrow this range. The EMEF's own records hold each respondent's exact age, so its kilometres at 65–74 and at 75 and over, tabulated by the Institut Metròpoli or the ATM, would replace the transfer of Madrid's pattern; a request for those tables has been prepared but not sent (the data request). A survey of driving on every day of the week across Spain, by exact age, would replace the assumptions about weekends and about the kilometres the working-day survey does not cover.

Drivers who drive few kilometres, at any age, tend to have more crashes per kilometre, partly because more of their driving is on streets with junctions and less on motorways, so a higher rate per kilometre at 75 and over would not by itself show that age makes driving less safe. These rates are averages over everyone of an age who drives and say nothing about any one driver. They count involvement in injury crashes, whoever caused them; responsibility is not measured. The higher deaths once involved are counted and do not depend on any of these assumptions. The Madrid survey data are reused under the open-data licence of the Consorcio Regional de Transportes de Madrid. Powered by CRTM.

Changed in October 2026: this page used to divide the drivers involved by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, and on those kilometres said that drivers aged 75 and over were involved about as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54 (1.02 times). A car's owner is often not its driver: owners aged 75 and over have 1.14 cars registered to them for every car-licence holder of that age, more cars than holders, and others drive some of them, so those kilometres do not measure how far drivers of that age drive. The page now estimates the kilometres driven by drivers of each age and gives, for 75 and over, a conditional estimate beside the sensitivity range; the former figure is compared like for like at the end of the section on crashes per kilometre.

Men and women: no clear difference in crashes per kilometre, more deaths among men

The survey's kilometres can be split by sex and carried to Spain in the same way as by age. On that estimate men drove 66.4% of car kilometres in 2024. Per kilometre, male private-car drivers aged 18 and over were involved in injury crashes 0.91 times as often as female drivers (95% sampling interval 0.86–0.98), but across the regional profiles that ratio runs from 0.60 to 1.23, so neither sex is shown to be involved more often per kilometre. Men were killed 2.65 times as often (95% sampling interval 2.1–3.4; sensitivity range 1.75–3.55). Per licence holder, pooling 2022–2024, men were involved 1.39 times as often as women (95% interval 1.38–1.40) and, once involved, died 2.62 times as often (2.28–3.00); combined, they died at the wheel 3.63 times as often (3.17–4.15). Under the central profile, men's higher involvement per licence holder comes from their driving further; their higher death rate holds per kilometre under every profile and, in every age band, once a crash has happened.

Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders, the drivers of vans, trucks and buses and other motor vehicles, the gaps are wider: men died 6.42 times as often as women per licence holder and 3.23 times as often once involved. The licence holders are holders of any class of licence. The census gives car-licence holders by sex and age for 2023–2024 only; counting only them raises the ratio of men's to women's deaths per licence holder in those years from 3.49 to 3.57.
