Spain

Driver age and sex

Car drivers compared by age and by sex, using DGT's counts of drivers involved in injury crashes and killed, set against the measures of driving available for each group.

Once they are in an injury crash, car drivers aged 75 and over die far more often than middle-aged drivers: in 2024, 15.9 of every 1,000 involved died within 30 days, against 4.1 per 1,000 at 35–54. That comparison uses only crash records. How often each age group is involved in crashes for the distance it drives is less certain, because DGT counts kilometres by the age of a car's registered owner, not its driver. On those kilometres, drivers aged 18–24 were involved in 6.75 times as many injury crashes per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54; for them to be involved no more often, each would have to drive about twice as far as the licence holders of any owner age group are credited with. Drivers aged 75 and over were involved about as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54, and 1.43 times as often as drivers aged 65–74. Per licence holder, men died at the wheel of a car 3.63 times as often as women in 2022–2024.

Deaths once a crash has happened

3.93×

Car drivers aged 75 and over who were involved in an injury crash died within 30 days 3.93 times as often as drivers aged 35–54 (15.9 against 4.1 per 1,000 drivers involved, 2024; 95% interval 2.98–5.18).

This rate divides the car drivers killed within 30 days by all car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not, of the same age in the same year. Both counts come from DGT's tables of drivers involved in crashes, so no measure of driving enters it. It rises with every band after 35–54: 5.3 deaths per 1,000 drivers involved at 55–64, 8.4 at 65–74 and 15.9 at 75 and over. Drivers aged 18–24 (4.2) and 25–34 (3.6) cannot be distinguished from those aged 35–54 (4.1): the 95% intervals of their ratios to 35–54, 0.75–1.41 and 0.67–1.18, both include 1.

The rate counts only the driver's own death. The tables do not say how often other people in the same crashes died, so the measure cannot show whether older drivers' crashes are more dangerous to others.

Crashes relative to kilometres by owner age

The rates in this section rest on kilometres counted by the age of a car's registered owner, not its driver, so they are less certain than the deaths once involved above.

DGT estimates the kilometres driven by cars in 2024 from odometer readings taken at roadworthiness (ITV) inspections, and publishes them by the age of each car's registered owner. No source gives kilometres by the driver's age. The rates per kilometre on this page therefore divide the drivers of each age involved in injury crashes by the kilometres of cars registered to owners of the same age. On those kilometres, drivers aged 18–24 were involved in 2,351 injury crashes per billion km and drivers aged 25–34 in 637, against 348 at 35–54, 252 at 55–64, 247 at 65–74 and 354 at 75 and over.

Few cars are registered to young owners. For every holder of a B (car) licence, there are 0.23 cars and 3,095 km a year registered to owners aged 18–24, 0.56 cars and 7,631 km at 25–34, and 0.80 cars and 10,345 km at 35–54. The gap can reflect less driving by young licence holders, their use of cars registered to older people or to companies, or a combination of these. The source cannot separate them. Kilometres that young people drive in cars registered to others are counted in another band, which makes their published rates per kilometre too high.

A sensitivity scenario, the grey band in the chart, shows how much this could change the result. It moves kilometres from the 35–54 band to the two young bands until all three have the same kilometres per licence holder (9,030 a year), as if the whole gap were young people driving cars registered to owners aged 35–54. The reassignment is deliberately extreme: 15.3 billion km, 12.7% of the 35–54 kilometres. Under it, drivers aged 18–24 were involved in injury crashes 2.02 times as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54, against 6.75 times on the published owner-age kilometres; for drivers aged 25–34 the figures are 1.35 and 1.83. The two values form a sensitivity range, not a confidence interval: the data do not show how many kilometres should be reassigned between age groups, so the true ratio need not lie between them.

The distance each age group would have to drive

The owner-age kilometres cannot be corrected without knowing who drives each car, and the data do not say: DGT publishes drivers' recorded infractions by vehicle type, not by age, and its national crash file has no records of drivers. The question can be turned round. The table gives, for each age group, the distance each B-licence holder would have to drive in a year for the group to be involved in injury crashes no more often per kilometre than drivers aged 35–54, beside the kilometres per licence holder credited to cars of owners that age.

Drivers aged 18–24 would each have to drive about 20,900 km a year (95% interval 20,500–21,300): 6.75 times the kilometres credited to cars of owners their age, and about twice the most credited per licence holder to any owner age group (10,734 at 55–64). Even after the scenario moves kilometres to them, they would need 18,200. Their higher involvement per kilometre therefore does not depend on the owner-age kilometres being right. Drivers aged 25–34 would need about 14,000 km, 1.30 times the most credited to any owner age group, so their higher involvement is likely but less clear-cut. Drivers aged 55–64 and 65–74 would be involved as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35–54 if they drove about 28% and 29% less than the kilometres credited to their cars. The data cannot rule that out, so their lower involvement per kilometre is less firmly established than the young drivers' higher one.

For drivers aged 75 and over the distance needed to match drivers aged 35–54, about 8,200 km, is almost exactly what is credited to cars of owners their age (8,023), so that comparison turns on whether they drive more or less than their own cars' kilometres. Owners aged 75 and over also have 1.14 cars per licence holder, the only band with more cars than licence holders, which shows again that owner age cannot be treated as driver age; the data do not identify who drives those cars. Drivers aged 65–74 give a closer comparison. Against them, on the published owner-age kilometres, drivers aged 75 and over were involved in injury crashes 1.43 times as often per kilometre (1.38–1.49) and died 1.90 times as often once involved (1.34–2.68). To be involved no more often per kilometre than drivers aged 65–74, they would each need to drive about 11,500 km a year (95% interval 11,100–11,900), more than is credited per licence holder to any owner age group. The data give no sign that they do: cars registered to owners aged 75 and over are driven fewer kilometres a year (7,031 per car) than the cars of any other owner age group.

Men and women

No source records kilometres driven by sex, so men and women are compared per licence holder and per driver involved, pooling 2022–2024. Male car drivers were involved in injury crashes 1.41 times as often as female drivers per licence holder (1.40–1.42) and, once involved, died 2.57 times as often (2.24–2.94). Combined, per licence holder, men died at the wheel 3.63 times as often as women (3.17–4.15). The gap once involved holds in every age band. Involvement per licence holder combines how far men and women drive with how often they crash per kilometre, and no source separates the two.

Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders and the drivers of vans, trucks and buses, the gaps are wider: men died 6.42 times as often as women per licence holder and 3.23 times as often once involved.

The same deaths under four denominators

Against drivers aged 35–54, car drivers aged 75 and over died 1.23× as often per resident, 3.10× per holder of a B permit, 3.99× per kilometre on the published owner-age kilometres and 3.93× per driver involved. The deaths are the same in each case; only the denominator changes. Dividing by licence holders allows for how many people in each band hold a licence: in 2024, 37.9% of residents aged 75 and over held one, against 77.9%–82.9% at 35–54. Dividing by kilometres allows for distance driven only as far as the kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age reflect it. Dividing by drivers involved removes crash frequency and leaves severity. Drivers aged 18–24 are missing from this comparison because INE's resident counts are grouped as 15–19 and 20–24.

Company cars and the kilometre denominator

Cars registered to companies, 13.8% of car kilometres, have no owner age, so their kilometres drop out of the denominator while their drivers stay in the numerator. Spreading those kilometres over every band in proportion changes no ratio. Spreading them over the bands from 18 to 64 raises the ratio of deaths per kilometre at 75 and over from 3.99× to 4.76×. Deaths once involved are unaffected.

LimitationsDGT describes its kilometre estimates as valid only in aggregate, and the intervals treat them as exact.
