Age and sex: how often drivers crash, and how often a crash kills them

Are older drivers, or male drivers, more dangerous? Deaths per kilometre driven are crashes per kilometre times deaths per crash; splitting the two shows which one makes the difference for each group.

A driver's risk of being killed for each kilometre driven is how often they crash per kilometre times how often a crash kills them. Split that way, the two ends of the age range are dangerous for opposite reasons. Car drivers aged 75 and over are killed 3.99× (3.03–5.27) as often per kilometre as drivers aged 35 to 54: they are in injury crashes 1.02× as often per kilometre, and a crash kills them 3.93× as often. Drivers aged 18 to 34 are killed 2.43× (1.92–3.08) as often per kilometre: they crash 2.57× as often, and a crash kills them no more often: 0.95× (0.75–1.20). Men are killed 3.63× as often per licence holder as women: 1.41× the crashes and 2.57× the deaths per crash.

Crashes per kilometre: lowest at 55–74, not flat

Against drivers aged 35 to 54, those 75 and over crash 1.02× (0.99–1.05) as often per kilometre, which on its own reads as no difference. The comparison hides the shape of the curve. The crash rate per kilometre falls from its peak at 18–34 to a low at 55–64 and 65–74 (0.72× and 0.71× the 35–54 rate) and rises again after 75. Against the drivers just younger than them, aged 65 to 74, drivers of 75 and over crash 1.43× (1.38–1.49) as often per kilometre and are killed 1.90× (1.34–2.68) as often per crash, 2.71× (1.92–3.83) per kilometre in all.

Part of that rise may be how little they drive rather than their age. Drivers who cover few kilometres crash more per kilometre at any age, because more of their driving is short trips in towns, with more junctions per kilometre (Langford, Methorst & Hakamies-Blomqvist 2006). A car whose owner is 75 or over covers 7,031 km a year against 12,899 for owners aged 35 to 54. These tables cannot separate mileage from age.

Deaths per crash: what a crash does to the driver

Against drivers aged 35 to 54, the share of drivers in an injury crash who are killed rises with every band after it: 1.32× at 55–64, 2.07× at 65–74 and 3.93× at 75 and over; drivers aged 18 to 34 are at 0.95×. That is what physical frailty predicts: the same crash does more harm to an older body. US data show the same split (Li, Braver & Chen 2003): the high death rate per mile of older drivers came mainly from their fragility, much less from crashing more. The measure counts only the driver's own death; DGT's tables do not say how often the other people in those crashes die.

Residents, licences, crashes, kilometres

The same driver deaths give a different answer under each denominator. DGT reports road deaths of people 65 and over per million inhabitants of that age. On that denominator drivers 75 and over die 1.23 times as often as drivers aged 35 to 54, barely more, because most people over 75 do not drive at all. Per licence holder the ratio is 2.61, because holding a licence is not the same as driving. Per driver in a crash it is 3.93 and per kilometre 3.99: the last two agree because crashes per kilometre are about the same at the two ages. Only the kilometre compares like with like, distance with distance.

That is also why the per-resident rate says so little about driving. In 2024, 38% of residents aged 75 and over held a licence against 83% of those aged 45–54, and the gap between the sexes widens with age: 74% of men over 74 hold one and 13% of women. A per-resident rate for older women is describing passengers and pedestrians far more than drivers.

Whose kilometres

DGT's 2024 kilometre release estimates how far the whole circulating fleet is driven, by the age band of the registered owner: the same source, year and construction for every band. It is the owner's age, not the driver's, and the biases this brings pull both ways. Drivers aged 18 to 34 own far fewer cars per licence than anyone else, so part of their driving is registered to their parents in the 35–54 baseline; that inflates the baseline's kilometres and overstates the older bands against it. In the extreme case, with the young driving as far per licence as the baseline, the 75-and-over crash ratio per kilometre would be 0.89 rather than 1.02. The other way, a car registered to a person of 75 may be driven by a relative, and a car registered to a company has no age at all: company cars are 14% of car kilometres, mostly driven by people of working age, and they leave the denominator while their drivers stay in the numerator. Spreading them over the bands from 18 to 64 would raise the 75-and-over deaths-per-kilometre ratio from 3.99 to 4.76. Neither moves the main result: for drivers of 75 and over the deaths per crash, which need no kilometres, carry the excess.

Men and women: the same chain per licence holder

No Spanish source measures kilometres by sex, so the chain for men and women runs per licence holder. In 2022–2024, male car drivers were involved in injury crashes 1.41× (1.40–1.42) as often as female ones per licence holder; once involved they were killed 2.57× (2.24–2.94) as often; together, 3.63× (3.17–4.15) the deaths per licence holder. The first factor includes how much each sex drives; the second does not depend on driving at all, because it starts from drivers already in a crash.

How much of the crash gap is driving? The national travel survey MOVILIA (2006) counts trips by car or motorcycle, as driver or passenger, by sex and age. Under 65, men made 1.3 to 2.3 times as many such trips per head as women; car-driver involvement per head in 2022–2024 runs 1.5 to 2.1 times. Per trip, that leaves 0.9 to 1.2: about equal. The survey is old and counts passengers, who are more often women, so it understates the driving gap; the conclusion it supports is only that the involvement gap is of the size a travel gap could produce. The deaths gap is not: per trip, deaths still run 1.8 to 3.6 times.

The deaths per crash mean something different here than for age. Frailty does not explain the men's excess: in comparable crashes women are, if anything, more likely to be seriously injured (Bose, Segui-Gomez & Crandall 2011, US data). So the men's higher deaths per crash point to the crashes themselves and to behaviour within them: the roads, hours and speeds at which men crash, and belt use, which these tables cannot separate.

Conclusion

Age and sex raise the risk of dying at the wheel through different factors. For drivers 75 and over it is mostly what a crash does to them: 3.93× the deaths per crash of drivers aged 35 to 54, with crashes per kilometre at 1.02× that rate, though 1.43× the rate of drivers aged 65 to 74. For drivers 18 to 34 it is all how often they crash: 2.57× the crashes per kilometre, with no more deaths per crash. For men it is mostly deaths per crash, 2.57×, beside 1.41× the crashes per licence holder, about what their extra travel produces. The split says where the excess sits, not what would remove it: for older drivers it points at what a crash does, which speed and the vehicle govern, more than at how often they crash, and for young drivers the reverse.

Limits. Owner age is a proxy for driver age, and the two diverge most in the households where a car is shared. The kilometres are modelled from roadworthiness-inspection odometer readings and are valid for aggregates only; the intervals here come from the crash counts and treat them as known. The numerator counts drivers of cars on Spanish roads, including foreign-registered ones, while the denominator covers Spanish-registered cars only. The kilometre comparison is one year, 2024. Deaths per crash count the driver only. No source measures kilometres by sex, so the sex comparison uses licences and a 2006 travel survey that counts passengers; only the deaths per crash are free of that limit.
