Drivers, vehicles and factors

Driver age and sex

In Spain in 2024, car drivers aged 18–29 were involved in injury crashes 2.53 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (95% interval 2.25–2.82; sensitivity range 1.64–3.63), and drivers aged 65 and over slightly more often, 1.19 times (95% interval 1.03–1.36; sensitivity range 0.99–1.65). The kilometres by age are estimated from a Barcelona-area survey of working days, and the sensitivity range spans the other assumptions tested. In Barcelona city on working days, drivers aged 65 and over were involved at about the 45–64 rate. At 75 and over the national ratio depends on how the 65-and-over kilometres are split, from 1.13 to 3.09 times the 45–64 rate. The clearest difference at older ages is the outcome once a crash happens: 15.9 of every 1,000 car drivers aged 75 and over involved in an injury crash died within 30 days, against 4.6 at 45–64. Involvement counts every driver in a crash, whoever caused it, and the death rate only the driver's own death: no rate here shows who caused a crash or whether older drivers' crashes are more dangerous to others.

Crashes per kilometre: highest at 18–29

No national source counts kilometres by the age of the driver, so they are estimated, and the ratios per kilometre are estimates with ranges, not measurements; the method and its checks are set out in the exposure study. The EMEF, the working-day mobility survey of the province of Barcelona, records the car trips its respondents drove on one working day, with each trip's duration and, from 2021, its straight-line distance in bands, from which each trip's kilometres are estimated. Its 2022–2024 editions give each age group's car-driver kilometres per resident, by sex. Applied to the population of Spain and scaled to DGT's 2024 car kilometres from inspection odometer readings, less taxis and ride-hailing cars (whose drivers are outside both counts), they put 11.4% of car kilometres with drivers aged 18–29, 47.7% with 45–64 and 13.0% with 65 and over. The survey's working-day driving, counted over all 262 weekdays of 2024, comes to at most 53% of those kilometres. The age mix of the rest, driven on weekends and holidays, in the months the survey's fieldwork does not cover, and by people driving for a living, is assumed to be the working-day mix; the weekend and professional-driving tests below check that only in part. The drivers involved are DGT's count of private-car drivers in injury crashes in 2024.

Two further choices raise the 65-and-over ratio. The share of the survey's older respondents in work was about 4.3% in 2019–2021, the same as the census for Catalonia, but 6.0% to 8.3% in 2022–2024; setting it back to the census share moves the 65-and-over ratio from 1.19 to 1.23. And the survey does not record the work trips of people who drive for a living, most of them under 65: counting a quarter or a half of those trips as car trips raises the ratio to 1.26–1.33. The weekend age mix and Barcelona city's profile point the other way, so the evidence does not say in which direction the central figure errs.

Earlier versions of this page divided the same drivers, taxi and ride-hailing drivers included, by DGT's kilometres of cars registered to owners of each age, the only kilometres by age that DGT publishes. On the age groups used here, those owner kilometres put drivers aged 18–29 at 4.62 times the 45–64 rate and drivers aged 65 and over at 1.00, against 2.53 and 1.19 here; the figure the page used to give, 6.75, compared drivers aged 18–24 with 35–54. A car's owner is often not its driver: young people drive cars registered to their parents, and older owners' cars are partly driven by others, so the owner kilometres credited too little driving to the young and too much to the old.

Deaths once a crash has happened: highest at 75 and over

This rate divides the car drivers killed within 30 days by all car drivers involved in injury crashes, injured or not, of the same age and year, so no measure of driving enters it. It counts only the driver's own death: DGT's tables do not say how often other people in the same crashes died. In 2024 it was 4.1 per 1,000 at 18–29, 3.9 at 30–44, 4.6 at 45–64, 8.5 at 65–74 and 15.9 at 75 and over, about 3.4 times the 45–64 rate.

Combined with involvement per kilometre, this means that drivers aged 65 and over were killed 2.92 times as often per kilometre driven as drivers aged 45–64 (95% interval 2.21–3.82; sensitivity range 2.43–4.04), although they were involved in crashes only 1.19 times as often: their excess deaths come mainly from the outcome once involved, not from being involved in more crashes.

Barcelona on working days: 65 and over at about the 45–64 rate

Inside Barcelona on working days, involvement per kilometre can be compared without carrying the survey to Spain or assuming anything about weekends. The Guàrdia Urbana's 2025 records give the age of 93.9% of the car drivers involved in injury crashes in the city on working days, uninjured drivers included; the EMEF gives the kilometres the survey area's residents drove inside the city on a working day in 2022–2024, counted over the 248 working days of 2025 (weekdays less the Catalan and Barcelona public holidays). The part of a trip into or out of the city that lies inside it cannot be measured, so the table gives three versions of the kilometres, and only the ratios between ages are read.

75 and over: a range, not a single figure

No source for Spain or the province of Barcelona measures driving at 75 and over separately from 65–74, so the figures in this section rest on an assumed split of the 65-and-over kilometres and are labelled model-dependent.

The EMEF's public files group everyone aged 65 and over, although the survey sampled 65–74 and 75 and over separately until 2016. The Madrid household travel survey of 2018 records exact ages: there, residents aged 75 and over drove 0.34 times the distance per resident of those aged 65–74 if men and 0.28 times if women. Men and women: no clear difference in crashes per kilometre, more deaths among men

The survey's kilometres can be split by sex and carried to Spain in the same way as by age. On that estimate men drove 66.4% of car kilometres in 2024. Per kilometre, male private-car drivers aged 18 and over were involved in injury crashes 0.91 times as often as female drivers (95% interval 0.85–0.98), but across the regional profiles that ratio runs from 0.60 to 1.23, so neither sex is shown to be involved more often per kilometre. Men were killed 2.65 times as often (95% interval 2.10–3.38; sensitivity range 1.75–3.55). Per licence holder, pooling 2022–2024, men were involved 1.39 times as often as women (95% interval 1.38–1.40) and, once involved, died 2.62 times as often (2.28–3.00); combined, they died at the wheel 3.63 times as often (3.17–4.15). Under the central profile, men's higher involvement per licence holder comes from their driving further; their higher death rate holds per kilometre under every profile and, in every age band, once a crash has happened.

Across all motor-vehicle drivers, which adds motorcyclists, moped riders, the drivers of vans, trucks and buses and other motor vehicles, the gaps are wider: men died 6.42 times as often as women per licence holder and 3.23 times as often once involved. The licence holders are holders of any class of licence. The census gives car-licence holders by sex and age for 2023–2024 only; counting only them raises the ratio of men's to women's deaths per licence holder in those years from 3.49 to 3.57.
